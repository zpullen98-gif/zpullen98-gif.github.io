The French herbsIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Compare the tender fines herbes quartet with the smaller, tougher leaves of thyme and rosemary.

Parsley, chervil, chives and tarragon form the classic fines herbes quartet. Thyme and rosemary are shown for contrast, not as part of that blend. This is a kitchen comparison of known culinary herbs, not a wild-plant identification guide; confirm the house’s current herb mix at lineup.

  1. 1
    Flat-leaf parsley

    Look for broad, flat, toothed leaflets. Its fresh green flavour is the familiar starting point for the tender-herb comparison.

  2. 2
    Chervil

    The softer, finely divided leaves look lacy beside parsley. Its delicate anise note is best appreciated in fresh finishing herbs.

  3. 3
    Chives

    The narrow leaves are round and hollow, with a mild onion flavour. These are common chives, rather than the flat leaves of garlic chives.

  4. 4
    French tarragon

    Long, narrow, smooth leaves accompany a pronounced anise-like aroma. Tarragon is the distinctive herb in the béarnaise family of sauces.

  5. 5
    Thyme

    Tiny leaves cluster on short woody sprigs. Compare this compact, sturdier structure with the four tender herbs above.

  6. 6
    Rosemary

    Firm, needle-like leaves grow along woody stems. Its resinous aroma is stronger than the soft green character of parsley.

Read two separate things: the browning on the surface and the band of cooked meat beneath it.

The wet pan and grey band illustrate different observations, not a single proven cause. Surface moisture, crowding, heat, time and steak thickness all matter. The pink interior is an illustration, not a temperature reading or a house doneness specification.

Resting and slicing across the grainIllustrated study · 2 numbered views

Find the direction of the muscle fibres, then compare a cross-cut slice with a strip cut along them.

Resting and slicing direction are separate decisions. Resting can reduce liquid lost when meat is cut, but a pool on the board alone cannot establish how long it rested. The hanger steak is a general technique example, not Brennan’s plating or doneness standard.

  1. 1
    Across the grain

    Locate the long, parallel fibres before slicing across them. Shortening those fibres makes a coarse-grained cut easier to chew. The small amount of board liquid is illustrative, not a pass-or-fail test.

  2. 2
    Along the grain

    These strips keep long fibres running along their length. The larger pool is a prompt to review resting and cooking, rather than proof that the steak was sliced too soon.

The classical knife cutsIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Compare cubes, square-sided sticks and leaf ribbons. Even pieces within a cut are the useful habit to practise.

Dimensions below follow the Table’s existing lesson; conventions and stick lengths vary between schools and kitchens. The illustration compares shapes and relative sizes, not actual millimetres on your screen. Use a ruler for practice and follow the recipe’s required cut.

  1. 1
    Brunoise

    Small, even cubes: about 3 × 3 × 3 mm in this lesson. Cut julienne crosswise to make matching dice.

  2. 2
    Small dice

    About 6 × 6 × 6 mm. Each edge is roughly twice the brunoise edge; compare the pieces within the pile for consistency.

  3. 3
    Medium dice

    About 12 × 12 × 12 mm. These larger cubes still need straight, even faces and closely matching dimensions.

  4. 4
    Julienne

    Slender square-sided sticks, about 3 × 3 mm and 5 cm long here. Aim for the same width and thickness throughout.

  5. 5
    Batonnet

    Thicker square-sided sticks, about 6 × 6 mm and 6 cm long here. Cutting them crosswise at the same interval produces small dice.

  6. 6
    Chiffonade

    Stack and roll tender leaves, then slice across the roll into ribbons. Keep the cut clean rather than crushing the leaves.

Roux: from pale to darkIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Compare an evenly cooked roux at five colour stages with a scorched batch.

Stop at the stage the recipe calls for. Darker roux develops a deeper toasted flavour and has less thickening power. The numbered stages are a visual comparison, not a cooking timer.

  1. 1
    White

    Pale and evenly mixed, with no dry pockets. A pale roux is used for sauces such as béchamel.

  2. 2
    Blond

    A light golden stage, used for sauces such as velouté.

  3. 3
    Peanut-butter brown

    An intermediate brown: keep stirring and judge against the recipe’s intended stage.

  4. 4
    Milk-chocolate brown

    An even, deeper brown, the target named by the Table’s chicken and andouille gumbo recipe.

  5. 5
    Brick

    A deep red-brown stage. Even colour matters; dark roux should not contain scorched black flecks.

  6. 6
    Scorched

    Black flecks signal a burnt batch. Start again; stirring the flecks back in does not remove their bitterness.

Hollandaise: build, recognise, recoverIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Follow the yolk foam into an emulsion, then distinguish a smooth sauce from curdling and separation.

Use the linked recipe for the method. The last panel illustrates the fresh-base rescue for a separated sauce; it does not show cooked egg curds turning back into uncooked yolk.

  1. 1
    Whisk the sabayon

    The yolk mixture becomes pale and airy, leaving a ribbon briefly on the surface. The bowl sits clear of the water beneath it.

  2. 2
    Add butter gradually

    Work warm clarified butter into the yolks in a fine stream. Each addition should disappear into the sauce.

  3. 3
    A stable sauce

    Look for a smooth, glossy body that coats the whisk, without a separate oily slick.

  4. 4
    Too hot

    Graininess and cooked yolk lumps are different from a smooth emulsion.

  5. 5
    Separated

    Free butter over thin liquid signals a broken emulsion. Temperature and the pace of adding fat both matter.

  6. 6
    A fresh base

    The recipe’s rescue starts with a fresh yolk in a clean bowl, then works the separated sauce into it gradually.
