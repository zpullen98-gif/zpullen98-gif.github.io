Vepřo Knedlo Zelo

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

tangy-ferment · rich · aromatic · warm-spiced Leads with ferment tang with real depth behind it, richness that coats and comforts, fading in warm spice.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 kg pork shoulder with the skin on
  • 15 g caraway seed
  • 4 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 20 g coarse salt
  • 1 onion, thickly sliced
  • 250 ml water
  • 800 g sauerkraut, drained, brine kept
  • 1 onion, finely sliced, for the cabbage
  • 40 g lard
  • 15 g caster sugar
  • 5 g caraway seed, for the cabbage
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 1 floury potato, grated raw
  • 3 g black pepper

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Score the skin in a 1 cm lattice, cutting through the fat but stopping short of the meat, and rub the salt, garlic and caraway into the flesh and into every cut.
  2. Set the joint on the sliced onion with the water in the tin and roast at 160C (325F), basting with the pan liquid, until a skewer meets no resistance in the thickest part, about three hours.
  3. Meanwhile soften the finely sliced onion in the lard until deep gold, then add the drained sauerkraut, sugar, caraway, bay and a splash of the reserved brine, cover and stew until the shreds turn translucent and slack, about forty minutes.
  4. Stir the grated potato through the cabbage for the last ten minutes; it should cling to the shreds, not sit in a pool.
  5. Raise the oven to 220C (425F) for the final twenty minutes, uncovered and unbasted, until the skin blisters and cracks under the back of a spoon.
  6. Rest the pork fifteen minutes, carve thick, and spoon the pan juices over the dumpling slices.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Scoring 28 recipes

  • Lift one cut open with a fingertip: it parts through the skin and stops in the fat beneath, showing a pale wall at its base. Any cut showing red or fibrous flesh has gone too deep; any that has not reached the fat has left the skin tethered, and it will tighten under heat.
  • The lines run parallel at even spacing, roughly a finger's width apart, each carried the full length of the face and stopped the same margin short of the edge. Sight down them: lines that converge, wander or crowd at one end cook at different rates and read as careless on the plate.
  • Each line is one clean draw of the blade held at a slant, not a sawn or doubled track, and it leaves a bevelled lip standing slightly proud rather than a vertical slot. The surface should be dry and cold, near 4C (39F), when it is cut; worked warm and slack it drags, and the blade tears where it should part.
  • After cooking the cuts have opened and set, each a clear gap with firm browned walls. Lines closed back to faint scratches were cut too shallow or too straight down, and the surface has shrunk over them instead of through them.
  • The piece lies flat and true when it is set down, with no cupping through the middle and no lifted, curled edge. A bowed piece means the tightening surface was never released, so it pulled the whole thing up and left the underside pale where it stood off the heat.

Where it goes wrong The blade was pushed rather than drawn, through a surface left warm and slack, so it went past the fat and into the flesh. The cuts bleed as they cook and the juices flood the surface, so it steams instead of crisping: the lines gape into wet ragged trenches, the flesh between them dries into hard ridges, and the skin stays leathery and pale where it should have blistered and set.

Roasting in a hot oven 56 recipes

  • The oven was fully up to heat before anything went in, given time beyond the indicator light rather than judged by it.
  • Every piece has air around it. Nothing touches its neighbour and nothing is packed to the edge of the tray, because crowded food steams itself in its own moisture and never browns.
  • Colour is even across the whole tray, the tray having been turned at least once: no pale row at the back and no dark row at the front.
  • It came out BEFORE the target temperature, not at it. Carryover keeps driving the inside up by several degrees after it leaves the oven, and that rise is part of the cooking rather than an error in it.
  • The temperature was read at the coldest part rather than the easiest one to reach, and read again after the rest.

Where it goes wrong The tray was crowded, or the oven was not truly up to heat. The food releases steam faster than a cool box can carry it away, so it sits pale and slack, and the extra minutes spent chasing colour take the inside well past where it should have stopped.

From the pass

Two failures share this plate. Skin only crackles when it is dry, so the basting that keeps the meat moist has to stop for the last twenty minutes under high heat. And sauerkraut left in all its own brine stays squeaky and harshly sour; drain most of it off, then cook the shreds slack in onion and fat with a little sugar so the acid reads as lift rather than sting.
