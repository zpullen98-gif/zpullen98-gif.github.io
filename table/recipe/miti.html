Pacific Islands · Sauce

Miti

  • Easy
  • 10 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

rich · bright · fiery · silky Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 250 ml first pressing coconut cream, cold
  • 60 ml fresh lime juice
  • 1 small red onion, 80 g, cut into 3 mm dice
  • 3 bird's eye chillies, finely chopped with their seeds
  • 5 g fine sea salt
  • 1 spring onion, thinly sliced

Allergen screen

None found among the 14 screened.

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Salt the diced onion in the serving bowl and leave it to weep while you juice the limes; the salt takes the raw bite out of it.
  2. Whisk the lime juice into the cold coconut cream a spoonful at a time until the sauce is smooth and the colour of milk, with no thin streaks.
  3. Stir in the chilli and spring onion, then taste: it should read salty first, sour second, and the heat should arrive last.
  4. Pour into a shallow bowl and serve within the hour, for dipping fish, taro and cassava.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Building an emulsion 39 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Salting to draw the water out 41 recipes

  • Salted evenly over every surface rather than tipped on in one place and tossed once.
  • Laid out so the water has somewhere to go: a colander, a rack, or a sieve over a bowl, never a flat dish where it sits back in what it gave up.
  • Given real time, and there is visible liquid to show for it: a pool underneath, a drip, or a wrung handful.
  • That liquid is removed. It is squeezed, blotted or poured away, and the food is dry to the touch before it goes anywhere near heat.
  • The dish is seasoned knowing salt has already gone on, tasted before any more is added.

Where it goes wrong It went into the dish still wet, and was salted again out of habit. The water it was carrying dilutes and steams whatever it meets, so the result is watery and over-seasoned at once, and those are two faults that cannot be corrected in the same direction.

From the pass

Lime is what makes miti taste of the sea and also what kills it. Acid strips the charge holding the coconut emulsion together, so the cream thins and then curdles to oil and flakes. Adding the juice slowly into cold cream, whisking, keeps it whole long enough to reach the table. Capsaicin is fat soluble, so the heat climbs the longer the bowl stands. Never warm it.
