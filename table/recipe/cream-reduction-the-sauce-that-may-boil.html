Cream Reduction, the Sauce That May Boil

  • Easy
  • 15 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · bright · aromatic · herb-fresh Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 300 ml double cream at 48% fat, or heavy cream at not less than 36%
  • 60 ml dry white wine
  • 30 g shallot, finely minced
  • 10 g unsalted butter
  • 15 g Dijon mustard
  • 6 g tarragon leaves, chopped
  • 5 ml lemon juice
  • 3 g fine salt

Method

  1. Sweat the shallot in the butter without letting it colour, about three minutes, until it is translucent and smells sweet rather than raw.
  2. Add the wine and reduce until the pan is almost dry and the shallot glistens; harshness left in the wine will concentrate here, not cook away.
  3. Pour in the cream and boil it openly and hard, which no butter emulsion would survive; the agitation is what reduces it evenly.
  4. Take it to about 200 ml, until the sauce coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through leaves a channel that holds its edges.
  5. Off the heat, and only then, whisk in the mustard, lemon and tarragon, tasting for salt as the acid sharpens the reduction.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Building an emulsion 40 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Deglazing & pan sauces 49 recipes

  • The pan still holds its fond. Nothing has been rinsed, wiped, or soaked, and the browned layer is intact on the floor.
  • Excess fat was poured off first and the browned layer left behind; the fat is not the flavour and a sauce carrying all of it will not come together.
  • The liquid went into a still-hot pan and the floor came clean: scraped while it bubbles, the base goes from stuck brown to bare metal and the sauce darkens as it takes it up.
  • The fond was brown and not black. A burnt floor dissolves into bitterness that cannot be seasoned out, and is better washed away than deglazed.
  • Reduced first and seasoned afterwards, tasted at the consistency it will be served at rather than at the start.

Where it goes wrong The pan was left to stand and go cold before anybody came back to it. Cold fond will not dissolve, so the scraping lifts nothing, and the sauce arrives thin and pale with the flavour it was supposed to carry still welded to the metal.

From the pass

Cream boils without breaking because its fat is already dispersed as membrane coated globules in a casein rich water phase, with no free fat waiting to be released. That protection needs fat: below about 30% the casein flocculates under heat and acid. Reduction thickens by evaporation alone, so judge nappe, not minutes. Add lemon or mustard off the heat; acid near pH 4.6 drops the casein out as grains.
