Oven Hāngī Vegetables

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

smoky · rich · charred · briny Leads with wood-smoke running underneath, richness that coats and comforts, ending saline and clean.

Ingredients

  • 800 g kūmara, in 4 cm chunks
  • 800 g floury potatoes, in 4 cm chunks
  • 600 g crown pumpkin, in 6 cm wedges
  • half a green cabbage, plus its outer leaves
  • 2 onions, halved
  • 40 g butter
  • 300 ml water
  • 8 g salt
  • 1 large square of muslin, wetted

The skills inside

Charring over open flameSteaming: basket, leaf and lidThe wobble & the skewer: testing a bake

Allergen screen

Found: dairy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Blacken the outer cabbage leaves directly over a gas flame or on a dry pan until they blister and smell of smoke; this is the only honest smoke a kitchen can give you.
  2. Line a deep roasting tin with the charred leaves, pack in the kūmara, potato, pumpkin, onion and cabbage quarters, salt through the layers and dot with the butter.
  3. Pour the water down the side of the tin, lay the wet muslin over the vegetables and clamp on a lid or doubled foil so nothing can vent.
  4. Bake at 160C (320F) until a skewer meets no resistance in the kūmara and the cabbage beneath has gone translucent and sweet, about 90 minutes. 90 min
  5. Leave it sealed off the heat for ten minutes, then lift the vegetables out on their leaves and spoon the tin juices over.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Charring over open flame 31 recipes

  • The surface met the fiercest heat available (flame, a broiler, or bare hot metal) with nothing between them, and it shows as true blistering and blackening rather than gentle, even browning.
  • The char matches the surface’s fate: where it will be eaten, black sits in patches over deep colour with every face having had its turn, and where the skin is to be peeled away, it may go to an even black, because the char there works on the flesh below and then leaves with the skin.
  • It smells of smoke and toasted surface with no acrid edge that stings the nose: acrid means it went past, and acrid does not cook out.
  • Just under the char it is cooked (softened, steaming, collapsed, or puffed, whichever the dish wants) because the flame did the surface and the trapped heat did the rest.
  • A taste carries the smoke without mouthfuls of ash: the black that flakes away is a seasoning, not a layer.

Where it goes wrong It was left on one face too long or held too close, so one side carbonised to ash while the rest stayed raw, and the smell turned acrid, which follows the dish through every later step and cannot be seasoned away.

The wobble & the skewer: testing a bake 119 recipes

  • Tested at the true centre and the thickest point, not at the convenient edge: the edge is done long before the answer is.
  • The skewer comes out as the recipe describes, clean and dry or carrying the stated moist crumb, and it does the same on a second test in a different spot.
  • The wobble is read in motion: nudged, the centre moves as one set mass with a faint tremble, rather than a liquid ripple travelling across the surface.
  • The other signs agree with the test: risen and just shrinking from the sides, the top set and dry to a light touch with no wet gloss on it.
  • Pressed lightly at the middle it springs back and leaves no lasting dent.

Where it goes wrong It was judged on the clock and on how the edges looked, so a centre still liquid went to the rack and sank as it cooled, a dense wet seam under a perfectly browned top.

From the pass

Call this what it is: a steam bake, not a hāngī. No oven makes smoke and hot stone, and charred cabbage leaves are the nearest honest substitute. What an oven can copy is the long wet ramp, which keeps the kūmara below the point where its surface dries and lets its amylase go on turning starch into sugar. Lid off, or above 180C (355F), and you have roast vegetables: a good dish, a different one.
