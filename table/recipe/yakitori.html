Yakitori

  • Intermediate
  • 60 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost

charred · sweet-edged · smoky · herb-fresh Leads with char used as punctuation, a sweet edge rounding the corners, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 600g chicken thighs, cut in even 3cm pieces; the full izakaya spread adds skin (kawa), meatballs (tsukune), and liver for the brave
  • Negi: thick scallion or baby leek segments, skewered between the meat
  • The tare: 100ml soy + 100ml mirin + 50ml sake + 2 tbsp sugar + chicken trimmings, simmered 20 min to a glossy lacquer, kept and re-fed forever
  • Or the purist's other road: shio, just good salt
  • Bamboo skewers soaked 30 min; a narrow charcoal bed (binchotan is the dream) so the skewer ends hang off the heat

Method

  1. Thread meat and negi snugly: gaps dry out, crowding steams; the skewer should look like a tidy train.
  2. Grill over hot coals, turning every 45–60 seconds: yakitori is constant motion, small adjustments, no walking away.
  3. Tare skewers: dip into the sauce and RETURN to the grill; repeat twice more: layered dips lacquer; a single late dunk merely wets.
  4. Finish with a final gloss of tare or a pinch of salt; sansho pepper on the fatty ones. Serve immediately, two skewers at a time, with something cold.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

  • The fire was ready before the food was: coals evenly covered in grey ash with no flames licking through and no raw smoke coming off, and a hot zone and a cooler one both available.
  • The grate was hot and clean when the food landed, and the food released cleanly from it when the time came to turn rather than tearing away.
  • Marks are browned, not burnt: deep colour where the metal touched, with no carbon flaking off and no soot transferred onto the food.
  • The interior reaches its target with a thin gradient, not charred outside and cold within, and not driven through by a flare-up.
  • What comes off the fire smells of fire and food only: no raw wood smoke, no fuel, nothing acrid at the back of the nose.

Where it goes wrong It went on over live flames instead of settled coals, so the outside charred bitter in the flare-ups while the middle stayed cold, and everything on the plate tastes of soot.

From the pass

Tokyo's yakitori masters guard tare pots that have been topped up, never emptied, for decades; every dipped skewer feeds chicken essence back into the mother sauce. The dip-grill-dip rhythm caramelizes in coats, the same logic as the huli huli baste chapters away. Shio versus tare is the eternal counter question; order both and betray no allegiance.
