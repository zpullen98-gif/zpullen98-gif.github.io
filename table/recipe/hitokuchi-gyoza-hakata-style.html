Hitokuchi Gyoza, Hakata Style

  • Intermediate
  • 55 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

aromatic · nutty-toasted · herb-fresh · umami-deep Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, toasted, nutty warmth throughout, finishing long and savory.

Ingredients

  • 200g plain flour
  • 110g water at 90C (194F)
  • 4g salt, for the dough
  • 250g minced pork, 25% fat
  • 200g cabbage, finely chopped
  • 4g salt, for the cabbage
  • 50g garlic chives, finely sliced
  • 10g ginger, grated
  • 1 clove garlic, grated
  • 15g soy sauce
  • 10g toasted sesame oil
  • 5g sugar
  • 20g neutral oil, to fry
  • 80g water, to steam
  • Ponzu and yuzu kosho, to serve

Equipment

Sheet pan · Rolling pin · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Pour the near-boiling water into the salted flour, stir with chopsticks until it clumps, then knead 6 minutes once it is cool enough to handle. Rest 45 minutes: the dough should feel slack and pliable, not springy. 6 min
  2. Salt the cabbage, wait 15 minutes, wring it out hard, and mix it into the pork with the chives, ginger, garlic, soy, sesame oil and sugar until sticky. Chill. 15 min
  3. Roll the dough into 8g balls and press each to a 6cm round. Fill with 8g, pleat three or four folds along one side, and stand them on a floured tray.
  4. Set a heavy pan over medium-high heat with the neutral oil, pack the gyoza in tight rows, and fry undisturbed about 2 minutes, until the bases are set and hazelnut brown. 2 min
  5. Add the 80g water, cover at once, and steam 4 minutes. Uncover, let every drop of water go, then fry 1 minute more until the bases crackle under the spatula. Invert onto the plate. 4 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pleating dumplings 8 recipes

  • The seam is closed along its whole length with no gap at either end of the pleat run: hold one up to the light and no filling shows through the join. A dumpling that opens in the water was sealed against a wet or floured rim.
  • The rim is clean and dry where it meets itself. Filling smeared onto the sealing edge is the single most common cause of a burst, and it cannot be wiped off once the dough has taken it up.
  • The pleats are even in size and count across the batch, so every piece holds the same weight of filling and cooks in the same time. A tray that ranges from eight folds to eighteen will not finish together.
  • The wrapper is thicker at the centre than at the rim, so the base carries the filling without tearing while the pleated edge stays fine enough to eat. A disc rolled evenly flat splits at the bottom.
  • It stands up on its own base without slumping, and the pleats still read as pleats after cooking rather than having relaxed into a smooth seam.

Where it goes wrong The rim was wet, floured or smeared with filling, so the seal never took: the dumpling opens in the pot, the filling leaves, and what surfaces is a slack empty wrapper in cloudy water. Uneven pleat counts across a tray compound it, because the batch stops finishing at the same moment.

Salting to draw the water out 41 recipes

  • Salted evenly over every surface rather than tipped on in one place and tossed once.
  • Laid out so the water has somewhere to go: a colander, a rack, or a sieve over a bowl, never a flat dish where it sits back in what it gave up.
  • Given real time, and there is visible liquid to show for it: a pool underneath, a drip, or a wrung handful.
  • That liquid is removed. It is squeezed, blotted or poured away, and the food is dry to the touch before it goes anywhere near heat.
  • The dish is seasoned knowing salt has already gone on, tasted before any more is added.

Where it goes wrong It went into the dish still wet, and was salted again out of habit. The water it was carrying dilutes and steams whatever it meets, so the result is watery and over-seasoned at once, and those are two faults that cannot be corrected in the same direction.

From the pass

The Hakata version is one bite each, which is the point: a small skin crisps all over instead of turning leathery at the edges. The sequence is fry, steam, fry. That first fry sets and browns the base so the water that follows cannot dissolve it; add water to an unset base and the loose starch glues the row to the pan. The hot water dough is deliberate too, gelatinised starch and slack gluten, so it rolls thin and stays tender under the lid.
