Hane-tsuki Gyoza

  • Advanced
  • 1 h 30 m
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost

aromatic · gently hot · bright · herb-fresh Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, a warm current of chile, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 30 round gyoza wrappers (store-bought is standard even in Japan; your energy belongs to the filling and the fry)
  • Filling: 300g ground pork, 150g cabbage salted-squeezed dry, 3 garlic cloves, thumb of ginger, 2 scallions, 1 tbsp soy + 1 tsp sesame oil + 1 tsp sake
  • The crisp lace (hane): 1 tsp flour whisked into 100ml water
  • Dip: soy + rice vinegar 1:1, chili oil (rayu) to taste
  • A lidded nonstick skillet: the steam-fry demands both

Method

  1. Salt the chopped cabbage 10 min; wring it like laundry: water is the enemy of a juicy-not-soggy filling; knead with pork and aromatics until tacky. 10 min
  2. A teaspoon of filling per wrapper; wet the rim; pleat 5–7 folds on ONE side, pressing to the flat side: the crescent stands on its own seam.
  3. Arrange standing in the oiled hot pan; fry 2 min to brown bottoms; pour in the flour-water; LID ON, steam 4–5 min. 2 min
  4. Lid off; let the water vanish and the flour set into a golden lace web connecting the dumplings. Flip the whole raft onto a plate, lace-side up. Dip; repeat; regret the batch size, always.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Salting to draw the water out 41 recipes

  • Salted evenly over every surface rather than tipped on in one place and tossed once.
  • Laid out so the water has somewhere to go: a colander, a rack, or a sieve over a bowl, never a flat dish where it sits back in what it gave up.
  • Given real time, and there is visible liquid to show for it: a pool underneath, a drip, or a wrung handful.
  • That liquid is removed. It is squeezed, blotted or poured away, and the food is dry to the touch before it goes anywhere near heat.
  • The dish is seasoned knowing salt has already gone on, tasted before any more is added.

Where it goes wrong It went into the dish still wet, and was salted again out of habit. The water it was carrying dilutes and steams whatever it meets, so the result is watery and over-seasoned at once, and those are two faults that cannot be corrected in the same direction.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 130 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

Japan took the Chinese jiaozi, moved the garlic to the front row, and perfected the yaki (steam-fry) method: brown, steam, crisp, in one pan, one sequence. The hane (wing), that shared lattice of crisped flour-water, is the modern flourish that turns a plate of dumplings into a single shatterable raft. Pleats on one side only is the tell of practiced hands, and thirty is famously never enough.
