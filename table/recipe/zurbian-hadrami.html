Zurbian Hadrami

  • Advanced
  • 45 min
  • $$$$ roughly 4 of 4 for cost

warm-spiced · bright · aromatic · floral Leads with warm spice humming through the middle, acid holding everything up to the light, leaving a faint perfume.

Ingredients

  • 1 kg lamb shoulder on the bone, in 5 cm pieces
  • 300 g full fat yogurt
  • 4 garlic cloves and 20 g ginger, pounded to a paste
  • 1 tbsp hawaij lil shorba
  • 2 tsp baharat
  • 1 tsp ground cinnamon
  • 3 onions, finely sliced
  • 100 g samn (clarified butter)
  • 400 g waxy potatoes, quartered
  • 500 g basmati rice, soaked 30 minutes
  • large pinch saffron, steeped in 3 tbsp warm milk
  • 2 tsp salt, plus salt for the rice water
  • 20 g coriander leaf, chopped

Equipment

Mortar & pestle

Method

  1. Beat the yogurt with the garlic and ginger paste, hawaij, baharat, cinnamon and salt, turn the lamb through it until every face is coated, and leave it to marinate for at least 4 hours. 240 min
  2. Fry the onions in the samn until mahogany and crisp at the edges, lift out half for the top, then tip in the lamb with all its marinade.
  3. Cook covered on a low flame until the yogurt has broken and reduced to a thick oily masala clinging to the meat and the lamb gives at the bone; fry the potatoes in the same fat until a crust sets on the cut faces.
  4. Boil the drained rice in heavily salted water for about 5 minutes, until the grain still snaps at the core, and drain it. 5 min
  5. Layer the meat and potatoes, then the rice, then the reserved onions, saffron milk and coriander, seal with a tight lid and cook on the lowest flame or at 160C (320F) until the top grains are dry and separate and the steam smells of saffron, not raw rice.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 23 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Marinating: acid, salt and time 70 recipes

  • Contact is total: every surface is wetted, and the pieces are not welded into a clump with dry faces where they pressed together.
  • The stated time was kept and kept cold, read against a clock, because this is the one technique where longer is a different result rather than a safer one.
  • It worked at the surface and no further: cut a piece and the marinade’s colour stops a few millimetres in. A protein gone opaque and chalky to the centre before any heat reached it has been cooked by the acid.
  • The texture is still springy and whole, not mushy, mealy, or fraying at the surface when it is lifted.
  • Where the next step browns it, the surface was patted dry first: a wet surface cannot brown, and the marinade’s sugars will scorch before the food colours.

Where it goes wrong It was left well beyond the stated time on the assumption that more is better, so acid and salt worked through the thin edges: those cook to cotton and read grainy while the thick centre tastes of nothing at all.

From the pass

Yogurt is both the marinade and the braising liquid. Its acid swells the surface proteins so they hold water, and its fat and solids reduce into the masala that coats the meat. It is meant to split here, unlike a Levantine laban sauce, but it must split slowly: on high heat the whey floods out at once, the lamb boils grey in it and the masala never comes together. Low flame, lid on.
