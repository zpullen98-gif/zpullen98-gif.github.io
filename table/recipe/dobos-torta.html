Hungarian · Dessert

Dobos torta

  • Advanced
  • 1 h 30 m
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

rich · sweet-edged · bright · earthy Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a sweet edge rounding the corners, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 6 eggs, separated, at room temperature
  • 180 g caster sugar, for the sponge
  • 150 g plain flour, sifted
  • 30 g unsalted butter, melted and cooled
  • 6 egg yolks, for the buttercream
  • 180 g caster sugar, for the buttercream
  • 60 ml water
  • 300 g unsalted butter, soft
  • 150 g dark chocolate at 60 to 70 percent, melted and cooled
  • 150 g caster sugar, for the caramel
  • 10 ml lemon juice
  • 100 g walnuts, finely ground
  • sunflower oil, for the knife and paper
  • a pinch of fine salt

Equipment

Sheet pan

Found: gluten · dairy · egg · nuts

Method

  1. Whip the whites to a firm meringue with half the sponge sugar, beat the yolks with the rest until pale and ribboning, then fold the two together with the sifted flour and melted butter without knocking the air out.
  2. Spread the batter as six thin 22 cm discs on lined trays and bake at 200C (390F) until the edges are dry and just tan, then peel each one off hot and cool it flat under a board.
  3. Cook the buttercream sugar and water to 118C (245F), pour it in a thread onto the beating yolks and keep beating until the bowl is no warmer than your hand before working in the soft butter, the chocolate and the salt.
  4. Set the flattest layer on oiled paper, cook the caramel sugar with the lemon juice to a clear mid amber, and pour it over, tilting to a thin even sheet.
  5. The moment the caramel stops running, mark it into twelve wedges with an oiled knife and cut clean through while it still yields.
  6. Stack and fill the other five layers with buttercream, mask the sides and press on the ground walnuts, then prop each caramel wedge on a ridge of buttercream.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Icing & frosting 13 recipes

  • The temperatures were chosen, not defaulted: the bake fully cool where the finish is meant to sit on top of it, or both deliberately warm where the method wants the two to fuse, and the proof either way is a finish that stayed where it was put.
  • Smooth on the tongue: no grit of undissolved sugar and no lumps of anything that was never fully worked in.
  • The consistency matches the job: it spreads or pours the way the method intends, then holds the edge, swirl, or drip it was given.
  • The surface underneath is intact: covered rather than scrubbed, with no crumbs dragged through the finish and no torn patches showing.
  • By serving it has settled into the finish the method wants (matte, glossy, or soft) and a cut comes away clean rather than pulling the top off with it.

Where it goes wrong A finish meant to sit on top went on while the bake was still warm. The layer that touched first melted, slid, and soaked in; what is left sets greasy and streaked, and no amount of chilling afterwards puts that finish back.

Sugar stages & caramel 19 recipes

  • Nothing is stirred once it is boiling, and any crystals clinging to the side of the pan are brushed down rather than left to fall in.
  • Judged by temperature or by a tested stage rather than by time: consecutive stages are a few degrees apart and look identical in the pan.
  • Colour is read off the heat and in good light, remembering that sugar keeps darkening after the flame is out.
  • Stopped decisively at the point wanted: off the heat and into the next liquid or onto a cold surface, rather than left to coast in a hot pan.
  • The result is clear and smooth rather than grainy, cloudy, or seized into a solid mass.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred after coming to the boil, or a stray crystal from the side of the pan fell back in. One crystal seeds the whole pan and the syrup turns grainy within seconds, and once sugar has crystallised the only cure is more water and starting the whole thing again.

From the pass

The caramel lid is the whole difficulty. Sugar much past 180C (355F) turns acrid, and once it cools below roughly 100C (210F) it is glass: a knife then shatters it into shards. So it is scored and cut in the few seconds while it is still plastic, on oiled paper with an oiled blade. The lemon juice inverts some of the sugar and stops the sheet crystallising to a dull opaque slab.
