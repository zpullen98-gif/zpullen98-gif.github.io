Baked Mac & Cheese

  • Intermediate
  • 60 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · gently hot · warm-spiced Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a warm current of chile, fading in warm spice.

Ingredients

  • 400g elbow macaroni, cooked 2 min shy
  • 60g butter + 60g flour
  • 900ml milk, warmed
  • 400g sharp cheddar + 150g gruyère or monterey jack, grated (never pre-shredded)
  • Mustard powder, cayenne, nutmeg
  • Topping: buttered panko or more cheese

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Roux 2 min; whisk in milk to a sauce that coats a spoon. 2 min
  2. Cheese in off heat, one handful at a time; season boldly.
  3. Fold in pasta; into a buttered dish; top.
  4. Bake 200°C, 20–25 min, to bronze and bubble. Rest 10. 25 min

Stop at the stage the recipe calls for. Darker roux develops a deeper toasted flavour and has less thickening power. The numbered stages are a visual comparison, not a cooking timer.

    Black flecks signal a burnt batch. Start again; stirring the flecks back in does not remove their bitterness.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Making a roux 32 recipes

  • Taken to the stage the dish named and no further, judged against that colour (pale, blond, or brown to brick), and it got there slowly enough to have coloured evenly.
  • It is smooth and mobile: a paste with no dry pockets of flour and no lumps, loosening as it warms rather than sitting stiff in the pan.
  • The raw-flour smell has gone and been replaced by the toasted, biscuity smell of its stage, with nothing acrid behind it.
  • No black specks anywhere. One scorched fleck condemns the batch; it cannot be stirred back in and it will be tasted in the finished sauce.
  • Let down into liquid it goes smooth without lumps and thickens to the body the dish wants, leaving no floury taste on the tongue.

Where it goes wrong The heat was too high and the corners of the pan went unstirred, so it scorched at the edge; black specks run through the finished sauce and carry a bitterness that no amount of seasoning covers.

Reducing a sauce 95 recipes

  • A steady simmer with the surface moving, never a rolling boil that throws it up the sides and scorches what is left on the base.
  • Judged by behaviour rather than the clock: it coats the back of a spoon and holds a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • The drop in volume is visible against the side of the pan: there is a line to see, not a guess about whether anything has happened.
  • Seasoned at the end and not the beginning, because salt concentrates at exactly the rate everything else does.
  • Nothing is catching underneath: a spoon drawn across the base comes up clean, with no browned film building where the heat is fiercest.

Where it goes wrong It was seasoned at the start and then reduced by half. The salt concentrated along with everything else, so the sauce reaches perfect body and is too salty to serve. And the only remedy is to dilute it and reduce it all over again.

From the pass

Pre-shredded cheese is dusted with anti-caking starch that makes gritty sauce. Grate your own; it's the whole game. Cheese joins the béchamel OFF the heat, a handful at a time, because boiled cheese proteins seize and the sauce breaks to grease. Undercook the pasta by two minutes; it finishes in the oven drinking sauce. Season the béchamel past polite (mustard powder, cayenne, nutmeg) because a quarter of the seasoning gets lost inside all that dairy.
