Cuban · Sauce

Sofrito Cubano

  • Easy
  • 25 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

earthy · aromatic · warm-spiced · briny Leads with an earthy, grounding base, aromatics blooming from the first bite, ending saline and clean.

Ingredients

  • 120ml olive oil
  • 400g white onion, in 5mm dice
  • 250g green cubanelle peppers, seeded and in 5mm dice
  • 40g garlic (about 12 cloves)
  • 10g fine salt, plus more to taste
  • 5g ground cumin
  • 5g dried oregano, crumbled
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 300g ripe tomato, grated on the coarse side and skins discarded
  • 80ml vino seco (Cuban dry white cooking wine)
  • 1g cracked black pepper

Equipment

Sheet pan · Mortar & pestle · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Warm the oil in a wide pan over low heat, add the onion and pepper with a pinch of the salt and sweat until they slump and turn glassy with no trace of browning, about 15 minutes. 15 min
  2. Mash the garlic to a smooth paste in a mortar with a little salt, stir it in with the cumin and oregano and cook only until the raw sting turns nutty and the oil stains gold, roughly 90 seconds.
  3. Add the grated tomato and bay and cook down, stirring less and less often, until the mass pulls back from the pan and a clear rim of red oil pools at the edge.
  4. Pour in the vino seco, scrape the base clean and cook until the alcohol smell has gone and the pan is thick again; season with the remaining salt and the pepper.
  5. Cool completely, pack into a jar and cover the surface with a film of oil; it keeps ten days cold and freezes in ice cube trays.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

Mashing & puréeing 114 recipes

  • Smooth to the degree the dish asks for, judged on the tongue rather than by eye: no lumps, no fibres, no grit, unless texture is the point.
  • No gluey elasticity: a spoonful drops cleanly off the spoon rather than stretching in a rope behind it.
  • The consistency holds: it mounds on a spoon and stays there, neither slumping flat nor standing so stiff that a spoon has to cut it.
  • Nothing separates on the plate: no water or fat weeping out into a ring within a minute of serving.
  • The colour is even throughout, with no streaks of unincorporated fat, liquid, or seasoning at the edges of the bowl.

Where it goes wrong A starchy ingredient met a high-speed blade, so the cells burst and released their starch: what should be light comes out as an elastic glue that lifts off the spoon in one mass.

From the pass

The signal is the oil breaking out. Sofrito is done when the water has gone and the fat re-separates, because only then can the pan get above 100C and the sugars brown. Push the heat early and the onion colours while it is still wet: you get scorched bitterness and no sweetness underneath. Cumin goes into the hot oil, never the watery stage, since its aroma is fat soluble.
