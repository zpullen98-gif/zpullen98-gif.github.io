French · Dessert

Madeleines au Beurre Noisette

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · sweet-edged · nutty-toasted · fiery Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a sweet edge rounding the corners, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients

  • 100 g unsalted butter
  • 2 eggs (about 110 g)
  • 100 g caster sugar
  • 20 g runny honey
  • 100 g plain flour
  • 4 g baking powder
  • 1 g fine salt
  • zest of 1 lemon
  • 15 g unsalted butter, softened, for the moulds
  • 10 g plain flour, for the moulds

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Cook the 100 g butter over medium heat until it stops foaming and smells of hazelnut, then pour it off the browned solids and cool it to 40C (105F).
  2. Whisk the eggs, sugar, honey, salt and zest for 3 minutes to a pale foam, fold in the sifted flour and baking powder, then fold in the brown butter until glossy. 3 min
  3. Cover and refrigerate the batter for at least 12 hours: it has to go into the oven properly cold. 600 min
  4. Butter and flour the moulds and chill them, then fill each shell two thirds full with cold batter straight from the refrigerator.
  5. Bake at 220C (425F) for 4 minutes, drop the oven to 180C (350F) for 6 minutes to 7 minutes, and turn them out the moment the edges brown and the hump springs back. 4 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Folding: keeping the air in 34 recipes

  • The air was actually built first and it shows: whatever was creamed or whipped has changed colour and grown in volume, pale and light rather than dense.
  • The two mixtures were brought closer before being combined: a little of the light one worked into the heavy one first, so the rest is not beaten flat trying to move it.
  • Folded and not stirred: a broad implement down through the middle, across the base, up and over, with the bowl turned between strokes.
  • Stopped the moment the streaks disappear. It is still visibly aerated and holds its shape briefly when the spoon lifts away.
  • Into the tin and into the oven straight away: a batter left standing on the bench gives back the air it was built for.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred until it looked perfectly smooth. Every stroke past the point where the streaks vanish knocks out air that cannot be put back, so the bake comes out dense and short of the tin, and often wet through the middle where there was no structure to hold it.

From the pass

The hump is thermal shock. Cold batter meets a hot mould, the outside sets while the middle is still liquid, and the steam and carbon dioxide can only escape through that soft centre, so it domes. Let the batter warm up first and it bakes flat. The long cold rest also hydrates the flour and relaxes the gluten, which is what keeps the crumb tender rather than rubbery.
