Puerto Rican · Soup

Asopao de Pollo

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

briny · herb-fresh · earthy · aromatic Leads with a briny, maritime salinity, fresh herbs arguing happily on top, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 1 kg bone-in chicken thighs, skinned and cut through the bone in halves
  • 2 tsp adobo seasoning of salt, garlic powder, oregano, pepper and turmeric
  • 120 g sofrito
  • 60 ml achiote oil
  • 120 ml tomato sauce
  • 50 g alcaparrado (green olives with capers)
  • 300 g medium-grain rice
  • 1.8 litres chicken stock
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 100 g green peas
  • 1 roasted red pepper, in strips
  • a handful of recao or cilantro leaves, to finish

The skills inside

Frying the paste until the oil splits

Method

  1. Season the chicken with the adobo and leave it while you build the base.
  2. Warm the achiote oil in a caldero, add the sofrito and cook until it stops spitting water and smells sweet, then add the tomato sauce and alcaparrado and let the colour darken a shade.
  3. Add the chicken and bay, turn the pieces in the sauce for 5 minutes, pour in the stock and simmer until the meat is loose on the bone, about 25 minutes. 5 min
  4. Rain in the rice and cook at a lazy simmer, stirring every couple of minutes, until the grains are just tender but the pot still pours like thick soup, 18 to 20 minutes. 20 min
  5. Stir in the peas, scatter the pepper strips and recao on top, and carry it to the table at once with a jug of hot stock alongside.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Asopao is the opposite pot to arroz con gandules. Here you stir on purpose, to strip starch off the grains for body, and you finish it looser than you want to eat it. Rice keeps drinking after the pot leaves the heat, so a perfect consistency in the pan is sludge by the time it reaches the table. Hold back a ladle of hot stock to slacken each bowl, and serve the minute the rice turns tender.
