Nigerian · Breakfast

Akara

  • Intermediate
  • 35 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

fiery · sweet-edged Leads with real fire that builds and stays, a sweet edge rounding the corners.

Ingredients

  • 400 g dried black-eyed beans
  • 80 g onion
  • 3 red scotch bonnet
  • 10 g salt
  • 800 ml groundnut oil for frying

Equipment

Deep-fry pot

Method

  1. Soak the beans in warm water, then rub them between your palms and float the skins off, changing the water until what is left is clean and white.
  2. Blend the beans with the onion and scotch bonnet using as little water as you dare, stopping at a thick paste that holds a ridge from the spoon.
  3. Beat the paste hard with a wooden spoon until it lightens in colour and a small spoonful floats when dropped into cold water; salt it only now.
  4. Heat the oil to 175C (350F) and drop in spoonfuls well apart, frying until the undersides are deep gold before turning.
  5. Lift them out once both faces are crisp and the middle springs back, and drain standing on a rack so the bottoms stay dry.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Soaking dried goods back to life 69 recipes

  • Fully submerged with room to swell: the level is well above everything at the start and still above it at the end.
  • Given the full time, and judged by what is in the bowl rather than by the hours on the clock.
  • Tested at the CENTRE of the largest piece, cut or pressed. Anything with a hard dry core will still have one after cooking, because the outside softens first and stops the water getting further in.
  • The soaking water was dealt with on purpose: kept because it holds flavour, or thrown away because it holds what should not be eaten, but never simply left undecided.
  • Drained before it is used, so the dish does not silently gain all the water that came with it.

Where it goes wrong Too little water, or too little time. The pieces swell until they are packed tight against each other and the ones in the middle never fully take up water; they will not soften later in the dish either, so the finished plate has hard pieces scattered through it.

Draining fried food: a rack, never paper 77 recipes

  • The rack was in place before the first thing went into the oil, not fetched afterwards while food waited in a spider over the pot.
  • It lands on a rack with air moving underneath it. Anything set down on paper or a flat plate traps its own steam against its base.
  • Salted within seconds of landing, while it is still hot enough to take seasoning.
  • A minute later the underside is still as crisp as the top: no soft flat patch where it has been sitting.
  • Nothing is stacked or piled while hot, however tempting it is to keep it together. A pile steams itself soft from the inside out.

Where it goes wrong It was drained on paper, or piled up to keep warm. Steam leaving the food has nowhere to go but back into the crust, so the underside softens within a minute: the crispness the frying just bought is gone before the plate reaches the table.

From the pass

There is no raising agent. The lift is air you beat into the paste, so blend with barely any water, keep it stiff enough to hold a ridge, and whip until a spoonful floats in cold water. Salt late: salt early slackens the paste and the foam falls. Loose batter spreads flat in the oil and drinks it, giving heavy grey akara instead of a crisp shell over an open crumb. Soak the beans two hours first.
