Nicuatole

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

fiery Leads with real fire that builds and stays.

Ingredients

  • 250 g fresh nixtamal masa, from white corn
  • 1.2 L cold water
  • 220 g sugar
  • 1 stick canela
  • 2 g fine salt

Method

  1. Simmer the canela in 400 ml of the water for 10 minutes, until the liquid is fragrant and faintly amber, then lift the stick out. 10 min
  2. Slake the masa in the remaining 800 ml of cold water, rubbing it between your fingers until the liquid is uniformly milky, then press it through a fine sieve and discard the bran left in the mesh.
  3. Combine both liquids with the sugar and salt in a heavy pan and bring to the boil over medium heat, dragging a wooden spoon across the bottom the whole time.
  4. Hold it at a lazy boil 8-10 minutes: it thickens abruptly, the spoon leaves a channel that holds for a second, and the surface turns glossy and stops tasting chalky. 10 min
  5. Pour it hot into a wetted shallow mould and leave it uncovered at room temperature for about 2 hours, until it is set firm enough to cut into clean squares. 120 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Working with masa 13 recipes

  • Pinch a small piece and roll it closed between finger and thumb: hydrated correctly the edge stays smooth and seals over on itself; a fringe of fine cracks opening along the pinched edge is under hydration, and every piece off that batch will split at the rim. In the hand it feels soft and yielding and leaves a matt film on the palm, never a wet skin that strings when the hand lifts away.
  • After the rest it is damp and matt right through, and it has been kept covered the whole time: a pale dried skin over the mass, or grit that grinds under the thumb when a ball is squashed flat, means it stood open to the air or was shaped before the moisture had reached the centre of the ground grain.
  • It lifts off the press as one clean piece, the rim unbroken and the thickness even from side to side at roughly 2 mm, and a hand shaped piece is judged the same way, with no thin translucent windows worn through the middle. A lacy scalloped rim, or a piece that stretches and tears as it is lifted, fails the mark.
  • Held to the face it smells of toasted sweet corn, faintly mineral and clean. A sharp chalky or soapy smell of raw lime, or a bitterness sitting at the back of the tongue when a raw pinch is tasted, means it was under rinsed and no amount of heat afterwards will drive it off.
  • On a surface holding 190 to 200 C (375 to 390 F) it lifts and balloons across most of its face on the last turn, then settles soft and pliable as it cools. Rested a minute and folded in half, it creases without splitting: a piece that never lifts, or that cracks white along the fold, is failed however good its colour.

Where it goes wrong Too little moisture worked in, and no rest to let what there is reach the centre of the ground grain: it handles well while it is being mixed, then tightens as it stands, so the pieces come off the press with cracked scalloped rims, sit flat and pale on the heat and never lift, and go stiff within a minute of coming off, breaking along the crease instead of folding.

Straining & passing through a sieve 148 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

From the pass

Nicuatole sets on maize starch alone. The grains have to reach a full boil and stay there several minutes to swell and burst, or the gel is weak and the block weeps; it firms as it cools and the loose starch chains re-link. Slake the masa cold and sieve it, because tipped into hot water it lumps on contact. Do not chill it hard or freeze it: the gel goes grainy and pushes its water out.
