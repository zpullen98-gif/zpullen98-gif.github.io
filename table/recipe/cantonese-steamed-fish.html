Cantonese Steamed Fish

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost

herb-fresh · aromatic · smoky Leads with fresh herbs arguing happily on top, aromatics blooming from the first bite, on a drift of smoke.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole fish, immaculately fresh (~700g: sea bass, flounder, tilapia), cleaned; freshness IS the recipe
  • 3cm ginger: half in fine julienne for after, half in coins under the fish; 4 scallions: whites under, greens in long fine shreds
  • 2 tbsp light soy + 1 tbsp water + 1 tsp sugar, warmed, the seasoned soy
  • 3 tbsp neutral oil, heated until it SHIMMERS and just smokes
  • Cilantro sprigs; rice, inevitably

Method

  1. Lay the fish on ginger coins and scallion whites; steam over rolling water 8–10 min (8 per 500g is the old rule) until a chopstick slides into the thickest part. 10 min
  2. POUR OFF the fishy steaming liquid: unlike the Thai school, Cantonese doctrine discards it; the flavor arrives fresh in the next two steps.
  3. Blanket the fish with the ginger julienne and scallion greens; pour the warmed seasoned soy around (not over) the fish.
  4. The finale: the shimmering oil poured directly over the aromatics: the CRACKLE is the dish's applause; the herbs flash-fry against the fish's skin. Cilantro; serve whole, head toward the guest of honor.

How it should come out

  • Before it goes near the steamer: eyes clear and domed rather than sunken and cloudy, gills wet and red, the flesh springing back when pressed, and the smell nothing but clean seawater. This is the one fault that cannot be corrected downstream, so it is checked first and out loud.
  • A chopstick into the thickest part behind the head meets no resistance, and the flesh parts from the backbone in whole sheets. At the bone it reads just-set white, faintly translucent, never chalky and never pink.
  • The steaming liquid has been poured off: no grey pool under the fish. The seasoned soy sits clear around it, at a level below the flesh, so the skin stays unglazed.
  • The aromatics were cooked by the oil, not the steam: ginger julienne curled and translucent at the tips, scallion greens collapsed and shining, and there was an audible crackle at the pour. Slack raw-green shreds sitting in oil mean the oil went on below smoking.
  • Whole and unbroken on the platter: skin intact from head to tail, no flesh torn where the fish was lifted off the steaming plate, head to the guest of honour. A bite from the middle of the fillet tastes of fish first; the soy registers only when the flesh is dragged through it.

Where it goes wrong It is steamed to the timer instead of to the chopstick and comes out ninety seconds long; the fillet shreds into dry cotton fibre when lifted, and a milky pool of squeezed-out juice spreads under the fish and dilutes the soy.

From the pass

The Cantonese steamed fish is a freshness verdict delivered in under half an hour: banquet cooks say the recipe has three ingredients: the fish, the fish, and the fish. The hot-oil pour is the signature technique (it blooms ginger and scallion in one hiss, cousin to the biang biang and gomen finishes elsewhere in this guide), and the timing rule is absolute: an extra ninety seconds of steam is the difference between silk and cotton.
