South African · Bread

Roosterkoek

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

rich · charred Leads with richness that coats and comforts, char used as punctuation.

Ingredients

  • 500 g white bread flour
  • 7 g instant dried yeast
  • 10 g salt
  • 15 g sugar
  • 320 ml lukewarm water
  • 30 g butter, softened, plus more to serve
  • sunflower oil, for the grid
  • apricot jam, to serve

Method

  1. Mix the flour, yeast, salt and sugar, add the water and softened butter, and work to a slack, faintly tacky dough; knead until you can stretch a piece thin enough to see light through it without tearing, 8 to 10 minutes. 10 min
  2. Prove covered until doubled and the dough springs back slowly and incompletely when pressed with a finger, 60 to 90 minutes in a warm room. 90 min
  3. Divide into 12 pieces of about 70 g, roll each into a ball then flatten to a disc 2 cm thick, and prove again under a cloth until visibly puffed and light to lift.
  4. Burn the fire down until the coals are grey and you can hold a hand at grid height for five or six seconds, then oil the grid and lay the discs on well spaced.
  5. Grill, turning every 3 to 4 minutes and shifting them to cooler patches as they colour, until both faces are branded and mottled brown and the underside sounds hollow when knocked, 15 to 20 minutes; tear open and butter while hot. 4 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Grilling over live coals 69 recipes

  • The fire was ready before the food was: coals evenly covered in grey ash with no flames licking through and no raw smoke coming off, and a hot zone and a cooler one both available.
  • The grate was hot and clean when the food landed, and the food released cleanly from it when the time came to turn rather than tearing away.
  • Marks are browned, not burnt: deep colour where the metal touched, with no carbon flaking off and no soot transferred onto the food.
  • The interior reaches its target with a thin gradient, not charred outside and cold within, and not driven through by a flare-up.
  • What comes off the fire smells of fire and food only: no raw wood smoke, no fuel, nothing acrid at the back of the nose.

Where it goes wrong It went on over live flames instead of settled coals, so the outside charred bitter in the flare-ups while the middle stayed cold, and everything on the plate tastes of soot.

Proofing: the rise before the oven 77 recipes

  • Judged on volume against the container rather than on the clock: visibly grown, and grown by roughly the amount the recipe describes.
  • The poke test agrees: a floured fingertip pressed a centimetre in leaves a dent that springs back slowly and only part of the way. Straight back means it is not ready; no spring at all means it has gone.
  • The surface is domed, smooth, and slightly sheened: not cratered, wrinkled, or collapsed, and not skinned over and dry from proofing uncovered.
  • Handled, it feels alive and airy: light for its size, with bubbles visible under the skin.
  • It springs in the oven: the piece lifts in the first minutes and the scores open. A shape that does not move at all was proofed past its point before it went in.

Where it goes wrong It was proofed to the clock in a kitchen colder or warmer than the recipe assumed: overproofed, it collapses as the blade touches it and bakes flat and dense with a sour edge; underproofed, it tears at the side and bakes tight.

From the pass

Bread over coals bakes by heat conducting slowly inward, so the fire has to be well past flame. Over a hot bed the crust sets and blackens in three minutes while the middle is still raw dough, and no amount of extra time fixes a sealed crust. Hold your hand at grid height and count: five or six seconds is right. Turn often so neither face runs ahead, and knock the base; hollow means the crumb has set.
