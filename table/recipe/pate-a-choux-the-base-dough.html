Pate a Choux, the Base Dough

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich Leads with richness that coats and comforts.

Ingredients Method Check the result Pairings Your notes Videos

Ingredients

  • 125 g water
  • 125 g whole milk
  • 110 g unsalted butter, cubed
  • 140 g plain flour, T55 (flour 100 percent, liquid 179 percent, butter 79 percent, egg 161 percent)
  • 5 g caster sugar
  • 3 g fine salt
  • 225 g whole egg, beaten (about 4 medium)

Equipment

Piping bag · Sheet pan

Method

  1. Bring water, milk, butter, sugar and salt to a full rolling boil in one movement, with the butter fully melted before the surface breaks, so you do not boil away liquid before the flour goes in.
  2. Off the heat, beat in all the flour at once until no dry patch remains, then return to medium heat and stir 2 to 3 minutes until the paste pulls from the sides and a thin film forms on the pan base. 3 min
  3. Spread the paste on a cold tray and let it fall to 60C (140F), warm but not hot to a fingertip, then beat the egg in over four additions.
  4. Stop adding egg when the paste falls from the spatula in a slow V that closes in about three seconds, and a furrow drawn with a finger fills back halfway.
  5. Pipe on to a lined tray and bake at 200C (390F) for 20 minutes without opening the door, then drop to 170C (340F) for 15 to 20 minutes until deep gold and light in the hand. 20 min
  6. Pierce each shell at the base and return it to the switched off oven for 10 minutes to dry the inside. 10 min

Observe
Compare the compact cooked paste with the room inside the baked shell. Look for a pipeable paste rather than a pourable batter.
Explain
Choux uses moisture turning to steam for lift. Its structure must set and dry enough to hold the space that expansion creates.
Apply
Use this to explain the shell of a profiterole or éclair, then connect its filling to the custard study. Name the shell and filling separately at the table.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Salted water & the float test 188 recipes

  • The water was salted before anything went in, and tasting it confirms it: seasoned on the tongue, with the salt dissolved rather than sitting on the pot floor.
  • There is enough water, hot enough, that the pot returns to its boil quickly after the food goes in and never stops moving.
  • Where the dish uses the float as its signal, the signal is obeyed: the pieces rise and they come out then, not several minutes later once something else was finished.
  • Nothing is stuck to the bottom of the pot or welded to itself, and the water has not thickened into a cloudy sludge.
  • Drained on a bite rather than on the clock: resistance at the centre, with no chalky raw core.

Where it goes wrong The pot was too small and the water unsalted, so it never recovered its boil: the food sat in slack water, went gummy at the surface, and comes out bland in a way that no sauce afterwards can fix.

From the pass

The paste is a cooked starch gel carrying a great deal of egg. Water in it flashes to steam and inflates the shell while gelatinised starch and coagulating egg hold the wall until it sets. Add egg above 60C (140F) and it scrambles into lumps. Underbake and the wall is still soft, so the steam condenses and the shell folds.
