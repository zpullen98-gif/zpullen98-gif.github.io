North Dakota Wild Rice Hotdish

  • Intermediate
  • 1 h 30 m
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

umami-deep · rich Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, richness that coats and comforts.

Ingredients

  • 200g wild rice
  • 700g ground beef
  • 1 onion, diced
  • 250g mushrooms
  • 55g butter
  • 55g flour
  • 500ml beef stock
  • 250ml milk
  • 1 tsp Worcestershire
  • 150g cheddar
  • Salt and pepper

Method

  1. Simmer the wild rice 45 min until the grains split; drain. 45 min
  2. Brown the beef with the onion and mushrooms; drain and season.
  3. Make a roux, whisk in the stock and milk, and simmer to a sauce with the Worcestershire.
  4. Fold in the rice and beef, turn into a dish, and top with cheddar.
  5. Bake at 190C for 30 min until bubbling. 30 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Making a roux 32 recipes

  • Taken to the stage the dish named and no further, judged against that colour (pale, blond, or brown to brick), and it got there slowly enough to have coloured evenly.
  • It is smooth and mobile: a paste with no dry pockets of flour and no lumps, loosening as it warms rather than sitting stiff in the pan.
  • The raw-flour smell has gone and been replaced by the toasted, biscuity smell of its stage, with nothing acrid behind it.
  • No black specks anywhere. One scorched fleck condemns the batch; it cannot be stirred back in and it will be tasted in the finished sauce.
  • Let down into liquid it goes smooth without lumps and thickens to the body the dish wants, leaving no floury taste on the tongue.

Where it goes wrong The heat was too high and the corners of the pan went unstirred, so it scorched at the edge; black specks run through the finished sauce and carry a bitterness that no amount of seasoning covers.

Searing: the hard crust 141 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Wild rice grows in the northern lakes and rivers and gives the hotdish a nutty backbone. Cook the rice until the grains truly burst; undercooked wild rice stays tough no matter how long it bakes.
