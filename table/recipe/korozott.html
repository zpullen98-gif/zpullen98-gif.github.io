Hungarian · Starter

Körözött

  • Easy
  • 15 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · silky · herb-fresh · nutty-toasted Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a silken, spoon-slow texture, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 250 g juhtúró (soft sheep's milk curd), at room temperature
  • 100 g unsalted butter, soft
  • 60 g onion, grated to a pulp
  • 10 g édesnemes paprika (noble sweet, ground)
  • 5 g caraway seed, toasted and ground
  • 10 g mustard
  • 15 g chives, finely cut
  • fine salt

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Beat the butter on its own until it is pale, light and holds a soft peak.
  2. Rub the paprika and ground caraway into the butter and let it stand until the butter has gone apricot and smells of spice rather than of dust.
  3. Work in the curd a spoonful at a time, then the grated onion, mustard and chives, and salt harder than feels right.
  4. Press into a bowl, cover and hold in the cold for an hour, then bring it back to cool room temperature before serving.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 70 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

From the pass

Paprika's colour and most of its aroma sit in a fat-soluble oleoresin. Stirred into cheese alone it stays gritty and pale and the spread tastes of raw dust; creamed into soft butter first, the pigment dissolves and the whole bowl turns apricot. Butter below about 18C (65F) will not accept the curd, and the mixture breaks into wet grains that no amount of beating brings back.
