Pork and Century Egg Congee

  • Easy
  • 20 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

aromatic · fiery Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, real fire that builds and stays.

Ingredients

  • 200 g jasmine or other long grain rice
  • 2.5 L water
  • 15 ml neutral oil
  • 8 g salt, plus more to finish
  • 250 g pork loin, rubbed with 6 g salt and chilled overnight
  • 2 century eggs, peeled and diced
  • 25 g ginger, in fine matchsticks
  • 2 g ground white pepper
  • 30 g spring onion, sliced thin
  • 20 g fried shallots

Method

  1. Rinse the rice until the water runs milky rather than white, drain it well, then toss it with the oil and salt and leave it 30 minutes; an hour in the freezer does the same job faster. 30 min
  2. Bring the water to a rolling boil in a tall pot, add the rice and ginger, return it to the boil, then hold a lively simmer with the lid ajar, scraping up from the base every few minutes.
  3. After 50 to 70 minutes the grains will have burst and lost their edges, and the congee should fall off the spoon in a sheet rather than in drops. Slacken with boiling water if it gets ahead of you. 70 min
  4. Slice the salted pork thin across the grain, stir it in with half the century egg and simmer 5 minutes, just until the pork turns opaque and firms. 5 min
  5. Season with the white pepper and more salt, then ladle out and top with the remaining century egg, the spring onion and the fried shallots.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 49 recipes

  • It came off the heat BEFORE the target temperature, measured with a probe rather than by time, because carryover keeps driving the inside up after it is out.
  • The probe went into the coldest likely part rather than the easiest one to reach: the reading that matters is the lowest one, not the first one.
  • Rested for a time matched to its size, somewhere warm, and not cut into partway through to see how it is doing.
  • The board is nearly dry when it is finally cut. A spreading pool means it was cut too early, and that liquid should have stayed inside.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong It was cut straight off the heat, usually because everything else was ready. The muscle is still contracted and the juice still driven to the centre, so it runs out onto the board; the meat is drier than it ever needed to be, and the flavour that left it is sitting in a puddle under the knife.

Knife cuts: dice, julienne, bias 115 recipes

  • Take any three pieces from anywhere in the pile: they match closely enough in every dimension that you would have to measure them to argue otherwise.
  • The cut faces are flat and clean rather than ragged or crushed; leaves and herbs are cut through, not bruised, and the board carries no dark smear where they were.
  • The stated size is the actual size, held against a rule or a known reference rather than called about right.
  • Everything in the pile is the shape that was asked for: no wedges, no half pieces, and no offcuts swept in to avoid waste.
  • They cook evenly, which is the entire point: at the end of cooking nothing is still raw and nothing has collapsed.

Where it goes wrong The knife was dull, or the vegetable was never squared off first, so the pieces vary: the small ones are mush by the time the large ones are cooked, and the dish has two textures where it wanted one.

From the pass

Congee is a starch suspension, not rice sitting in soup, and agitation is what builds it: stirring shears starch off the grains until the liquid thickens on its own. The oil and salt rub, or a spell in the freezer, damages the grain surface so it collapses sooner. Work at about 1:12 rice to water by weight and stop it looser than you want to eat it, because congee sets hard as it cools.
