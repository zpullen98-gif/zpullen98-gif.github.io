Brazilian · Main

Barreado

  • Advanced
  • 40 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

nutty-toasted · earthy · aromatic · silky Leads with toasted, nutty warmth throughout, an earthy, grounding base, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 2 kg beef chuck, in 4 cm cubes
  • 200 g smoked bacon, diced
  • 400 g onion, sliced
  • 30 g garlic, chopped
  • 12 g ground cumin
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 18 g fine salt
  • 5 g black pepper
  • 300 ml water
  • 250 g cassava flour, for the seal
  • 200 g cassava flour, toasted, to serve
  • 4 ripe bananas, to serve
  • 2 oranges, in segments, to serve

Method

  1. Toss the beef with the bacon, onion, garlic, cumin, bay, salt and pepper and leave it 2 hours in the fridge. 120 min
  2. Pack it into a heavy pot, pour in the water and put the lid on.
  3. Mix the 250 g of cassava flour with enough water to make a stiff putty and press it right around the join of lid and pot so no steam can leave.
  4. Cook at 130C (265F) for 10 hours to 12 hours without opening it; the meat is done when it collapses into threads under the back of a spoon. 600 min
  5. Mash the meat into its own fat and juices to a rough purée and serve it with the toasted flour, the banana and the orange.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

  • Smooth to the degree the dish asks for, judged on the tongue rather than by eye: no lumps, no fibres, no grit, unless texture is the point.
  • No gluey elasticity: a spoonful drops cleanly off the spoon rather than stretching in a rope behind it.
  • The consistency holds: it mounds on a spoon and stays there, neither slumping flat nor standing so stiff that a spoon has to cut it.
  • Nothing separates on the plate: no water or fat weeping out into a ring within a minute of serving.
  • The colour is even throughout, with no streaks of unincorporated fat, liquid, or seasoning at the edges of the bowl.

Where it goes wrong A starchy ingredient met a high-speed blade, so the cells burst and released their starch: what should be light comes out as an elastic glue that lifts off the spoon in one mass.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 130 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

Nothing in the pot thickens anything, and there is no sauce until you make one. The long cook turns the chuck's collagen to gelatine and renders the bacon fat; mashing the meat while it is hot drives gelatine, fat and juices together, and the gelatine is the emulsifier that keeps the fat in suspension instead of floating clear. Work it warm, because gelatine stiffens as it cools. The toasted farinha at the table is the second thickener, drinking up what the meat cannot hold.
