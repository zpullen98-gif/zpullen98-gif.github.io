Kotlet Schabowy

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

rich · bright Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light.

Ingredients

  • 4 boneless pork loin chops, 150 g each, cut across the grain
  • 80 g plain flour
  • 2 eggs, beaten with 1 tbsp water
  • 150 g fine dry white breadcrumbs, bułka tarta
  • 200 g lard, or clarified butter
  • Fine salt and white pepper
  • 1 lemon, in wedges

Method

  1. Nick through the rim of fat and silverskin at 2 cm intervals all round each chop.
  2. Beat the chops between plastic to an even 5 mm, thinner at the edges than the middle, then salt both sides and leave 10 minutes until the surface looks damp. 10 min
  3. Pass through flour, then egg, then crumbs, pressing only lightly; crumbs packed down glue themselves to the meat and turn to sand.
  4. Heat the lard 1 cm deep to 170C (338F). Slide in one cutlet and tilt the pan so hot fat washes over the top; it should sizzle steadily, not roar.
  5. Fry about 3 minutes a side to deep gold, then rest on a wire rack, never on paper, so the underside stays crisp. 3 min

Resting and slicing across the grainIllustrated study · 2 numbered views

Find the direction of the muscle fibres, then compare a cross-cut slice with a strip cut along them.

Resting and slicing direction are separate decisions. Resting can reduce liquid lost when meat is cut, but a pool on the board alone cannot establish how long it rested. The hanger steak is a general technique example, not Brennan’s plating or doneness standard.

One numbered view at a time. No grade or change to your learning record.

    Across the grain

    Locate the long, parallel fibres before slicing across them. Shortening those fibres makes a coarse-grained cut easier to chew. The small amount of board liquid is illustrative, not a pass-or-fail test.

  2. 2
    Along the grain

    These strips keep long fibres running along their length. The larger pool is a prompt to review resting and cooking, rather than proof that the steak was sliced too soon.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 56 recipes

  • It came off the heat BEFORE the target temperature, measured with a probe rather than by time, because carryover keeps driving the inside up after it is out.
  • The probe went into the coldest likely part rather than the easiest one to reach: the reading that matters is the lowest one, not the first one.
  • Rested for a time matched to its size, somewhere warm, and not cut into partway through to see how it is doing.
  • The board is nearly dry when it is finally cut. A spreading pool means it was cut too early, and that liquid should have stayed inside.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong It was cut straight off the heat, usually because everything else was ready. The muscle is still contracted and the juice still driven to the centre, so it runs out onto the board; the meat is drier than it ever needed to be, and the flavour that left it is sitting in a puddle under the knife.

From the pass

Pork loin is lean and at 5 mm it is cooked in roughly six minutes, so the crumb sets the schedule, not the meat. Below 160C (320F) the coating drinks fat and goes greasy; above 185C (365F) it darkens before the centre is warm. Nicking the fat rim matters more than it sounds: the connective tissue there shrinks first and cups the cutlet off the pan, leaving a pale ring of raw crumb.
