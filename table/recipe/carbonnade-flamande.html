Carbonnade Flamande

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

rich · bright · sweet-edged · nutty-toasted Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 1.3 kg beef chuck, in 4 cm cubes
  • 8 g fine salt
  • 3 g black pepper
  • 60 g butter
  • 700 g onions, sliced
  • 30 g plain flour
  • 500 ml dark Belgian abbey ale
  • 30 g dark brown sugar
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 4 g thyme leaves
  • 2 thick slices of country bread
  • 40 g strong Dijon mustard
  • 20 ml red wine vinegar

Method

  1. Dry the beef on cloth, salt it, and brown it hard in half the butter in two batches; a crowded pan steams and you lose the fond the whole sauce is built on.
  2. Drop the heat, add the rest of the butter and the onions with a pinch of salt, and cook 25 minutes until they slump and go tawny, scraping the brown film off the base as their water releases it. 25 min
  3. Dust in the flour, return the beef, pour in the ale, and add the sugar, bay, thyme and pepper. Bring it to a bare simmer.
  4. Spread the bread thickly with mustard, float it mustard side down on the surface, cover, and braise at 150C (300F) for 3 hours. Stir the collapsed bread through the sauce and finish with the vinegar off the heat. 180 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 39 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

The bare simmer: holding liquid below the boil 183 recipes

  • The surface is read rather than assumed: bubbles break slowly in one or two places, or the surface merely shivers, and it does not reach a rolling boil at any point.
  • It was held there: the pot was watched and the heat adjusted rather than set once and left, and there is no tide mark up the pot wall from a boil-over.
  • The liquid stayed as clear as it should be: no clouding from agitation, and no fat churned into an emulsion through it.
  • Whatever is in the liquid is still intact: nothing shredded, broken up, or worried apart by movement.
  • The level is roughly where the elapsed time should have left it; a large drop is the evidence that it ran hotter than this while nobody was looking.

Where it goes wrong It was brought to a boil and then left on the same heat, so it churned for the whole cook: the liquid clouded, the fat emulsified into it, and the solids broke apart into the broth.

From the pass

Brown ale is bitter and three hours of reduction concentrates that, so sugar goes in at the start and vinegar at the end: sweetness suppresses the perception of bitterness, acid lifts what long heat has flattened. The bread is a thickener dressed up as folklore. Its starch swells and the crumb dissolves into the sauce, while the mustard loses its raw volatile heat in minutes and leaves behind the toasted bitterness of the seed.
