Hyderabadi Dum Biryani

  • Advanced
  • 45 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$$$

warm-spiced · rich · bright · aromatic Leads with warm spice humming through the middle, richness that coats and comforts, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 700 g goat shoulder on the bone, in 4 cm pieces
  • 500 g aged basmati rice
  • 300 g whole milk yogurt
  • 200 g onion, sliced thin and fried brown in ghee
  • 30 g ginger and garlic paste
  • 20 g green chillies, slit
  • 15 g mint leaves, torn
  • 15 g coriander leaves, chopped
  • 8 g garam masala
  • 6 g red chilli powder
  • 30 ml lime juice
  • 60 g ghee
  • 0.5 g saffron steeped in 60 ml warm milk
  • 4 green cardamom, 1 black cardamom, 5 cm cassia bark, 4 cloves, 2 bay leaves
  • 4 L water
  • 45 g salt
  • 150 g plain flour worked to a stiff dough for sealing

Equipment

Rolling pin

Method

  1. Mix the goat with the yogurt, ginger and garlic paste, chilli powder, garam masala, lime, chillies, mint, coriander, half the fried onion, 20 g of the ghee and 12 g of the salt. Leave it 4 hours at cool room temperature, or overnight in the fridge. 240 min
  2. Soak the rice 30 minutes and drain. Boil the water hard with the rest of the salt and the whole spices, drop in the rice and cook 3 to 4 minutes only, until a grain crushed between finger and thumb is soft outside with a firm white core. Drain at once. 30 min
  3. Spread the marinated meat in an even layer in a heavy pot, pile the drained rice over it without pressing, and scatter on the rest of the fried onion, the saffron milk and the remaining melted ghee.
  4. Roll the dough into a rope, press it round the rim and clamp the lid on. Cook 5 minutes on high to raise steam, then on the lowest flame, or on a tava, for 45 minutes. 5 min
  5. Rest it sealed off the heat for 15 minutes, break the crust, and lift the biryani out in wedges with a flat spoon from the bottom of the pot rather than stirring. 15 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

The overnight rest: letting a dish marry 18 recipes

  • Cooled quickly before it went in (spread out, decanted, or stood in cold water) rather than carried to the fridge hot in the pot it was cooked in.
  • Protected from the rest of the fridge once cold: covered, or given space where nothing strong is sitting beside it. A rest this long is long enough to take on whatever it shares the shelf with, and some dishes want the open air.
  • Given the whole time. This is the one technique whose entire content is waiting, and cutting it short leaves the dish exactly as it was when it went in.
  • Brought back deliberately: reheated gently, or brought up to the temperature it is meant to be eaten at, rather than served straight out of the cold because it is late.
  • Tasted and corrected AFTER the rest. Seasoning and brightness both flatten in the cold, so the dish is finished at the end of the wait rather than at the start of it.

Where it goes wrong It went into the fridge hot and covered. It held its own heat for hours in the range where food spoils, and the lid sweated and dripped back into it all night: what comes out is duller and wetter than what went in, which is the exact opposite of the point.

Marinating: acid, salt and time 55 recipes

  • Contact is total: every surface is wetted, and the pieces are not welded into a clump with dry faces where they pressed together.
  • The stated time was kept and kept cold, read against a clock, because this is the one technique where longer is a different result rather than a safer one.
  • It worked at the surface and no further: cut a piece and the marinade’s colour stops a few millimetres in. A protein gone opaque and chalky to the centre before any heat reached it has been cooked by the acid.
  • The texture is still springy and whole, not mushy, mealy, or fraying at the surface when it is lifted.
  • Where the next step browns it, the surface was patted dry first: a wet surface cannot brown, and the marinade’s sugars will scorch before the food colours.

Where it goes wrong It was left well beyond the stated time on the assumption that more is better, so acid and salt worked through the thin edges: those cook to cotton and read grainy while the thick centre tastes of nothing at all.

From the pass

Dum finishes rice in trapped steam instead of water. The grain goes in at about 70 percent, soft outside and chalky in the middle, and the meat's rising juices carry it the rest of the way. Boil the rice one minute too long and the top layer turns to paste under the lid; leave the seal open and the steam escapes, giving chalky grains over wet meat. Use basmati aged a year: the drier grain elongates rather than splitting.
