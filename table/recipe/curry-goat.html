Curry Goat

  • Intermediate
  • 3 h
  • Serves 4
  • $

earthy · aromatic · fiery · charred Leads with an earthy, grounding base, aromatics blooming from the first bite, with a bitter-edged char at the rim.

Ingredients

  • 1kg goat (or mutton), bone-in, chunked
  • 3 tbsp Jamaican curry powder (turmeric-heavy)
  • 1 scotch bonnet, 6 spring onions, garlic, ginger, thyme
  • 2 potatoes, chunked
  • Oil, salt, pimento berries

The skills inside

Searing: the hard crustFrying the paste until the oil splitsMarinating: acid, salt and time

Allergen screen

None found among the 14 screened.

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Season meat with curry powder and aromatics; marinate overnight.
  2. 'Burn the curry': fry another spoon of curry powder in oil until it darkens and smells toasty.
  3. Brown meat in that; add water to barely cover; simmer 2–2.5 h until sliding off the bone. 300 min
  4. Potatoes in for the last 30: they thicken the gravy as they fray at the edges.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 26 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

Marinating: acid, salt and time 46 recipes

  • Contact is total: every surface is wetted, and the pieces are not welded into a clump with dry faces where they pressed together.
  • The stated time was kept and kept cold, read against a clock, because this is the one technique where longer is a different result rather than a safer one.
  • It worked at the surface and no further: cut a piece and the marinade’s colour stops a few millimetres in. A protein gone opaque and chalky to the centre before any heat reached it has been cooked by the acid.
  • The texture is still springy and whole, not mushy, mealy, or fraying at the surface when it is lifted.
  • Where the next step browns it, the surface was patted dry first: a wet surface cannot brown, and the marinade’s sugars will scorch before the food colours.

Where it goes wrong It was left well beyond the stated time on the assumption that more is better, so acid and salt worked through the thin edges: those cook to cotton and read grainy while the thick centre tastes of nothing at all.

From the pass

Burning the curry, blooming an extra spoonful in hot oil until it darkens and smells toasty, is the step that separates curry goat from stew with curry powder in it; raw curry powder tastes like dust, burnt curry tastes like Sunday. Jamaican curry is turmeric-heavy, the Indian spice box rebuilt by indentured laborers who came after emancipation. Bone-in is mandatory: the bones feed the gravy, and the potatoes fraying at the edges in the last half hour are the thickener.
