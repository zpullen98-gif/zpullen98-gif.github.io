Profiteroles (Pâte à Choux Master)

  • Advanced
  • 1 h 30 m
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · silky · earthy · floral Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a silken, spoon-slow texture, leaving a faint perfume.

Ingredients Method Check the result Pairings Your notes Videos

Ingredients

  • The choux: 125ml water + 125ml milk, 100g butter, pinch salt, 1 tsp sugar, 150g flour, 4 eggs (about: the batter tells you)
  • Vanilla ice cream or crème pâtissière for filling
  • The sauce: 150g dark chocolate + 150ml hot cream, whisked to a pourable ganache
  • A piping bag; a wet finger for the peaks
  • An oven you will NOT open for 25 minutes, on your honor

Method

  1. Boil water, milk, butter, salt, sugar; dump in the flour and beat over heat 2 min until the panade pulls from the pan and films the bottom: this dry-out step is the puff's foundation. 2 min
  2. Off heat, cool 3 min; beat in eggs one at a time; STOP when the batter falls from the spoon in a slow V. You may not need all of the last egg. 3 min
  3. Pipe walnut-sized mounds; wet-finger the peaks flat; bake 200°C 25 min WITHOUT OPENING, then 170°C 10 min to dry the walls; pierce each and cool. 25 min
  4. Split; a scoop of ice cream inside; stack in a bowl; warm ganache poured over at the table.

  1. 1
    Cook the panade

    The flour and hot liquid form a thick cohesive paste. Follow the recipe’s cooking step before cooling enough to incorporate eggs.

  2. 2
    Incorporate the eggs

    Mix eggs in gradually, incorporating each addition. The finished paste is smooth and pipeable; the lifted sample shows a soft tapered V.

  3. 3
    Pipe with room to expand

    Similar-sized mounds help the batch bake consistently. Leave space between them and keep the recipe’s intended shape.

  4. 4
    Examine the baked cavity

    Steam expands the paste while the shell sets. The cut puff reveals a hollow interior; complete the recipe’s drying and cooling before filling.

Observe
Compare the compact cooked paste with the room inside the baked shell. Look for a pipeable paste rather than a pourable batter.
Explain
Choux uses moisture turning to steam for lift. Its structure must set and dry enough to hold the space that expansion creates.
Apply
Use this to explain the shell of a profiterole or éclair, then connect its filling to the custard study. Name the shell and filling separately at the table.

How it should come out

  • Before the oven: the batter falls from the spoon in a slow V rather than a blob or a ribbon, and each piped mound holds a defined dome that settles under a wet finger without spreading toward its neighbour.
  • Each puff is round and roughly double its piped height, with no baked-on peak and no flat sunken side; the tray reads as one size.
  • Colour is even gold-brown over the dome and carries down to the crease where it meets the base: a blond, soft-looking waist there means it came out early.
  • Ten minutes on the rack and nothing has sunk or gone soft: pressed at the side the wall resists, then cracks. Split one and it is a single hollow cavity with a few wisps of webbing, walls dry to the touch, no wet dough sitting in the base.
  • The ganache coats the back of a spoon and drapes over the stack in one glossy sheet; two minutes under it, the shells still crack.

Where it goes wrong They come out at the 25-minute mark without the low dry-out leg and are left unpierced: the walls are still damp inside, their own trapped steam soaks them on the rack, and within ten minutes crisp shells have gone to chewy leather that turns to sponge under the ganache.

From the pass

Choux is the only pastry leavened almost purely by steam, which is why the two disciplines are absolute: cook the panade dry so it can drink the eggs, and never open the oven while the steam is doing the lifting; one peek and the puffs sigh flat forever. The V-test beats every egg count ever printed. Master this dough and you own profiteroles, éclairs, gougères and the croquembouche; it is four doors behind one handle.
