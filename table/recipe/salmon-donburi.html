Salmon Donburi

  • Easy
  • 20 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

bright · briny · nutty-toasted Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, a briny, maritime salinity, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 2 sushi-grade salmon fillets or hot-seared salmon
  • Warm sushi rice (rice + 2 tbsp seasoned vinegar)
  • Avocado, cucumber
  • Soy, wasabi, sesame, nori
  • Optional: spicy mayo (mayo + sriracha)

Method

  1. Season warm rice with sushi vinegar; fan to glossy.
  2. Slice salmon against the grain (raw) or sear 2 min a side and flake. 2 min
  3. Bowl the rice; arrange salmon, avocado, cucumber.
  4. Finish with sesame, nori, and sauce.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 56 recipes

  • It came off the heat BEFORE the target temperature, measured with a probe rather than by time, because carryover keeps driving the inside up after it is out.
  • The probe went into the coldest likely part rather than the easiest one to reach: the reading that matters is the lowest one, not the first one.
  • Rested for a time matched to its size, somewhere warm, and not cut into partway through to see how it is doing.
  • The board is nearly dry when it is finally cut. A spreading pool means it was cut too early, and that liquid should have stayed inside.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong It was cut straight off the heat, usually because everything else was ready. The muscle is still contracted and the juice still driven to the centre, so it runs out onto the board; the meat is drier than it ever needed to be, and the flavour that left it is sitting in a puddle under the knife.

Searing: the hard crust 141 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Body-temperature rice, cool fish: the temperature contrast is the luxury, and it is the entire reason sushi rice is fanned rather than refrigerated; cold rice turns hard and dull as its starch retrogrades. Season the rice while warm so the vinegar penetrates. Slice the salmon against the grain in single confident strokes, sawing shreds the flesh, and if searing instead, two minutes a side leaves the center blushing, which is where the richness lives.
