Dal Makhani

  • Advanced
  • 50 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

rich · aromatic · warm-spiced · fiery Leads with richness that coats and comforts, aromatics blooming from the first bite, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients

  • 250 g sabut urad (whole black gram), picked over for stones
  • 60 g rajma (red kidney beans)
  • 2.5 litres water, plus 2 litres for soaking
  • 30 g ginger, half pounded to a paste and half cut in fine julienne
  • 20 g garlic, pounded to a paste
  • 2 green chillies, slit lengthways
  • 500 g ripe tomatoes, blended smooth and passed through a sieve
  • 5 g deghi mirch, for colour rather than heat
  • 150 g unsalted butter, cubed and kept cold
  • 120 ml double cream
  • 3 g garam masala, ground within the week
  • 2 g kasoori methi, crushed between the palms
  • 12 g fine salt

Equipment

Mortar & pestle

Method

  1. Rinse the urad and rajma until the water runs clear, then soak both in 2 litres of cold water for at least 8 hours: the urad is ready when a bean crushes to a smooth paste between finger and thumb. 480 min
  2. Drain, cover with the fresh water, add the ginger paste, garlic, chillies and 8 g of the salt, boil hard for 10 minutes, then drop the flame until the surface only trembles and hold it there 4 to 5 hours, topping up, until the skins have collapsed and the liquid turns glossy and clings to the spoon. 10 min
  3. Stir in the sieved tomato and the deghi mirch and cook uncovered another 45 minutes, dragging a spoon across the base every few minutes, until the raw tomato smell is gone and the colour has dulled from orange to a deep maroon. 45 min
  4. Take the pot off the heat and beat in the cold butter one cube at a time, waiting until each disappears before the next goes in so it never pools, then the cream: the dal should be the colour of milky coffee and fall from the spoon in one thick sheet.
  5. Crush the kasoori methi over the surface, add the garam masala, the ginger julienne and the remaining salt to taste, and rest it 20 minutes off the heat before serving. 20 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Straining & passing through a sieve 69 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

Salted water & the float test 89 recipes

  • The water was salted before anything went in, and tasting it confirms it: seasoned on the tongue, with the salt dissolved rather than sitting on the pot floor.
  • There is enough water, hot enough, that the pot returns to its boil quickly after the food goes in and never stops moving.
  • Where the dish uses the float as its signal, the signal is obeyed: the pieces rise and they come out then, not several minutes later once something else was finished.
  • Nothing is stuck to the bottom of the pot or welded to itself, and the water has not thickened into a cloudy sludge.
  • Drained on a bite rather than on the clock: resistance at the centre, with no chalky raw core.

Where it goes wrong The pot was too small and the water unsalted, so it never recovered its boil: the food sat in slack water, went gummy at the surface, and comes out bland in a way that no sauce afterwards can fix.

From the pass

Urad starch has to leach out and gelatinise before the dal will body up, and that only happens under a simmer, over hours. Boil it hard and the beans burst before the starch disperses, leaving grit suspended in thin water. The butter goes in cold and off the heat: dropped into a boiling dal it melts faster than it can emulsify and the fat floats off as a slick.
