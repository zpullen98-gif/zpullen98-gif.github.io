Provence & the Midi · Soup

Soupe au Pistou

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · herb-fresh · aromatic · warm-spiced Leads with richness that coats and comforts, fresh herbs arguing happily on top, fading in warm spice.

Ingredients

  • 250 g fresh white coco beans, shelled, or soaked dried white beans
  • 150 g fresh red borlotti beans, shelled
  • 2 courgettes, in 1 cm dice
  • 250 g green beans, in 2 cm lengths
  • 2 potatoes, diced
  • 2 carrots, diced
  • 1 leek, sliced
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, peeled and chopped
  • 80 g small pasta, coquillettes or broken vermicelli
  • 2 litres water
  • Salt and black pepper
  • For the pistou: 80 g basil leaves, from three large bunches
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 80 g hard sheep's cheese made with vegetarian rennet, finely grated
  • 150 ml fruity olive oil
  • 1 ripe tomato, peeled and seeded, Nice style (optional)

Equipment

Mortar & pestle · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Simmer the shelled beans in the salted water until their skins are tender and the insides no longer chalky, 30 to 40 minutes depending on how freshly they were picked. 40 min
  2. Add potato, carrot and leek, then ten minutes later the green beans, courgette and tomato, so that everything arrives at tender together instead of the soft ones dissolving.
  3. Cook the pasta in the soup itself; the starch it sheds is what gives the broth its cling.
  4. Pound the garlic to a paste with a pinch of salt, add the basil a handful at a time and work it to a dark green cream, then the cheese, then the oil in a thread.
  5. Pull the pot off the heat, let it stop bubbling, and only then stir in half the pistou; the rest goes on the table in the mortar.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Building an emulsion 40 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Mashing & puréeing 114 recipes

  • Smooth to the degree the dish asks for, judged on the tongue rather than by eye: no lumps, no fibres, no grit, unless texture is the point.
  • No gluey elasticity: a spoonful drops cleanly off the spoon rather than stretching in a rope behind it.
  • The consistency holds: it mounds on a spoon and stays there, neither slumping flat nor standing so stiff that a spoon has to cut it.
  • Nothing separates on the plate: no water or fat weeping out into a ring within a minute of serving.
  • The colour is even throughout, with no streaks of unincorporated fat, liquid, or seasoning at the edges of the bowl.

Where it goes wrong A starchy ingredient met a high-speed blade, so the cells burst and released their starch: what should be light comes out as an elastic glue that lifts off the spoon in one mass.

From the pass

Basil's scent lives in volatile oils that boil away, and its phenols oxidise to a blackish grey the moment they meet sustained heat. So the pistou goes in off the flame, never simmers, and reheated leftovers want a fresh spoonful. Note also that there are no pine nuts: that is Genoa. Here it is garlic, basil, cheese and oil, pounded in that order so the salt and garlic can grind the leaves.
