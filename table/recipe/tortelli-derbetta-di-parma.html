Tortelli d'Erbetta di Parma

  • Intermediate
  • 60 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

umami-deep · rich · warm-spiced · earthy Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, richness that coats and comforts, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 300 g farina 00
  • 3 whole eggs
  • 400 g Swiss chard leaves, stalks removed
  • 250 g fresh ricotta, drained overnight in a sieve
  • 120 g Parmigiano Reggiano, grated
  • 1 egg yolk
  • quarter of a nutmeg, grated
  • 80 g unsalted butter
  • fine salt

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Wilt the chard in only the water clinging to the leaves, then wring it in a cloth until nothing more runs out and the ball is dense and dark; chop it fine.
  2. Fold the chard, ricotta, Parmigiano, yolk and nutmeg together and season; the filling should sit on a spoon in a soft mound and hold its shape rather than slump.
  3. Knead the flour and eggs 10 minutes to a firm dough, rest 30 minutes, then roll by hand to a sheet you can see your fingers through, working fast so it does not dry and crack. 10 min
  4. Place walnut-sized mounds along the sheet, fold it over, press the air out from the fold outward, and cut with a fluted wheel so the crimped edge seals itself.
  5. Boil 3 minutes until they float and the sealed edge turns translucent, then dress with butter melted to a nut-brown foam and a heavy hand of Parmigiano. 3 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pie crust & blind baking 29 recipes

  • The shell goes into the oven cold and the oven is fully up to heat, so the structure sets before the fat has time to melt out of it.
  • Lined and weighted right into the corners rather than heaped in the middle, so the sides are held against the tin as the fat softens.
  • Docked where it is not meant to puff, and the base stays flat rather than lifting into a dome under its own steam.
  • The weights come out and the base gets dry heat of its own before anything wet arrives. Pale and damp is not baked, and a filling will not finish it.
  • The edges are set and coloured and the shell releases from the tin rather than gripping it.

Where it goes wrong It went in warm, or the weights only filled the middle. The sides slump down the tin before the pastry sets, so the case is shallower than it should be, the filling overflows it, and the base is still raw underneath: the soggy bottom, arrived at three ways at once.

Kneading dough 57 recipes

  • The work changed it: what began shaggy and rough is one smooth, cohesive mass, with nothing raggy hanging off it.
  • The surface has gone satin and barely tacky, releasing cleanly from hands and board instead of leaving a trail.
  • Worked to the point this dough asks for and no further: springy and elastic where structure is wanted, just brought together where tenderness is.
  • Torn or cut open, the inside is uniform: no dry streaks, no unmixed pockets, no core of flour that never met the water.
  • After its rest it rolls and shapes without snapping back. A dough that fights the pin needed more rest, not more force.

Where it goes wrong Sticking was fought with flour instead of technique or patience, stroke after stroke, until the dough had taken in far more than the formula asked; it turns tight and dry under the hands, and bakes dense however well everything else goes.

From the pass

Chard carries far more water than it looks. Leave it in and the filling steams inside the parcel, softening the seal from within until it splits in the pot, so wring the leaves until the cloth runs dry. Undrained ricotta does the same. Judge it on the spoon: a filling that slumps there will slump on the sheet, and no crimp will hold a seam it is pushing apart.
