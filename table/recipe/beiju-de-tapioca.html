Brazilian · Bread

Beiju de Tapioca

  • Easy
  • 15 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich Leads with richness that coats and comforts.

Ingredients

  • 250 g polvilho doce (sweet cassava starch)
  • 80 ml cold water
  • 3 g fine salt
  • 40 g fresh or frozen grated coconut
  • 20 g unsalted butter

Equipment

Sheet pan · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Rub the water and salt into the polvilho doce with your fingertips until the starch feels like damp sand: no dry pockets, no wet lumps.
  2. Press the damp starch through a fine sieve onto a tray. What falls through should be loose and powdery; discard the hard grains left behind.
  3. Scatter a 4 mm even layer of sieved starch across a dry non-stick pan over medium heat and press it flat. The grains turn glassy and knit into one sheet in about 90 seconds.
  4. Flip when the disc lifts free in a single piece and the top has lost its chalky look; give it 30 seconds more, with no colour.
  5. Butter the hot disc, scatter the coconut over half, fold and eat at once.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Rubbing fat into flour 47 recipes

  • The fat is cold when it starts and still firm when the rubbing stops, and the bowl has not warmed under the hands.
  • Stopped early, while it still looks like coarse crumbs with pieces of fat visible in it, not worked on to a smooth even sand, which means the pieces have gone.
  • Nothing is greasy or glossy and nothing has smeared into a paste against the side of the bowl.
  • Liquid added last and only enough to bring it together: pressed, it just holds, with no wet patch and nothing sticking to the hand.
  • Rested cold before it is rolled or shaped, so the gluten relaxes and the fat firms again after being handled.

Where it goes wrong The fat was too warm, or it was rubbed until the mixture looked uniform. Once the fat has melted into the flour there are no solid pieces left to steam apart in the oven, so the bake is sandy or tough instead of flaky, and nothing done later puts those layers back.

Straining & passing through a sieve 148 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

From the pass

Nothing binds this but the starch itself: cassava granules gelatinise on dry contact heat and fuse into a sheet, so water content is the whole recipe. Too wet and the layer cements into a rubbery pancake; too dry and it stays powder and never knits. Sieving is the calibration step, and the disc must come off pale, because browning makes it brittle.
