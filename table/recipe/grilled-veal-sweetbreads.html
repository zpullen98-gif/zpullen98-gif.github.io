Grilled Veal Sweetbreads

  • Advanced
  • 45 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$$

bright · briny · gently hot · rich Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, a briny, maritime salinity, on a rich, rounded finish.

Ingredients

  • 600 g veal sweetbreads, heart lobes
  • 1 litre cold water with 20 g salt, for soaking
  • 1 carrot, 1 onion and 1 celery stick, roughly cut
  • 1 bay leaf and 6 black peppercorns
  • 150 ml dry white wine
  • 40 g unsalted butter
  • 20 ml olive oil
  • 25 g capers, rinsed
  • 1 lemon
  • 15 g flat parsley, chopped
  • Fine salt and black pepper

Equipment

Sheet pan

Allergen screen

Found: dairy · celery · alcohol

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Soak the sweetbreads in the salted cold water in the fridge, changing it four times over 4 hours, until they are pale and no longer leaking pink. 240 min
  2. Cover with fresh water, the wine, vegetables, bay and peppercorns, bring slowly up to 85C (185F), hold 4 minutes, then lift straight into ice water. 4 min
  3. While they are still warm, peel away the outer membrane, fat and ducts with your fingers, keeping each lobe whole.
  4. Press between two trays under a 1 kg weight in the fridge for 2 hours, until every lobe is an even 2 cm thick. 120 min
  5. Season, then sear in the butter and oil over high heat until deep brown and springy to the finger, about 3 minutes a side, and finish with capers, lemon and parsley. 3 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Blanching & shocking 65 recipes

  • A large volume at a hard rolling boil, and enough of it that adding the food barely interrupts it; a pot that goes quiet has started stewing rather than blanching.
  • Timed rather than watched, and short. This is an interruption in the cooking of something, not the cooking of it.
  • The ice bath was made and standing beside the pot BEFORE anything went into the water, not started once the timer went off.
  • It went straight into the ice water, fully submerged, and stayed until cold at the centre: a piece still warm inside is still cooking, and the arrest has not happened yet.
  • Drained and dried afterwards rather than left sitting in water, so it does not carry that water into whatever it meets next.

Where it goes wrong The ice bath was not ready, so the food sat draining in a colander while its own residual heat carried on cooking it. By the time it cooled it was a stage past where it was pulled, and exact arrest is the entire reason for blanching rather than simply boiling.

Searing: the hard crust 128 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Sweetbreads are glands wrapped in a membrane that shrinks hard in heat and buckles the lobe, so it comes off while the gland is warm and slack. The soak is not superstition: four changes of cold water pull the blood that would otherwise cook to grey. The 2 hour press matters just as much. Unpressed, the thick end is still raw when the thin end has gone to leather.
