Thai · Main

Gaeng Phet Ped Yang

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

rich · umami-deep · sweet-edged · herb-fresh Leads with richness that coats and comforts, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 400 g Chinese roast duck, boned and sliced 1 cm thick, skin on
  • 250 ml coconut cream
  • 250 ml coconut milk
  • 60 g red curry paste
  • 30 ml fish sauce
  • 20 g palm sugar
  • 150 g cherry tomatoes, halved
  • 150 g pineapple, in 2 cm pieces
  • 120 g lychees, peeled and stoned
  • 6 makrut lime leaves, torn
  • 2 red chillies, sliced on the angle
  • 15 g Thai basil leaves
  • 15 ml lime juice

Equipment

Sheet pan

Method

  1. Put the coconut cream alone in a wide pan over medium heat and stir it until the emulsion breaks and clear oil pools at the edges, 5 minutes to 8 minutes. 5 min
  2. Stir the curry paste into that split oil and fry it until the raw garlic and shrimp smell has gone and the paste smells sweet and toasted, 3 minutes to 4 minutes. 3 min
  3. Loosen with the coconut milk, season with the fish sauce and palm sugar, bring to a bare simmer, then add the tomatoes, pineapple, lychees and lime leaves and cook 4 minutes. 4 min
  4. Reheat the duck apart from the curry: spread the slices skin up on a tray and give them 8 minutes to 10 minutes at 180C (350F), until they read 74C (165F) and the skin has crisped again. 8 min
  5. Take the pan off the heat, stir in the basil, chillies and lime juice, then lay the hot duck on top with the skin proud of the sauce.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 23 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Building an emulsion 40 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

From the pass

Coconut cream is oil suspended in water. Heat and stirring break that suspension, the oil pools out, and it is the only thing in the pan that can actually fry the paste. Frying is a different reaction from boiling: it browns the aromatics and drives off the raw allium and shrimp notes that simmering leaves sitting there. The duck is reheated on a tray because two minutes over a bare simmer will not carry cold slices to 74C (165F), and skin left in the sauce slackens within a minute anyway.
