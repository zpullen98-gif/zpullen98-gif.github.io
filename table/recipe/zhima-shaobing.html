Northern Chinese · Bread

Zhima Shaobing

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

nutty-toasted · fiery · sweet-edged Leads with toasted, nutty warmth throughout, real fire that builds and stays, trailing gentle sweetness.

Ingredients

  • 400 g plain wheat flour, for the dough
  • 240 g water at 35C (95F)
  • 4 g instant yeast
  • 6 g salt
  • 10 g neutral oil
  • 90 g plain wheat flour, for the yousu
  • 70 g neutral oil, for the yousu
  • 4 g salt, for the yousu
  • 6 g ground huajiao (Sichuan pepper)
  • 3 g five spice powder
  • 40 g maltose, loosened with 40 g hot water
  • 120 g white sesame seeds

Equipment

Rolling pin

The skills inside

Proofing: the rise before the oven

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · sesame

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Mix the dough ingredients and knead 8 minutes, until smooth and able to stretch to a rough window, then cover and prove 60 minutes until doubled. 8 min
  2. Heat the 70 g of oil to 150C (300F) and pour it over the yousu flour, salt, huajiao and five spice, stirring to a loose paste with the texture of wet sand that holds a ribbon for a second before sinking.
  3. Roll the dough into a large thin rectangle, spread the yousu edge to edge, roll it into a tight log and cut it into 8, then stand each piece on its cut end, coil it and press it flat so the layers spiral inside.
  4. Brush the tops with the loosened maltose and press them face down into the sesame; the maltose should be tacky rather than runny, or the seeds slide straight off.
  5. Bake at 220C (430F) for 15 to 18 minutes, until the sesame is gold, clear seams open along the sides, and the bun feels surprisingly light for its size. 18 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Proofing: the rise before the oven 64 recipes

  • Judged on volume against the container rather than on the clock: visibly grown, and grown by roughly the amount the recipe describes.
  • The poke test agrees: a floured fingertip pressed a centimetre in leaves a dent that springs back slowly and only part of the way. Straight back means it is not ready; no spring at all means it has gone.
  • The surface is domed, smooth, and slightly sheened: not cratered, wrinkled, or collapsed, and not skinned over and dry from proofing uncovered.
  • Handled, it feels alive and airy: light for its size, with bubbles visible under the skin.
  • It springs in the oven: the piece lifts in the first minutes and the scores open. A shape that does not move at all was proofed past its point before it went in.

Where it goes wrong It was proofed to the clock in a kitchen colder or warmer than the recipe assumed: overproofed, it collapses as the blade touches it and bakes flat and dense with a sour edge; underproofed, it tears at the side and bakes tight.

From the pass

The yousu is what makes leaves rather than one loaf. Oil at 150C (300F) poured onto flour cooks the starch and coats each granule in fat, so the dough sheets cannot weld together and steam prises them apart in the oven. Use cold oil, or a paste thin enough to run, and it soaks into the dough and bakes as one dense brick. Maltose for the wash, not honey: it stays tacky enough to grip sesame and browns without scorching.
