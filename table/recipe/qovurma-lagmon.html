Qovurma Lagmon

  • Advanced
  • 65 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

umami-deep · bright · herb-fresh · earthy Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, acid holding everything up to the light, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients Method Check the result Pairings Your notes Videos

Ingredients

  • 500 g plain flour
  • 260 ml warm water
  • 10 g salt for the dough
  • 2 g bicarbonate of soda
  • 60 ml sunflower oil for coating the ropes
  • 350 g lamb leg, sliced thin across the grain
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 sweet peppers, in strips
  • 150 g celery or green beans, cut on the angle
  • 3 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • 4 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 60 g Chinese chives or spring onion, in 4 cm lengths
  • 2 tsp cumin seed, crushed
  • 1 tsp chilli flakes
  • 1.5 tbsp black or rice vinegar
  • 8 g salt for the sauce

Method

  1. Dissolve the salt and soda in the water, mix into the flour to a shaggy mass, knead until smooth, then rest covered 30 minutes and knead again for 2 minutes; the dough should now stretch a hand's width before tearing. 30 min
  2. Cut into 12 ropes, roll each pencil thick, coat heavily in oil and coil them in a tray under cling film for at least 1 hour, until a rope pulled between the fingers thins without any spring back. 60 min
  3. Pull each rope out between your hands, doubling it back and slapping the loop on the board, until the strands run about 3 mm; boil in heavily salted water 2 to 3 minutes until they float and squeak against the tooth, then rinse cold and toss with oil. 3 min
  4. Sear the lamb in a screaming wok until crusted, add the onion, peppers, celery and garlic and fry until the edges char, then the cumin, chilli, tomato paste and tomatoes.
  5. Cook until the paste darkens and red oil separates at the edge, then add the noodles, vinegar, salt and chives and toss hard until every strand is coated and some are catching brown on the metal.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Wok technique 28 recipes

  • Everything went in dry and went in in order. Wet protein or wet vegetables drop the wok below the heat that browns, and what follows is a stew in a round pan however hard the burner is working.
  • The wok stays loud from the first moment to the last. A wok that falls quiet has been overloaded, and the food in it is now boiling in its own liquid.
  • Every piece is coated and glossy with no pooled sauce in the bottom of the dish: the sauce should cling by reduction and starch, not sit under the food.
  • Vegetables keep their colour and a bite at the centre. Grey-green greens or a limp stem mean too long in the pan, and nothing later in the dish recovers them.
  • It carries the faint scorched note that only comes from food touching metal above about 200C, distinct from bitterness, which means the aromatics were burnt and is a different failure entirely.

Where it goes wrong Too much went in at once, or it went in wet. The wok loses its heat, the food releases water and simmers, the sauce thins instead of clinging, and the dish arrives pale and slack with liquid pooling under it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 56 recipes

  • The required cooking process has been verified with a probe. Any allowance for carryover follows the validated method; an assumed rise during resting never substitutes for a required safety step.
  • The sensing area reaches the thickest relevant part, away from bone, fat and gristle, and more than one spot is checked. Thin pieces are measured from the side when needed.
  • The rest matches the cut, recipe and required holding conditions. Temperature is checked rather than assuming a fixed rise or a universal resting time.
  • The slices have the intended texture and moisture. Juice on the board is an observation to compare across batches, not proof of a failed rest or of safe doneness.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong Slicing before the planned rest or along the grain can leave a less pleasant result. Check the measured cooking process, rest and cutting direction separately: a dry slice or a puddle alone does not identify the cause.

From the pass

Pulling works on relaxed gluten, so the long oiled rest is the recipe. Salt tightens the strand so it thins without snapping, and a pinch of soda raises the pH and keeps it springy in the water. If a rope fights back, return it to the oil for fifteen minutes rather than forcing it; forcing bruises the dough and the bruised spot cooks up thick and gummy.
