Jiangnan · Main

Hong Shao Rou

  • Intermediate
  • 35 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$$

umami-deep · rich · sweet-edged · aromatic Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, richness that coats and comforts, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 800 g skin-on pork belly, cut into 4 cm cubes
  • 40 g rock sugar (bing tang), crushed
  • 30 ml neutral oil
  • 60 ml Shaoxing huadiao wine
  • 30 ml light soy sauce
  • 15 ml dark soy sauce
  • 5 slices ginger
  • 3 spring onions, knotted
  • 2 star anise
  • 1 short piece cassia bark
  • 600 ml just-boiled water
  • 3 g salt

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · soy · alcohol

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Cover the belly with cold water, bring it up slowly with a slice of ginger and a splash of the wine, and lift the cubes out once the grey scum stops rising; drain and pat every face dry or the sugar will spit at you.
  2. Melt the rock sugar in the oil over a low flame and watch it go from clear syrup to weak tea to the colour of dark honey, about 90 seconds from the first colour; the moment it smells nutty, tip in the pork.
  3. Toss hard until every cube is lacquered mahogany and the fat has started to run clear at the edges.
  4. Splash the Shaoxing down the hot side of the pot, add both soys, the aromatics, the salt and just enough boiling water to cover, then hold at a bare tremble for 60 minutes until a chopstick passes through the skin with no resistance. 60 min
  5. Take the lid off, raise the heat and reduce, spooning the liquid back over the cubes, until it hangs on them in a glossy sheet and the oil runs clear red round the rim.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Sugar stages & caramel 41 recipes

  • Nothing is stirred once it is boiling, and any crystals clinging to the side of the pan are brushed down rather than left to fall in.
  • Judged by temperature or by a tested stage rather than by time: consecutive stages are a few degrees apart and look identical in the pan.
  • Colour is read off the heat and in good light, remembering that sugar keeps darkening after the flame is out.
  • Stopped decisively at the point wanted: off the heat and into the next liquid or onto a cold surface, rather than left to coast in a hot pan.
  • The result is clear and smooth rather than grainy, cloudy, or seized into a solid mass.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred after coming to the boil, or a stray crystal from the side of the pan fell back in. One crystal seeds the whole pan and the syrup turns grainy within seconds, and once sugar has crystallised the only cure is more water and starting the whole thing again.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 78 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Tang se is caramel, not soy sauce. Rock sugar melts near 185C (365F) and browns, and that browning gives the red plus a faint bitterness that keeps the dish off pure syrup. Past roughly 195C it goes acrid and nothing added later covers it. Work by colour and smell with the pork already at hand: tipping it in is what stops the sugar cooking.
