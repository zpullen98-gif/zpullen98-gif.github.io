Sambal Terasi

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

aromatic · sweet-edged · fiery · umami-deep Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, a sweet edge rounding the corners, finishing long and savory.

Ingredients

  • 10 red chilies + 5 bird's eye (adjust courage accordingly)
  • 5 shallots, 3 garlic cloves
  • 1 tsp terasi (fermented shrimp paste), toasted in foil until it announces itself to the neighborhood
  • 1 tomato; 1 tbsp palm sugar; lime juice; salt
  • Oil for frying

Equipment

Blender · Mortar & pestle · Deep-fry pot

The skills inside

Pounding a curry pasteFermentationDry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nutsFrying the paste until the oil splits

Allergen screen

Found: shellfish

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Toast the terasi in foil in a dry pan: 2 min a side; it will smell alarming and taste essential. 2 min
  2. Fry chilies, shallots, garlic, tomato until soft and blistered.
  3. Pound everything in a stone mortar (ulekan) to a rough, glistening paste; a blender makes it too polite.
  4. Fry the paste in the same oil 5 min; season with palm sugar, salt, lime. Keeps a week; improves for three days. 5 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 19 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Fermentation 50 recipes

  • Held somewhere with a steady temperature it can actually work at, chosen on purpose rather than wherever there happened to be room.
  • There is visible activity before it is called started: bubbles, rise, or movement in the liquid. A ferment showing nothing after its first hours is not slow, it is not going.
  • It smells clean: sour, yeasty, or alcoholic, with nothing putrid, and with nothing fuzzy or coloured growing where air meets it.
  • Everything is under the liquid or under a cover, as the method requires: nothing solid left sitting dry in the air to spoil while the rest works.
  • Judged by taste and behaviour and then STOPPED: chilled, baked, or refrigerated at the point wanted, rather than left running because the stated hours had not elapsed.

Where it goes wrong It was run on the clock in a kitchen colder or warmer than the recipe assumed. Fermentation is a rate and not a duration, so the same number of hours gives an under-developed ferment in a cold room and a slack, over-soured one in a warm one, and only tasting it would have said which.

From the pass

Indonesia runs on sambal: hundreds of varieties, and terasi is the backbone of the classic. The mortar matters: pounding ruptures cells and marries oils in a way blades cannot. A meal without sambal, say the Javanese, is a conversation without warmth.
