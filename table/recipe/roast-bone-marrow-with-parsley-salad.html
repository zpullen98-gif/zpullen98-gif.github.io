Roast Bone Marrow with Parsley Salad

  • Easy
  • 15 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

rich · herb-fresh · briny · aromatic Leads with richness that coats and comforts, fresh herbs arguing happily on top, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 4 beef marrow bones, centre cut, 7 cm long, split lengthways
  • 2 litres cold water with 40 g salt, for soaking
  • 30 g flat parsley leaves, picked
  • 40 g shallot, sliced paper thin
  • 20 g capers, rinsed
  • 15 ml lemon juice
  • 20 ml extra virgin olive oil
  • 4 thick slices sourdough
  • Flaky salt

Equipment

Sheet pan

Method

  1. Soak the bones in the salted iced water in the fridge for 12 to 24 hours, changing the water twice, until the marrow is pale ivory rather than red. 600 min
  2. Drain and pat dry, sit them cut side up on a tray and roast at 220C (425F) for 15 to 20 minutes. 20 min
  3. Pull them the moment the marrow is glossy, loose and translucent but still holding its shape, when a skewer slides through with no cold core.
  4. Dress the parsley, shallot and capers with the lemon, oil and a pinch of salt only as the bones come out, so the leaves stay crisp.
  5. Toast the bread, scrape the marrow onto it while it is hot, and eat with the salad and flaky salt.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Roasting in a hot oven 56 recipes

  • The oven was fully up to heat before anything went in, given time beyond the indicator light rather than judged by it.
  • Every piece has air around it. Nothing touches its neighbour and nothing is packed to the edge of the tray, because crowded food steams itself in its own moisture and never browns.
  • Colour is even across the whole tray, the tray having been turned at least once: no pale row at the back and no dark row at the front.
  • It came out BEFORE the target temperature, not at it. Carryover keeps driving the inside up by several degrees after it leaves the oven, and that rise is part of the cooking rather than an error in it.
  • The temperature was read at the coldest part rather than the easiest one to reach, and read again after the rest.

Where it goes wrong The tray was crowded, or the oven was not truly up to heat. The food releases steam faster than a cool box can carry it away, so it sits pale and slack, and the extra minutes spent chasing colour take the inside well past where it should have stopped.

Soaking dried goods back to life 69 recipes

  • Fully submerged with room to swell: the level is well above everything at the start and still above it at the end.
  • Given the full time, and judged by what is in the bowl rather than by the hours on the clock.
  • Tested at the CENTRE of the largest piece, cut or pressed. Anything with a hard dry core will still have one after cooking, because the outside softens first and stops the water getting further in.
  • The soaking water was dealt with on purpose: kept because it holds flavour, or thrown away because it holds what should not be eaten, but never simply left undecided.
  • Drained before it is used, so the dish does not silently gain all the water that came with it.

Where it goes wrong Too little water, or too little time. The pieces swell until they are packed tight against each other and the ones in the middle never fully take up water; they will not soften later in the dish either, so the finished plate has hard pieces scattered through it.

From the pass

Marrow is close to 90 percent fat and starts flowing near 40C (104F), so the oven is a race rather than a rest. Fifteen to twenty minutes at 220C (425F) leaves it translucent and wobbling; five minutes more and it has run out of the bone into the tray, leaving an empty tube. The overnight salted soak draws out blood that would otherwise roast grey and taste of iron.
