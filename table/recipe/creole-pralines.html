New Orleans · Dessert

Creole Pralines

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

rich · sweet-edged · floral · nutty-toasted Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a sweet edge rounding the corners, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 200g white sugar + 200g light brown sugar
  • 120ml evaporated milk (the Creole signature) + 60g butter
  • 200g pecan halves, lightly toasted
  • 1 tsp vanilla; pinch of salt
  • A candy thermometer and a sheet of parchment ready BEFORE you start: pralines wait for no one

Equipment

Thermometer · Parchment

The skills inside

Allergen screen

Found: dairy · nuts

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Boil sugars and milk, stirring, to soft-ball stage, 114–116°C; butter and pecans in.
  2. Off heat: rest 2 min, then STIR with purpose 2–3 min as it turns from glossy to matte and thickens: this is the crystallization you are conducting. 2 min
  3. Vanilla and salt; drop in quick spoonfuls onto parchment: they set as you work, so move like you mean it.
  4. Cool 30 min to a creamy, fudge-adjacent set with a gentle sparkle. Store airtight, humidity permitting, and in this town, humidity rarely permits. 30 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Sugar stages & caramel 15 recipes

  • Nothing is stirred once it is boiling, and any crystals clinging to the side of the pan are brushed down rather than left to fall in.
  • Judged by temperature or by a tested stage rather than by time: consecutive stages are a few degrees apart and look identical in the pan.
  • Colour is read off the heat and in good light, remembering that sugar keeps darkening after the flame is out.
  • Stopped decisively at the point wanted: off the heat and into the next liquid or onto a cold surface, rather than left to coast in a hot pan.
  • The result is clear and smooth rather than grainy, cloudy, or seized into a solid mass.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred after coming to the boil, or a stray crystal from the side of the pan fell back in. One crystal seeds the whole pan and the syrup turns grainy within seconds, and once sugar has crystallised the only cure is more water and starting the whole thing again.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 17 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

From the pass

French pralines were almonds in hard caramel; New Orleans rewrote them with pecans and cream into something softer and entirely its own, sold by the praline ladies of the Quarter whose recipes built businesses. The matte-turn is the moment of truth: stop stirring too early and they never set, too late and they set in the pot. Humid days genuinely sabotage them; even the candy here consults the weather.
