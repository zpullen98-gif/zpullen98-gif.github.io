Maine Lobster Stew

  • Intermediate
  • 45 min
  • $$$$ roughly 4 of 4 for cost

rich Leads with richness that coats and comforts.

Ingredients

  • Meat from 2 lobsters, cut in chunks, shells reserved
  • 115g butter
  • 500ml whole milk
  • 250ml heavy cream
  • 1 small onion, minced
  • Sweet paprika
  • Salt and white pepper

Method

  1. Melt the butter over low heat and sweat the onion until soft without color.
  2. Add the lobster meat and cook gently 5 min so the butter turns coral: this is the color of the stew. 5 min
  3. Warm the milk and cream separately, then add slowly to the lobster, stirring.
  4. Heat until steaming but never boiling; boiling toughens the meat and can break the milk.
  5. Season with salt, white pepper, and a dusting of paprika. Rest overnight in the fridge if you can.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

The overnight rest: letting a dish marry 29 recipes

  • Cooled quickly before it went in (spread out, decanted, or stood in cold water) rather than carried to the fridge hot in the pot it was cooked in.
  • Protected from the rest of the fridge once cold: covered, or given space where nothing strong is sitting beside it. A rest this long is long enough to take on whatever it shares the shelf with, and some dishes want the open air.
  • Given the whole time. This is the one technique whose entire content is waiting, and cutting it short leaves the dish exactly as it was when it went in.
  • Brought back deliberately: reheated gently, or brought up to the temperature it is meant to be eaten at, rather than served straight out of the cold because it is late.
  • Tasted and corrected AFTER the rest. Seasoning and brightness both flatten in the cold, so the dish is finished at the end of the wait rather than at the start of it.

Where it goes wrong It went into the fridge hot and covered. It held its own heat for hours in the range where food spoils, and the lid sweated and dripped back into it all night: what comes out is duller and wetter than what went in, which is the exact opposite of the point.

The bare simmer: holding liquid below the boil 183 recipes

  • The surface is read rather than assumed: bubbles break slowly in one or two places, or the surface merely shivers, and it does not reach a rolling boil at any point.
  • It was held there: the pot was watched and the heat adjusted rather than set once and left, and there is no tide mark up the pot wall from a boil-over.
  • The liquid stayed as clear as it should be: no clouding from agitation, and no fat churned into an emulsion through it.
  • Whatever is in the liquid is still intact: nothing shredded, broken up, or worried apart by movement.
  • The level is roughly where the elapsed time should have left it; a large drop is the evidence that it ran hotter than this while nobody was looking.

Where it goes wrong It was brought to a boil and then left on the same heat, so it churned for the whole cook: the liquid clouded, the fat emulsified into it, and the solids broke apart into the broth.

From the pass

The old Maine rule is that lobster stew must age a day before serving: the butter and lobster essence marry into something the fresh pot cannot match, and the overnight rest is written into every coastal community cookbook since the 1800s. Cook the lobster meat gently in butter first; that infused butter is the stew's entire flavor engine. Milk and cream, salt, patience: nothing else. A stew this plain has nowhere to hide, which is exactly the Down East point.
