Tamales Rojos

  • Advanced
  • 4 h
  • Serves 4
  • $$

rich · gently hot · earthy · aromatic Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a warm current of chile, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 500g masa harina for tamales (coarser grind) beaten with 200g lard or softened butter, 1 tsp baking powder, salt, and ~400ml warm broth, until a spoonful of masa FLOATS in water; that is the test
  • Filling: 600g braised pork or chicken, shredded into salsa roja (guajillo + ancho chiles, garlic, cumin, blended and simmered)
  • 30 dried corn husks, soaked pliable in hot water
  • A steamer pot with coins in the bottom water, the old rattle alarm
  • Patience and, traditionally, several relatives

Allergen screen

Found: dairy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Beat the masa with the fat until genuinely fluffy: the float test is pass/fail and non-negotiable; a sinking masa steams into a brick.
  2. Spread masa on the smooth side of each husk in a thin rectangle; a stripe of the red-sauced meat down the center.
  3. Fold sides over, fold the tail up; stack UPRIGHT, open ends up, packed snugly in the steamer.
  4. Steam 75–90 min. The coins rattle while there is water; silence means refill. Done when the tamal releases cleanly from the husk. Rest 15 min; they set as they stand. 90 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Working with masa 12 recipes

  • Pinch a small piece and roll it closed between finger and thumb: hydrated correctly the edge stays smooth and seals over on itself; a fringe of fine cracks opening along the pinched edge is under hydration, and every piece off that batch will split at the rim. In the hand it feels soft and yielding and leaves a matt film on the palm, never a wet skin that strings when the hand lifts away.
  • After the rest it is damp and matt right through, and it has been kept covered the whole time: a pale dried skin over the mass, or grit that grinds under the thumb when a ball is squashed flat, means it stood open to the air or was shaped before the moisture had reached the centre of the ground grain.
  • It lifts off the press as one clean piece, the rim unbroken and the thickness even from side to side at roughly 2 mm, and a hand shaped piece is judged the same way, with no thin translucent windows worn through the middle. A lacy scalloped rim, or a piece that stretches and tears as it is lifted, fails the mark.
  • Held to the face it smells of toasted sweet corn, faintly mineral and clean. A sharp chalky or soapy smell of raw lime, or a bitterness sitting at the back of the tongue when a raw pinch is tasted, means it was under rinsed and no amount of heat afterwards will drive it off.
  • On a surface holding 190 to 200 C (375 to 390 F) it lifts and balloons across most of its face on the last turn, then settles soft and pliable as it cools. Rested a minute and folded in half, it creases without splitting: a piece that never lifts, or that cracks white along the fold, is failed however good its colour.

Where it goes wrong Too little moisture worked in, and no rest to let what there is reach the centre of the ground grain: it handles well while it is being mixed, then tightens as it stands, so the pieces come off the press with cracked scalloped rims, sit flat and pale on the heat and never lift, and go stiff within a minute of coming off, breaking along the crease instead of folding.

Braising 35 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

From the pass

The tamalada, the assembly-line family tamale day, is the real recipe: one person spreads, one fills, one folds, everyone talks. Masa that floats is aerated enough to steam light; the coin-in-the-pot alarm is grandmother engineering; and the husk releasing cleanly is the only doneness test that matters. Tamales freeze beautifully, which is why no one has ever made a small batch.
