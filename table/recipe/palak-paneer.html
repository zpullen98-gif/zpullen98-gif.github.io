Palak Paneer

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian

rich · aromatic · gently hot · silky Leads with richness that coats and comforts, aromatics blooming from the first bite, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 500g spinach, blanched 60 sec and shocked
  • 250g paneer, cubed, briefly pan-fried
  • 1 onion, 2 tomatoes
  • Ginger, garlic, green chili
  • 1 tsp each cumin and garam masala
  • Cream, ghee, kasuri methi

The skills inside

Allergen screen

Found: dairy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Blend the shocked spinach coarsely: it should stay emerald.
  2. Fry onion, then ginger-garlic-chili, then tomato until the fat rises.
  3. Add spinach; simmer only 5 min to keep the color. 5 min
  4. Fold in paneer, cream, methi; done.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Sautéing & the shallow fry 34 recipes

  • Everything sits in a single layer with visible space between the pieces: nothing stacked, nothing touching, or the pan is too small for the quantity.
  • The pieces move freely and keep moving, sliding across the pan rather than sticking and tearing when the pan is worked.
  • Light even browning arrived at quickly: colour on most faces, not the deep crust of a sear and not grey.
  • A thin film of fat with the food glossed by it: no pool left in the pan at the end, and nothing greasy on the fingers or the plate.
  • Cooked through but still firm, having spent only a few minutes in the pan: anything that has gone soft has been in there too long.

Where it goes wrong Too much went in at once, the pan cooled, and the food released its water: what was meant to brown in three minutes stews for ten and comes out grey, slack and wet.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 38 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Blanch-and-shock keeps chlorophyll bright: sixty seconds in boiling water, straight into ice, and the purée stays emerald; long-simmered spinach turns army green because heat converts chlorophyll's magnesium out of the molecule. So the spinach joins the masala for only five minutes, at the end. Pan-frying the paneer first gives it a skin that survives the sauce. The cream and kasuri methi finish is what moves it from vegetable purée to Punjabi Sunday lunch.
