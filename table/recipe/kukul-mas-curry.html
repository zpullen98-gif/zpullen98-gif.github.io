Kukul Mas Curry

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

warm-spiced · aromatic · rich · earthy Leads with warm spice humming through the middle, aromatics blooming from the first bite, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 1 kg bone in chicken thighs and drumsticks, skin removed
  • 1 large red onion, 150 g, sliced
  • 400 ml thin coconut milk
  • 150 ml thick coconut milk
  • 2 tbsp roasted Sri Lankan curry powder
  • 2 tbsp coconut oil
  • 3 garlic cloves and 3 cm ginger, pounded to a paste
  • 1 tsp chilli powder
  • 1 tsp ground turmeric
  • 1 tsp coarsely ground black pepper
  • 1 sprig curry leaves
  • 10 cm pandan leaf
  • 5 cm Ceylon cinnamon quill
  • 4 cardamom pods, bruised
  • 3 cloves
  • 2 tsp salt
  • half a lime

Equipment

Mortar & pestle

Method

  1. Rub the chicken with the roasted curry powder, chilli, turmeric, pepper, garlic ginger paste and salt, and leave it while you slice the aromatics.
  2. Heat the coconut oil and fry the onion, curry leaves, pandan, cinnamon, cardamom and cloves until the onion edges brown and the leaves smell of toasted spice.
  3. Add the chicken and turn it until the rub darkens, stops looking dusty and clings to the meat, then pour in the thin coconut milk.
  4. Simmer uncovered, turning the pieces occasionally, until the meat pulls back from the drumstick bone and the gravy has reduced by about a third and gone glossy.
  5. Off the heat, stir in the thick coconut milk and the lime, then return it to the stove only until steam rises.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 22 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 124 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

Roasted curry powder is already at the far edge of toasting: coriander, cumin and fennel taken to the colour of dark chocolate before grinding. Bloom it in hot oil the way you would a raw masala and it goes past toast into ash, acrid and unrecoverable. Rub it onto the wet chicken instead so it meets liquid, not bare fat. Thick milk last, below a simmer.
