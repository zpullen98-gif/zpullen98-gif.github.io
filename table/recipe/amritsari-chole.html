Amritsari Chole

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

aromatic · bright · rich · earthy Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, acid holding everything up to the light, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 300 g kabuli chana (chickpeas)
  • 2 g bicarbonate of soda
  • 8 g loose Assam tea, tied in muslin
  • 2 black cardamom, a 5 cm piece of cassia, 4 cloves and 2 bay leaves
  • 60 g ghee
  • 250 g onions, sliced thin
  • 300 g ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 25 g ginger, half pounded to a paste and half in julienne
  • 20 g garlic, pounded to a paste
  • 6 g anardana (dried pomegranate seeds), ground
  • 3 g amchur
  • 6 g ground coriander
  • 3 g deghi mirch
  • 2 g kala namak (black salt)
  • 3 g cumin seeds, dry roasted and coarsely ground
  • 10 g salt
  • 3 green chillies, slit, and 1 lemon

Method

  1. Soak the chana 8 hours in 1.5 litres of cold water with the bicarbonate: they are ready when each has doubled and splits cleanly instead of snapping. 480 min
  2. Boil them in fresh water with the muslin bag of tea, the whole spices and 6 g salt for 45 minutes, until a chana crushes to a smooth paste against the roof of the mouth and the skins have stained near black; lift out the bag and the whole spices. 45 min
  3. Brown the onions in the ghee over medium heat for 15 minutes, until they slump, lose their edges and go the colour of an old penny, then add the ginger and garlic paste and cook until the raw garlic smell turns sweet. 15 min
  4. Take the pan off the flame before you add the ground coriander, deghi mirch, anardana and amchur, slacken them with a spoonful of the black cooking liquor, and set the pan back on.
  5. Add the tomatoes and bhuna 12 minutes until the mass pulls from the sides in one piece and dark oil stands at the rim, then fold in the chana with 300 ml of their liquor and simmer 15 minutes until the gravy clings. 12 min
  6. Mash a ladleful of chana against the side to thicken it, then finish with the kala namak, roasted cumin, ginger julienne, slit chillies and lemon.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 23 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 70 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

From the pass

Bicarbonate raises the water's pH, weakening the pectin between cell walls so the chana turns creamy rather than mealy; go much past 2 g per 300 g and it saponifies the skins into slime with a soapy taste. Whole spices can sit in the boil for 45 minutes and only sweeten. Ground spice has far more surface area and scorches bitter in hot fat, so it goes in off the flame, wetted.
