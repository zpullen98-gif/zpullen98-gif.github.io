Nihari

  • Advanced
  • 5 h 30 m
  • Serves 4
  • $$

aromatic · rich · warm-spiced · gently hot Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, richness that coats and comforts, with warmth in the tail.

Ingredients

  • 1.2kg beef shank with marrow bones (the marrow is the finish), or the traditional slow lamb shank
  • Nihari masala: fennel, cumin, coriander, black cardamom, cloves, cinnamon, black pepper, dried ginger, a little nutmeg and mace, toasted and ground (or a trusted boxed masala, widely used and unashamed)
  • 2 onions, fried to deep brown; garlic-ginger paste, generous
  • 100g ghee; 4 tbsp atta (wheat flour) slurried in water: the silken thickener
  • The garnish court: julienned fresh ginger, green chilies, cilantro, lemon wedges, crisp fried onions; naan for the drag

Method

  1. Fry the onions to mahogany in ghee; add garlic-ginger, then the meat, searing; masala in, bloomed 2 min. 2 min
  2. Water to cover generously; the lowest possible simmer, lid on, 4–6 hours (overnight in tradition) until the shank collapses and the marrow loosens. 360 min
  3. Whisk in the atta slurry; simmer 20 min uncovered: the broth turns glossy, thick and clinging, its signature body. 20 min
  4. Rest; skim some of the spiced fat and pour it BACK over the top (the tarka crown). Serve in shallow bowls buried in ginger, chili, cilantro and lemon, naan in hand.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 17 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

Knife cuts: dice, julienne, bias 49 recipes

  • Take any three pieces from anywhere in the pile: they match closely enough in every dimension that you would have to measure them to argue otherwise.
  • The cut faces are flat and clean rather than ragged or crushed; leaves and herbs are cut through, not bruised, and the board carries no dark smear where they were.
  • The stated size is the actual size, held against a rule or a known reference rather than called about right.
  • Everything in the pile is the shape that was asked for: no wedges, no half pieces, and no offcuts swept in to avoid waste.
  • They cook evenly, which is the entire point: at the end of cooking nothing is still raw and nothing has collapsed.

Where it goes wrong The knife was dull, or the vegetable was never squared off first, so the pieces vary: the small ones are mush by the time the large ones are cooked, and the dish has two textures where it wanted one.

From the pass

Nihari comes from 'nihar' (morning) because Delhi and Lucknow's old cooks simmered it overnight for laborers' dawn breakfast; Karachi later claimed it so completely that Pakistan considers it a national dish. The atta slurry is the identity: a wheat-silk body no cornstarch replicates. And the old shops seed each day's pot with a ladle of yesterday's (the taar): some claim their lineage is unbroken for generations.
