Lam Potjiekos

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

rich · charred · umami-deep · aromatic Leads with richness that coats and comforts, char used as punctuation, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 kg lamb neck and shank on the bone, in thick pieces
  • 45 ml sunflower oil
  • 300 g onions, thickly sliced
  • 6 garlic cloves, bruised
  • 250 ml dry red wine
  • 200 ml water
  • 30 g tomato paste
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 4 sprigs thyme
  • 1 tsp cracked black peppercorns
  • 12 g salt
  • 500 g baby potatoes, left whole
  • 400 g carrots, in thumb lengths
  • 400 g butternut, in large chunks
  • 200 g green beans, topped

Method

  1. Set the three legged pot over a bed of coals and brown the lamb in the oil in batches until every face is properly dark; crowding steams it, so give each piece room, then lift it all out.
  2. Soften the onions and garlic in the same fat until slack and browning at the edges, return the meat, and add wine, water, tomato paste, bay, thyme, pepper and half the salt.
  3. Lid on, rake the coals down to a low glow you can hold a hand over for six seconds, and braise untouched until the neck meat gives when pushed with a spoon, about 2 hours; the lid should mutter, never rattle. 120 min
  4. Now layer, and do not stir: potatoes straight onto the meat, then carrots, then butternut, then the green beans on top. Lid back on.
  5. Cook until a skewer slides through the butternut without resistance and the beans are tender but still dark green, 45 to 60 minutes, then lift the pot off, tilt it and spoon gravy from the bottom over the top; serve in layers rather than mixed. 60 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 39 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

Grilling over live coals 77 recipes

  • The fire was ready before the food was: coals evenly covered in grey ash with no flames licking through and no raw smoke coming off, and a hot zone and a cooler one both available.
  • The grate was hot and clean when the food landed, and the food released cleanly from it when the time came to turn rather than tearing away.
  • Marks are browned, not burnt: deep colour where the metal touched, with no carbon flaking off and no soot transferred onto the food.
  • The interior reaches its target with a thin gradient, not charred outside and cold within, and not driven through by a flare-up.
  • What comes off the fire smells of fire and food only: no raw wood smoke, no fuel, nothing acrid at the back of the nose.

Where it goes wrong It went on over live flames instead of settled coals, so the outside charred bitter in the flare-ups while the middle stayed cold, and everything on the plate tastes of soot.

From the pass

A potjie is a stacked steamer, not a stew. Only the meat sits in liquid; everything above it cooks in the steam rising off that liquid, which is why the vegetables go in by density and why lifting the lid to stir is the single thing that ruins the pot. Stir and they drop into the gravy, boil, shed their starch and go to mash. Keep the coals at a mutter; a hard boil does exactly the same damage from below.
