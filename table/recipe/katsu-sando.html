Katsu Sando

  • Intermediate
  • 45 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost

umami-deep · briny Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, a briny, maritime salinity.

Ingredients

  • 2 thick pork loin chops (2.5cm), fat cap scored, pounded slightly even
  • Flour, 1 beaten egg, PANKO: the coarse Japanese crumb is the architecture; fine crumbs need not apply
  • 4 slices shokupan (Japanese milk bread, see The Bakery), crusts OFF, unapologetically soft
  • Tonkatsu sauce (or: ketchup + Worcestershire + oyster sauce + a little sugar, whisked); finely shredded cabbage, optional but classic
  • Hot mustard, a thin swipe; oil for frying

Deep-fry pot

Method

  1. Season the chops; flour, egg, panko. Press the panko on so it stands up shaggy.
  2. Fry at 170°C, 5–6 min, to deep gold and just-blushing at the center; rest 3 min on a rack; the crumbs must stay vertical. 6 min
  3. Paint both cut faces of the katsu with sauce; mustard on the bread; cabbage down if using.
  4. Sandwich; press GENTLY under a plate 2 min; trim the edges square with a sharp knife and cut into neat thirds: the geometry is the presentation. 2 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Scoring 28 recipes

  • Lift one cut open with a fingertip: it parts through the skin and stops in the fat beneath, showing a pale wall at its base. Any cut showing red or fibrous flesh has gone too deep; any that has not reached the fat has left the skin tethered, and it will tighten under heat.
  • The lines run parallel at even spacing, roughly a finger's width apart, each carried the full length of the face and stopped the same margin short of the edge. Sight down them: lines that converge, wander or crowd at one end cook at different rates and read as careless on the plate.
  • Each line is one clean draw of the blade held at a slant, not a sawn or doubled track, and it leaves a bevelled lip standing slightly proud rather than a vertical slot. The surface should be dry and cold, near 4C (39F), when it is cut; worked warm and slack it drags, and the blade tears where it should part.
  • After cooking the cuts have opened and set, each a clear gap with firm browned walls. Lines closed back to faint scratches were cut too shallow or too straight down, and the surface has shrunk over them instead of through them.
  • The piece lies flat and true when it is set down, with no cupping through the middle and no lifted, curled edge. A bowed piece means the tightening surface was never released, so it pulled the whole thing up and left the underside pale where it stood off the heat.

Where it goes wrong The blade was pushed rather than drawn, through a surface left warm and slack, so it went past the fat and into the flesh. The cuts bleed as they cook and the juices flood the surface, so it steams instead of crisping: the lines gape into wet ragged trenches, the flesh between them dries into hard ridges, and the skin stays leathery and pale where it should have blistered and set.

The coat before the fry: dredge, crumb, batter 80 recipes

  • The coat is continuous before it goes anywhere near the fat: held up to the light, no bare patch of food shows through anywhere, seams and thin edges included.
  • It is bonded rather than resting on top: a gentle shake sheds loose dust only, and the coat does not slide off as a sheet when the piece is handled.
  • It bubbles hard and steadily from the moment it enters the fat, and the bubbling visibly slows as it finishes; those bubbles are the surface water leaving, and a quiet entry means the fat is too cool.
  • Out and drained, it is an even gold-brown all over and it is still crisp several minutes later on a rack; a coat that softens within a minute of coming out never lost its water.
  • Broken open, there is no paste of raw flour under the crust and no hollow gap where the coat has lifted away from the food.

Where it goes wrong The fat was below temperature or the pan was crowded, so the coat drank fat instead of sealing against it: it comes out pale and heavy, greasy to the fingers, and slumps off the food as it cools.

From the pass

The katsu sando is a study in engineered contrast: shatteringly crisp panko against cloud-soft crustless milk bread, sweet-tangy sauce mediating. The edge-trim is not vanity: konbini and depachika counters sell them in perfect windowed boxes, and the clean cross-section is the genre's signature. Rest the katsu before building or the steam will sog the crumb from inside; the three-minute wait is the whole difference.
