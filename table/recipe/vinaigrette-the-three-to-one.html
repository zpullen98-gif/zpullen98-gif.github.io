Garde Manger · Sauce

Vinaigrette, the Three to One

  • Easy
  • 10 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

bright · aromatic · rich · briny Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, aromatics blooming from the first bite, ending saline and clean.

Ingredients

  • 60 g red wine vinegar, 6 to 7 percent acidity
  • 180 g extra virgin olive oil (3 parts oil to 1 part acid by weight)
  • 15 g Dijon mustard
  • 20 g shallot, minced to 2 mm
  • 4 g fine salt, about 1.4 percent of the finished sauce
  • 1 g black pepper, cracked

Method

  1. Steep the shallot in the vinegar with the salt for 10 minutes, until every crystal has gone and the shallot has turned translucent at the cut edges; salt will not dissolve once the oil is in. 10 min
  2. Whisk in the mustard until the acid phase is smooth and opaque, the colour of thin cream, with no seed flecks sitting apart from the liquid.
  3. Add the oil in a thread no thicker than a pencil lead, whisking hard, until the sauce turns pale, holds the whisk marks for a beat and coats the back of a spoon without running clear.
  4. Taste on a leaf rather than a spoon: dip a lettuce leaf and adjust, until the vinegar reads as brightness across the tongue and not a sting at the back of the throat.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Building an emulsion 40 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Knife cuts: dice, julienne, bias 150 recipes

  • Take any three pieces from anywhere in the pile: they match closely enough in every dimension that you would have to measure them to argue otherwise.
  • The cut faces are flat and clean rather than ragged or crushed; leaves and herbs are cut through, not bruised, and the board carries no dark smear where they were.
  • The stated size is the actual size, held against a rule or a known reference rather than called about right.
  • Everything in the pile is the shape that was asked for: no wedges, no half pieces, and no offcuts swept in to avoid waste.
  • They cook evenly, which is the entire point: at the end of cooking nothing is still raw and nothing has collapsed.

Where it goes wrong The knife was dull, or the vegetable was never squared off first, so the pieces vary: the small ones are mush by the time the large ones are cooked, and the dish has two textures where it wanted one.

From the pass

Three to one is calibrated to a 6 to 7 percent wine vinegar, so the ratio moves with the acid: an 8 percent sherry vinegar wants 4 to 1, lemon juice at roughly 5 percent acid wants 2.5 to 1. Taste the acid before you set the ratio. Mustard is the stabiliser; its mucilage coats the oil droplets and keeps them from touching. Pour the oil in a thread, because oil dumped in cannot be sheared fine enough and the sauce slicks instead of thickening.
