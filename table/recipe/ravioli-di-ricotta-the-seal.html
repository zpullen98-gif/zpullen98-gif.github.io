Ravioli di Ricotta, the Seal

  • Advanced
  • 60 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · warm-spiced Leads with richness that coats and comforts, warm spice humming through the middle.

Ingredients

  • 300 g 00 flour (100%)
  • 2 whole eggs and 6 yolks, 218 g total
  • 5 g fine sea salt for the dough
  • 500 g whole milk ricotta, drained 12 hours in a cloth to about 380 g
  • 80 g hard aged grating cheese, finely grated
  • 1 egg yolk, 18 g, for the filling
  • 2 g fine sea salt for the filling
  • 0.2 g grated nutmeg
  • semola rimacinata for the tray

Equipment

Piping bag · Sheet pan · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Make and rest the egg dough as for sfoglia, then beat the drained ricotta with the cheese, yolk, salt and nutmeg until it is stiff enough to hold a piped ridge without slumping.
  2. Roll one sheet to 0.8 mm and work fast; a sheet dried past tacky will not weld, and the parcels open in the pot.
  3. Pipe 8 g mounds at 4 cm centres, drape the second sheet over, and press outward from each mound with the sides of both hands, chasing every pocket of air to the edge before the border goes shut.
  4. Cut with a wheel, pinch each border once more between finger and thumb, and set the parcels on semola, never on flour.
  5. Cook at a bare simmer, 90C to 95C (194F to 203F), for 3 minutes; a rolling boil batters the seams open. 3 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

The bare simmer: holding liquid below the boil 183 recipes

  • The surface is read rather than assumed: bubbles break slowly in one or two places, or the surface merely shivers, and it does not reach a rolling boil at any point.
  • It was held there: the pot was watched and the heat adjusted rather than set once and left, and there is no tide mark up the pot wall from a boil-over.
  • The liquid stayed as clear as it should be: no clouding from agitation, and no fat churned into an emulsion through it.
  • Whatever is in the liquid is still intact: nothing shredded, broken up, or worried apart by movement.
  • The level is roughly where the elapsed time should have left it; a large drop is the evidence that it ran hotter than this while nobody was looking.

Where it goes wrong It was brought to a boil and then left on the same heat, so it churned for the whole cook: the liquid clouded, the fat emulsified into it, and the solids broke apart into the broth.

Salted water & the float test 188 recipes

  • The water was salted before anything went in, and tasting it confirms it: seasoned on the tongue, with the salt dissolved rather than sitting on the pot floor.
  • There is enough water, hot enough, that the pot returns to its boil quickly after the food goes in and never stops moving.
  • Where the dish uses the float as its signal, the signal is obeyed: the pieces rise and they come out then, not several minutes later once something else was finished.
  • Nothing is stuck to the bottom of the pot or welded to itself, and the water has not thickened into a cloudy sludge.
  • Drained on a bite rather than on the clock: resistance at the centre, with no chalky raw core.

Where it goes wrong The pot was too small and the water unsalted, so it never recovered its boil: the food sat in slack water, went gummy at the surface, and comes out bland in a way that no sauce afterwards can fix.

From the pass

Two things burst a raviolo. Trapped air expands as it heats and splits the weakest seam, and a wet filling steams and does the same. So the ricotta is drained overnight and bound with a yolk that sets near 65C (149F), and the air is chased outward before the border closes. Seal with dry hands on a still tacky sheet: brushed water makes a slippery layer, not a weld.
