Michigan Dried Cherry Salad

  • Easy
  • 20 min
  • Serves 4
  • $
  • Vegetarian

nutty-toasted · rich · earthy · bright Leads with toasted, nutty warmth throughout, richness that coats and comforts, with a clean, bright snap.

Ingredients

  • 200g mixed greens
  • 100g dried Michigan cherries
  • 100g walnuts, toasted
  • 100g blue cheese or goat cheese, crumbled
  • 1 apple, sliced thin
  • For the dressing: 3 tbsp cider vinegar, 6 tbsp oil, 1 tbsp maple syrup, 1 tsp Dijon, salt and pepper

Method

  1. Toast the walnuts until fragrant and cool them.
  2. Whisk the dressing ingredients into an emulsion.
  3. Toss the greens with just enough dressing to coat.
  4. Scatter the dried cherries, walnuts, cheese, and apple over.
  5. Serve at once, with extra dressing on the side.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Building an emulsion 14 recipes

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 17 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

From the pass

Traverse City's dried cherries are a year-round crop from the summer glut. The sweet-tart cherry against blue cheese and toasted walnut is a study in balance: dress the greens lightly so the toppings stay the stars.
