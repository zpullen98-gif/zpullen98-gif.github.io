Okinawa · Main

Sōki Soba

  • Advanced
  • 55 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

umami-deep · earthy · gently hot Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, an earthy, grounding base, with warmth in the tail.

Ingredients

  • 1.5kg pork neck bones
  • 10g kombu
  • 30g katsuobushi
  • 800g pork spare ribs, cut into 5cm lengths
  • 200ml awamori
  • 60g kokuto
  • 60ml koikuchi soy sauce
  • 600g Okinawa soba noodles (wheat, alkaline, no buckwheat)
  • 8g salt, or to taste
  • 4 slices kamaboko
  • 2 spring onions, sliced thin
  • beni shoga
  • koregusu (island chillies steeped in awamori), to serve

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · fish · soy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Cover the neck bones with cold water, bring to the boil, then throw the water away and scrub both bones and pot clean of grey scum. Refill with 3 litres cold water and the kombu, pull the kombu out just before it boils, and hold at a lazy simmer for 3 hours. 180 min
  2. Meanwhile simmer the ribs in the awamori with water to cover for 1 hour, then add the kokuto and soy and cook down 30 minutes more, turning them, until they are sticky and the meat has drawn back from the bone. 60 min
  3. Strain the pork broth, bring it to 80C (176F), cut the heat, drop in the katsuobushi and leave 3 minutes. Strain again through a cloth without pressing, and salt it to just past what tastes right. 3 min
  4. Boil the noodles in unsalted water until they are springy rather than soft, drain, and rinse briefly in warm water to wash the surface alkali off.
  5. Load the bowls with noodles, hot broth, ribs, kamaboko, spring onion and a pinch of beni shoga. Koregusu goes on at the table, drop by drop.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Straining & passing through a sieve 126 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

Salted water & the float test 180 recipes

  • The water was salted before anything went in, and tasting it confirms it: seasoned on the tongue, with the salt dissolved rather than sitting on the pot floor.
  • There is enough water, hot enough, that the pot returns to its boil quickly after the food goes in and never stops moving.
  • Where the dish uses the float as its signal, the signal is obeyed: the pieces rise and they come out then, not several minutes later once something else was finished.
  • Nothing is stuck to the bottom of the pot or welded to itself, and the water has not thickened into a cloudy sludge.
  • Drained on a bite rather than on the clock: resistance at the centre, with no chalky raw core.

Where it goes wrong The pot was too small and the water unsalted, so it never recovered its boil: the food sat in slack water, went gummy at the surface, and comes out bland in a way that no sauce afterwards can fix.

From the pass

Okinawa soba holds no buckwheat: it is wheat raised with alkaline water, and the high pH tightens the gluten and yellows the flour, which is why the strand stays springy sitting in hot broth. The broth is the other local marker, half pork and half katsuobushi rather than one or the other. Boil the bonito and you pull out bitterness and cloud; hold the pot near 80C (176F) for three minutes and you get the savour and nothing sour.
