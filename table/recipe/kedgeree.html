Kedgeree

  • Intermediate
  • 50 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

smoky · rich · herb-fresh · earthy Leads with wood-smoke running underneath, richness that coats and comforts, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 400g smoked haddock, poached 5 min in 400ml milk with a bay leaf, milk kept, per this guide's standing tradition
  • 250g basmati rice; 1 onion, sliced, cooked sweet in 50g butter
  • 2 tsp curry powder + ½ tsp turmeric, bloomed in the butter (the fifteen-second bloom, the Singapore-noodle law applies here too)
  • 3 boiled eggs, quartered, jammy, not chalky; big handful parsley + cilantro
  • Lemon wedges; a pot of tea; optionally a spoon of cream stirred through

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · dairy · egg · fish

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Poach and flake the haddock; cook the basmati in the strained poaching milk topped up with water: the rice absorbs the smoke.
  2. Melt the onion in butter; bloom the spices until the kitchen smells like the East India Company's ledger.
  3. Fold rice, fish and half the herbs through the spiced onion gently: large flakes intact, everything stained gold.
  4. Eggs on top, remaining herbs, lemon squeezed over. Serve warm, not piping: kedgeree is a breakfast that refuses to be rushed.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

The bare simmer: holding liquid below the boil 59 recipes

  • The surface is read rather than assumed: bubbles break slowly in one or two places, or the surface merely shivers, and it does not reach a rolling boil at any point.
  • It was held there: the pot was watched and the heat adjusted rather than set once and left, and there is no tide mark up the pot wall from a boil-over.
  • The liquid stayed as clear as it should be: no clouding from agitation, and no fat churned into an emulsion through it.
  • Whatever is in the liquid is still intact: nothing shredded, broken up, or worried apart by movement.
  • The level is roughly where the elapsed time should have left it; a large drop is the evidence that it ran hotter than this while nobody was looking.

Where it goes wrong It was brought to a boil and then left on the same heat, so it churned for the whole cook: the liquid clouded, the fat emulsified into it, and the solids broke apart into the broth.

Straining & passing through a sieve 69 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

From the pass

Khichri, India's rice-and-lentil comfort, sailed home with colonial officers, traded its lentils for smoked haddock and eggs, and settled onto the Victorian breakfast sideboard as kedgeree; the dish is empire history you can eat, with the smoked-milk-into-rice trick doing the same one-flavor-circulating work as the fish pie two recipes over. Anglo-Indian families kept the truest versions; the jammy egg and the spice bloom are the two details that separate revival from relic.
