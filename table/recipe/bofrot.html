Ghanaian · Dessert

Bofrot

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

warm-spiced Leads with warm spice humming through the middle.

Ingredients

  • 500 g plain flour
  • 100 g caster sugar
  • 7 g instant yeast
  • 1 tsp nutmeg, freshly grated
  • 1/2 tsp salt
  • 400 ml warm water
  • 1 egg
  • 1.5 litres groundnut oil, for frying

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Whisk the flour, sugar, yeast, nutmeg and salt together, then beat in the egg and warm water to a thick sticky batter that falls from the spoon in a slow ribbon.
  2. Cover and leave somewhere warm until it has doubled and domed, the surface pocked with bubbles that pop when you knock the side of the bowl.
  3. Heat the oil to 170C (340F); a scrap of batter should surface within three seconds and take a full minute to take colour.
  4. Squeeze the batter up between thumb and forefinger into a ball, scoop it off with a wet spoon straight into the oil, and fry until each one rolls over by itself and is an even deep amber all round; 5 to 6 minutes. 6 min
  5. Lift onto a rack rather than paper so the crust stays crisp, and eat within the hour.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Draining fried food: a rack, never paper 77 recipes

  • The rack was in place before the first thing went into the oil, not fetched afterwards while food waited in a spider over the pot.
  • It lands on a rack with air moving underneath it. Anything set down on paper or a flat plate traps its own steam against its base.
  • Salted within seconds of landing, while it is still hot enough to take seasoning.
  • A minute later the underside is still as crisp as the top: no soft flat patch where it has been sitting.
  • Nothing is stacked or piled while hot, however tempting it is to keep it together. A pile steams itself soft from the inside out.

Where it goes wrong It was drained on paper, or piled up to keep warm. Steam leaving the food has nowhere to go but back into the crust, so the underside softens within a minute: the crispness the frying just bought is gone before the plate reaches the table.

Proofing: the rise before the oven 78 recipes

  • Judged on volume against the container rather than on the clock: visibly grown, and grown by roughly the amount the recipe describes.
  • The poke test agrees: a floured fingertip pressed a centimetre in leaves a dent that springs back slowly and only part of the way. Straight back means it is not ready; no spring at all means it has gone.
  • The surface is domed, smooth, and slightly sheened: not cratered, wrinkled, or collapsed, and not skinned over and dry from proofing uncovered.
  • Handled, it feels alive and airy: light for its size, with bubbles visible under the skin.
  • It springs in the oven: the piece lifts in the first minutes and the scores open. A shape that does not move at all was proofed past its point before it went in.

Where it goes wrong It was proofed to the clock in a kitchen colder or warmer than the recipe assumed: overproofed, it collapses as the blade touches it and bakes flat and dense with a sour edge; underproofed, it tears at the side and bakes tight.

From the pass

This is a batter, not a dough: nothing is kneaded, there is no gluten network, and the lift is all yeast gas held in a loose starch matrix. Give it the full rise or the centres stay wet and heavy. Hold the oil at 170C (340F). Hotter and the crust browns while the middle is raw batter; cooler and the ball sits still, drinks oil and comes out greasy. A pale belt round the equator says it turned at the right moment.
