Emilia-Romagna · Starter

Erbazzone Reggiano

  • Intermediate
  • 45 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

rich · aromatic · umami-deep · herb-fresh Leads with richness that coats and comforts, aromatics blooming from the first bite, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients Method Check the result Pairings Your notes Videos

Ingredients

  • 300 g farina 00
  • 60 g strutto (rendered pork lard), cold
  • 130 ml warm water
  • 6 g fine salt
  • 1 kg Swiss chard, leaves and tender stalks
  • 80 g pancetta or lardo, in small dice
  • 1 small onion, sliced fine
  • 3 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 1 bunch flat parsley, chopped
  • 120 g Parmigiano Reggiano, grated
  • 1 whole egg
  • black pepper

Equipment

Sheet pan · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Rub the cold strutto into the flour and salt to a coarse crumb, add the water and work briefly to a smooth slack dough, then rest it 30 minutes so it will stretch thin without tearing. 30 min
  2. Blanch the chard 2 minutes, refresh it, and wring it in a cloth to a tight dark ball; chop it and season hard with salt and pepper. 2 min
  3. Fry half the pancetta with the onion, garlic and parsley until the fat runs clear and the garlic smells sweet, then fold it through the chard with the Parmigiano and egg.
  4. Roll two thin sheets, line an oiled tray with one, spread the filling 2 cm deep, cover with the second, seal the rim and prick the lid all over; scatter the remaining raw pancetta on top.
  5. Bake at 200C (400F) on the oven floor until the lid is blistered and crackling brown and the base lifts crisp off the tray, 30 to 35 minutes. 35 min

Blanch, cool, drain and finishIllustrated study · 4 numbered views

Follow slender green beans through a preparation for later service. Look beyond their green colour.

This is a prep-ahead example. An ice bath is optional when vegetables go straight to their next cooking step or to service. Timing and the desired bite depend on the vegetable, its size and the recipe.

One numbered view at a time. No grade or change to your learning record.

Whole illustration. Choose a view for a closer look.

  1. 1
    Prepare an even batch

    Trim the beans and compare their size. Similar pieces cook more evenly; a thick bean and a fine haricot vert need different checks.

  2. 2
    Blanch and check

    Briefly cook in boiling water for this method. Test a sample for the intended bite; brightness alone cannot tell you its texture.

  3. 3
    Cool for later service

    Transfer the beans to an ice-water bath to stop further cooking promptly. Remove them once cold, rather than leaving them to soak.

  4. 4
    Drain before the finish

    Drain well so carried water does not dilute the finish. Refrigerate promptly if holding for later; reheat and season according to the recipe.

Explain
Blanching and shocking are separate actions. Cooling interrupts cooking; draining prepares the vegetable for an effective final seasoning or reheating.
Apply
Practise describing haricots verts as slender green beans. At lineup, ask whether today’s vegetables are blanched ahead or cooked directly to order.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Blanching & shocking 76 recipes

  • The water begins at the heat specified by the method and recovers promptly after the food enters. A brief drop in bubbling is expected; a batch that prevents recovery needs adjustment.
  • Time and the required texture or purpose are checked together. Piece size and the next step matter; where a preservation method specifies a blanching time, a visual check does not replace that timed process.
  • The ice bath was made and standing beside the pot BEFORE anything went into the water, not started once the timer went off.
  • It went straight into the ice water, fully submerged, and stayed until cold at the centre: a piece still warm inside is still cooking, and the arrest has not happened yet.
  • Drained and dried afterwards rather than left sitting in water, so it does not carry that water into whatever it meets next.

Where it goes wrong The ice bath was not ready, so the food sat draining in a colander while its own residual heat carried on cooking it. By the time it cooled it was a stage past where it was pulled, and exact arrest is the entire reason for blanching rather than simply boiling.

Knife cuts: dice, julienne, bias 150 recipes

  • Take any three pieces from anywhere in the pile: they match closely enough in every dimension that you would have to measure them to argue otherwise.
  • The cut faces are flat and clean rather than ragged or crushed; leaves and herbs are cut through, not bruised, and the board carries no dark smear where they were.
  • The stated size is the actual size, held against a rule or a known reference rather than called about right.
  • Everything in the pile is the shape that was asked for: no wedges, no half pieces, and no offcuts swept in to avoid waste.
  • They cook evenly, which is the entire point: at the end of cooking nothing is still raw and nothing has collapsed.

Where it goes wrong The knife was dull, or the vegetable was never squared off first, so the pieces vary: the small ones are mush by the time the large ones are cooked, and the dish has two textures where it wanted one.

From the pass

This crust is short, not layered: cold lard coats the flour and blocks gluten, which is why it stays crisp instead of turning to leather. Steam is its enemy. Wet chard boils under the lid, lifts the top sheet off the filling and leaves the base grey and sodden. Wring the leaves until the cloth runs dry, prick the lid so steam can leave, and bake low in a hot oven.
