Alaska King Crab Legs with Drawn Butter

  • Easy
  • 30 min
  • $$$$ roughly 4 of 4 for cost

rich · bright · aromatic · warm-spiced Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light, fading in warm spice.

Ingredients

  • 2kg Alaskan king crab legs, split
  • 2L water
  • 2 tbsp salt
  • 2 lemons, halved
  • 225g butter
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • Flaky salt and lemon wedges

Method

  1. King crab is sold cooked and frozen, so the job is reheating, not cooking. Thaw it fully in the fridge first.
  2. Bring the salted water and lemon to a boil in a wide pot with a steamer basket.
  3. Steam the split legs 4-5 min, just until heated through. Any longer and the meat toughens and goes stringy. 5 min
  4. Melt the butter gently with the garlic, skim the foam, and pour off the clear butter.
  5. Serve the legs on a warm platter with drawn butter, lemon, and crackers.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Skimming: foam, fat and a clear broth 73 recipes

  • It is held below a rolling boil for the whole time. Agitation drives fat and fine protein into the liquid as an emulsion, and nothing skimmed afterwards will take them back out.
  • The grey foam that rises in the first minutes is lifted off and discarded rather than stirred back in.
  • Fat is taken from the surface where it gathers, the pot moved off the centre of the heat if that is what it takes to collect it in one place.
  • The liquid is visibly clearer at the end than it was at the start: it is possible to see down into it.
  • Nothing lifted off goes back in. What was skimmed is discarded, not tipped back once the ladle is full.

Where it goes wrong The pot was allowed to boil, usually while something else was being attended to. Fat and protein emulsify into the liquid under agitation and cannot be lifted off afterwards, so the finished broth stays cloudy and greasy however patiently it is skimmed from then on.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 130 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

Bering Sea king crab is the great luxury of the Alaskan table. Because it is cooked at sea and frozen immediately, over-steaming is the only real way to ruin it: four minutes to warm through is all it needs.
