Chana Masala

  • Easy
  • 40 min
  • Serves 4
  • $
  • Vegetarian

bright · aromatic · herb-fresh · earthy Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, aromatics blooming from the first bite, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 2 cans chickpeas (or 250g dried, cooked)
  • 2 onions, finely chopped
  • 3 tomatoes or 1 can, crushed
  • Ginger-garlic paste
  • 2 tsp coriander, 1 tsp cumin, ½ tsp turmeric, chili, 1 tsp amchur or lemon
  • Cilantro, ghee or oil

Allergen screen

Found: dairy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Brown onions properly: 12 patient minutes, deep gold.
  2. Add ginger-garlic, then spices; fry 30 seconds in the fat.
  3. Add tomatoes; cook until the masala pulls away from the pan and the oil rises.
  4. Chickpeas + a cup of water; simmer 15 min, mashing a few. Finish with amchur and cilantro. 15 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 78 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

Mashing & puréeing 90 recipes

  • Smooth to the degree the dish asks for, judged on the tongue rather than by eye: no lumps, no fibres, no grit, unless texture is the point.
  • No gluey elasticity: a spoonful drops cleanly off the spoon rather than stretching in a rope behind it.
  • The consistency holds: it mounds on a spoon and stays there, neither slumping flat nor standing so stiff that a spoon has to cut it.
  • Nothing separates on the plate: no water or fat weeping out into a ring within a minute of serving.
  • The colour is even throughout, with no streaks of unincorporated fat, liquid, or seasoning at the edges of the bowl.

Where it goes wrong A starchy ingredient met a high-speed blade, so the cells burst and released their starch: what should be light comes out as an elastic glue that lifts off the spoon in one mass.

From the pass

The oil separating is India's version of reading the fond: when the masala pulls from the pan and fat beads at its edges, the water is gone and the onions and tomatoes have actually fried instead of stewed. Everything before that moment is preamble; everything after is assembly. Amchur, the dried-mango powder at the finish, is the sour spine street vendors use; lemon substitutes, but the funky-tart depth is the real signature. Mash a few chickpeas; they thicken the gravy from inside.
