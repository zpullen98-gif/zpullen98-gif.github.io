Ingredients

  • Dough: 500g flour, 250ml warm milk, 7g yeast, 1 egg, 60g butter, 1 tbsp sugar, 1 tsp salt
  • Filling one: braised cabbage with egg. Filling two: potato and fried onion. Filling three: seasoned ground beef
  • 1 egg for glazing (baked) or oil for frying, the two schools
  • Each filling cooked, seasoned boldly, and fully cooled
  • A kitchen and an afternoon

Method

  1. Rich yeasted dough; rise 1 h until doubled. 60 min
  2. Divide into 16; flatten rounds; a generous spoon of COLD filling each.
  3. Pinch into plump ovals seam-side down; proof 20 min. 20 min
  4. Bake glazed at 200°C, 20 min, or fry seam-down in a finger of oil. Cool under a towel; the towel-softened crust is traditional. 20 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 39 recipes

  • Where the recipe calls for browning, the faces have the intended colour and the fond is dissolved into the liquid without burnt residue. A pale braise is judged against its own method.
  • The liquid level matches the recipe and is checked during cooking; it has not run dry. The heat is gentle enough for the intended result, without violent agitation.
  • Tenderness is checked in the thickest piece, not assumed from elapsed time. The finished form matches the dish: intact for slicing or lifting, or deliberately yielding to pulling.
  • The cooking liquid is finished to the consistency the dish needs, with excess fat managed and seasoning checked. A light broth and a spoon-coating sauce are different intended results.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The result is tough, dry or falling apart when the dish calls for intact pieces. Check heat, liquid level, cut size and tenderness during cooking; elapsed time alone does not identify the cause, and pulled texture is not a fault when the dish asks for it.

Proofing: the rise before the oven 78 recipes

  • Judged on volume against the container rather than on the clock: visibly grown, and grown by roughly the amount the recipe describes.
  • The poke test agrees: a floured fingertip pressed a centimetre in leaves a dent that springs back slowly and only part of the way. Straight back means it is not ready; no spring at all means it has gone.
  • The surface is domed, smooth, and slightly sheened: not cratered, wrinkled, or collapsed, and not skinned over and dry from proofing uncovered.
  • Handled, it feels alive and airy: light for its size, with bubbles visible under the skin.
  • It springs in the oven: the piece lifts in the first minutes and the scores open. A shape that does not move at all was proofed past its point before it went in.

Where it goes wrong It was proofed to the clock in a kitchen colder or warmer than the recipe assumed: overproofed, it collapses as the blade touches it and bakes flat and dense with a sour edge; underproofed, it tears at the side and bakes tight.

From the pass

The train-station pirozhok, the grandmother pirozhok, the theater-buffet pirozhok, a portable civilization. Hot filling murders the dough from inside, so patience between steps is the recipe. Fried is the street; baked is the home; the towel rest is the secret third ingredient.
