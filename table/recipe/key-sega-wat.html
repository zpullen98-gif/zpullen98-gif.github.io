Ethiopian · Main

Key Sega Wat

  • Advanced
  • 70 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$$

aromatic · warm-spiced · rich · sweet-edged Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, warm spice humming through the middle, trailing gentle sweetness.

Ingredients

  • 900 g red onion, chopped fine and even
  • 800 g beef chuck, in 3 cm cubes
  • 120 g niter kibbeh
  • 60 g berbere
  • 30 g garlic, pounded to a paste
  • 20 g ginger, pounded to a paste
  • 300 ml water, plus more as needed
  • 30 ml tej or dry red wine
  • 3 g korarima, ground
  • 9 g salt

Equipment

Mortar & pestle

Allergen screen

Found: alcohol

Method

  1. Put the onions alone in a heavy dry pot over medium low heat, no fat and no water, and stir every few minutes as they weep and the liquid steams off.
  2. Keep going until the pile has sunk to a third of its volume, gone tan, and a spoon dragged across the base leaves a dry track that does not fill in; expect 45 minutes and do not rush it. 45 min
  3. Only now stir in half the niter kibbeh, then the garlic and ginger, and cook until the raw garlic smell turns sweet.
  4. Add the berbere and the remaining kibbeh and stir constantly over low heat until the paste darkens to brick red and beads of clear red oil rise at the edge, about eight minutes.
  5. Add the beef, the salt and 300 ml water, cover, and hold at the barest simmer, adding water in splashes whenever the surface tightens, until the meat parts under a spoon and the sauce holds a furrow.
  6. Off the heat, stir through the tej and korarima and let it sit 15 minutes before it goes to the mesob. 15 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 22 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Braising 35 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

From the pass

The dry onion cook is the whole technique. With no fat in the pot the onion's own water boils off and its sugars caramelise against the metal instead of frying in oil, and the cell walls collapse into a pulp that becomes the body of the sauce. A wat is thickened by onion, nothing else. Add butter at the start and the onion fries: the shreds stay intact, they never break down, and you finish with thin greasy gravy and floating strings. Total braise runs about 90 minutes beyond the active work.
