Lo Mai Gai (Lotus Leaf Sticky Rice Parcels)

  • Advanced
  • 45 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

umami-deep · rich · briny · aromatic Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, richness that coats and comforts, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 500 g glutinous rice
  • 6 dried lotus leaves
  • 300 g boneless chicken thigh, in 3 cm pieces
  • 2 lap cheong (Chinese cured sausage), sliced on the angle
  • 6 dried shiitake, soaked and quartered
  • 30 g dried shrimp, soaked
  • 3 salted duck egg yolks, halved
  • 30 ml oyster sauce
  • 20 ml light soy sauce
  • 10 ml dark soy sauce
  • 10 g caster sugar
  • 10 g cornflour
  • 15 ml Shaoxing wine
  • 3 g ground white pepper
  • 20 g ginger, minced
  • 45 ml groundnut oil
  • 200 ml shiitake soaking liquid

Method

  1. Soak the glutinous rice in cold water for 4 hours, until a grain crushes to powder between your nails, then drain; soak the lotus leaves in hot water until leathery and pliable and wipe both faces. 240 min
  2. Marinate the chicken in the oyster sauce, both soys, sugar, cornflour, wine and pepper for 20 minutes, until the pieces look glazed and cling to each other. 20 min
  3. Fry the ginger, dried shrimp and shiitake in the oil until the shrimp smells sweet and the mushrooms have squeaked and gone slack, add the soaking liquid, boil 2 minutes, then fold the lot through the drained raw rice until every grain is stained brown. 2 min
  4. Cup 150 g of rice into each leaf, bury chicken, lap cheong and half an egg yolk in the middle, cover with more rice and fold the leaf into a tight brick with no gap at the seam.
  5. Steam over a hard boil for 60 minutes, then open one: the rice should be glossy and hold its shape under a press, with no chalky white core. 60 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Marinating: acid, salt and time 70 recipes

  • Contact is total: every surface is wetted, and the pieces are not welded into a clump with dry faces where they pressed together.
  • The stated time was kept and kept cold, read against a clock, because this is the one technique where longer is a different result rather than a safer one.
  • It worked at the surface and no further: cut a piece and the marinade’s colour stops a few millimetres in. A protein gone opaque and chalky to the centre before any heat reached it has been cooked by the acid.
  • The texture is still springy and whole, not mushy, mealy, or fraying at the surface when it is lifted.
  • Where the next step browns it, the surface was patted dry first: a wet surface cannot brown, and the marinade’s sugars will scorch before the food colours.

Where it goes wrong It was left well beyond the stated time on the assumption that more is better, so acid and salt worked through the thin edges: those cook to cotton and read grainy while the thick centre tastes of nothing at all.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 130 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

Glutinous rice is almost pure amylopectin. Boiled, it never takes on enough water to cook through: it goes to paste outside and stays chalky in the centre. It has to soak cold for hours so water enters the grain slowly, then steam so it gelatinises evenly. The leaf is not decoration either; it seals the parcel so the rice cooks in its own trapped steam and picks up that tea-like smell.
