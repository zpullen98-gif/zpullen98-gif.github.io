Sichuan · Main

Hui Guo Rou

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

aromatic · rich · fiery · umami-deep Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, richness that coats and comforts, finishing long and savory.

Ingredients

  • 500 g pork belly, skin on, in one piece
  • 20 g ginger, sliced
  • 2 spring onions, whole
  • 10 ml Shaoxing wine
  • 5 g huajiao, whole, for the poaching water
  • 30 ml rapeseed oil
  • 30 g Pixian doubanjiang, chopped
  • 15 g tianmianjiang
  • 5 g douchi, rinsed
  • 200 g suan miao (green garlic shoots), cut on the bias in 4 cm lengths
  • 10 ml light soy sauce
  • 5 g sugar

Method

  1. Poach the belly whole with ginger, spring onion, wine and huajiao at a bare simmer until a chopstick enters the lean against slight resistance, about 30 minutes; cool it in its liquid, then chill hard. 30 min
  2. Slice the cold belly across into pieces 2 mm thick and about the size of a large stamp, skin, fat and lean in every slice.
  3. Fry the slices in the oil over medium heat without crowding until the fat runs out and each piece curls into a small cup with a browned rim: this is the deng zhan wo, the lantern shape, and it is the whole point.
  4. Push the pork aside, drop the doubanjiang into the rendered fat and fry until the oil runs red, then add tianmianjiang and douchi for 30 seconds, until they smell toasted and sweet.
  5. Add soy, sugar and the garlic shoots and toss about 40 seconds, only until the white stems turn translucent and the green tops slump; they must stay squeaky.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Tadka: blooming spices in fat 22 recipes

  • The fat is at tempering heat before anything goes in, around 180C (350F): one test seed dropped in surfaces and cracks within two or three seconds. A seed that sinks and sits quiet means the fat is cold and everything will steep rather than bloom; fat already hazing and thinning has gone past 200C (390F), and the first thing in will be black before the last thing is in.
  • Ask what went in and in what order, then read it back off the pan: the hardest seeds lead, with ten to twenty seconds before the next addition, and the softest seeds, any leaf and any powdered aromatic go last with only seconds left. Everything tipped in on one drop is a fail even when the pan smells right, because the most delicate thing in it cannot survive the time the hardest one needs.
  • The pan gave a hard scatter of popping that rose, peaked and thinned before it left the heat. A pan that stayed silent never reached popping heat and the seeds are still hard and raw against the teeth; seeds still cracking loudly as it is poured finished blooming in the dish instead of in the fat, where nothing was watching them.
  • Cumin is exactly one shade darker than it went in and no more, a warm even brown that smells nutty and sweet; anything near coffee colour, or smelling of scorch, has already carried bitterness into the fat. Fenugreek is pale gold, checked under the light with nothing copper or brown among it, and this is the mark to judge first: past gold it turns the whole pot bitter, and no salt, sugar or souring will pull it back.
  • The leaf is blistered and gone translucent, still green at the edge and crisp rather than black or limp, and it cracked sharply when it met the fat. It reached the dish still at full heat and answered with a violent hiss, and it reads on the surface afterwards as bright separate fat with whole spices suspended through it, not a dull dark sediment settled on the bottom.

Where it goes wrong The fat was pushed past hazing before anything went in, or every spice was tipped in together, so the hardest and the most delicate cooked on one clock: in the seconds the hardest seeds need to pop, fenugreek goes from gold to brown and the leaf goes black. Nothing can be corrected after that. The tempering pours in dark and smells scorched over the top of the dish, the specks read black rather than brown when the surface is turned to the light, and a flat acrid bitterness arrives at the back of the palate after the swallow and stays there through the next mouthful.

Rendering fat: and cooking in what runs out 73 recipes

  • It started cold or low and came up slowly: fat that met a hot pan seizes and scorches before the inside can melt, and there is no recovering it.
  • The fat in the pan is clear and clean-smelling, pale to golden, with no dark sediment on the floor and nothing acrid in the steam.
  • What is left behind is crisp and deep gold with no soft pale fat still clinging to it and no black burnt edges.
  • There is a real volume of it: enough to film the pan floor and be poured off and kept, not a dry smear that never ran.
  • Whatever cooks in it browns in it and takes its flavour, rather than sitting in a greasy pool that never gets hot enough to colour anything.

Where it goes wrong The heat was raised to hurry it along, so the outside seized and browned before the interior fat could melt; very little runs out, and the solids come out burnt on the outside and rubbery within.

From the pass

Twice cooked means twice. The poach sets the meat firm enough to slice thin when cold; the fry renders it. Slice it warm and it tears, slice it thick and it never curls, and the curl is what matters: a flat slice sits in its own fat and stews, while a cupped slice presents a browned rim and gives up its fat for the doubanjiang to fry in. Garlic shoots go in at the very end or they turn to grey rag.
