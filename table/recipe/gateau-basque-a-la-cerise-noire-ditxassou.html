The French Basque & Gascony · Dessert

Gâteau Basque à la Cerise Noire d'Itxassou

  • Advanced
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · nutty-toasted Leads with richness that coats and comforts, toasted, nutty warmth throughout.

Ingredients

  • 250 g plain flour
  • 50 g ground almonds
  • 180 g soft butter
  • 180 g caster sugar
  • 1 whole egg and 1 yolk
  • 6 g baking powder
  • 3 g fine salt
  • 15 ml dark rum
  • 300 g thick black cherry jam from Itxassou
  • 1 yolk beaten with 1 tsp water, to glaze

Equipment

Piping bag

Method

  1. Cream the butter and sugar to a pale soft paste, beat in the egg, yolk, rum and salt, then fold in the flour, almonds and baking powder just until no dry flour shows.
  2. Scrape the dough into two flat discs between sheets of paper and chill at least two hours, until it is firm enough to pipe but not hard.
  3. Spread or pipe half into a buttered 22 cm ring, taking it 1 cm up the sides, then spread the jam inside a clean 1.5 cm border of bare dough.
  4. Pipe the second half over in a tight spiral, smooth it, and seal the rim by pressing all round with a wet fingertip until you cannot see the join.
  5. Glaze, chill 20 minutes, glaze again and score a crosshatch with a fork, then bake at 175C (350F) for 40 to 45 minutes, until it is deep chestnut brown and firm in the centre. 20 min
  6. Cool completely in the tin before cutting, since the crumb sets only as it cools.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Scoring 28 recipes

  • Lift one cut open with a fingertip: it parts through the skin and stops in the fat beneath, showing a pale wall at its base. Any cut showing red or fibrous flesh has gone too deep; any that has not reached the fat has left the skin tethered, and it will tighten under heat.
  • The lines run parallel at even spacing, roughly a finger's width apart, each carried the full length of the face and stopped the same margin short of the edge. Sight down them: lines that converge, wander or crowd at one end cook at different rates and read as careless on the plate.
  • Each line is one clean draw of the blade held at a slant, not a sawn or doubled track, and it leaves a bevelled lip standing slightly proud rather than a vertical slot. The surface should be dry and cold, near 4C (39F), when it is cut; worked warm and slack it drags, and the blade tears where it should part.
  • After cooking the cuts have opened and set, each a clear gap with firm browned walls. Lines closed back to faint scratches were cut too shallow or too straight down, and the surface has shrunk over them instead of through them.
  • The piece lies flat and true when it is set down, with no cupping through the middle and no lifted, curled edge. A bowed piece means the tightening surface was never released, so it pulled the whole thing up and left the underside pale where it stood off the heat.

Where it goes wrong The blade was pushed rather than drawn, through a surface left warm and slack, so it went past the fat and into the flesh. The cuts bleed as they cook and the juices flood the surface, so it steams instead of crisping: the lines gape into wet ragged trenches, the flesh between them dries into hard ridges, and the skin stays leathery and pale where it should have blistered and set.

Creaming butter & sugar 28 recipes

  • The butter started at the right temperature: it took a fingerprint and held its shape, neither greasy and slumping nor hard from the fridge.
  • It has visibly paled, from yellow towards near-white, and grown in volume, and getting there took minutes rather than seconds.
  • The texture is light and aerated: a spoonful is fluffy and holds a soft peak rather than sitting dense on the spoon.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it is still faintly gritty with undissolved sugar, and not slick or oily.
  • It is one homogeneous cream: not split, slackened, or separating into a greasy mass with liquid running out of it.

Where it goes wrong The butter was too warm, or it was beaten well past its point, so the fat softened and gave up the air it had taken: the mixture goes slack and oily and the cake bakes dense and greasy with a sunken middle.

From the pass

This is a cake batter behaving as a pastry: nearly three quarters butter to flour and no added water, so almost no gluten develops and it can be piped but never rolled. Two faults spoil it. A loose jam boils, finds the seam and bursts out, so use a properly set jam and seal a bare border. And an underbaked gâteau is pasty at the middle; take the colour to deep chestnut, not gold.
