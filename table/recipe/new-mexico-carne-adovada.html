New Mexico Carne Adovada

  • Intermediate
  • 3 h
  • $ roughly 1 of 4 for cost

aromatic · bright · earthy · fiery Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, acid holding everything up to the light, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients Method Check the result Pairings Your notes Videos

Ingredients

  • 1.2kg pork shoulder, in large cubes
  • 10 dried New Mexico red chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tbsp cumin
  • 1 tbsp Mexican oregano
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 500ml chicken stock
  • 1 tbsp cider vinegar

Equipment

Dutch oven

Method

  1. Toast the dried chiles briefly, soak in hot water 20 min, then blend with the garlic, cumin, oregano, salt, and some soaking water into a smooth red sauce. 20 min
  2. Toss the pork cubes in the red chile sauce and marinate at least 4 hours, ideally overnight. 240 min
  3. Turn everything into a Dutch oven with the stock.
  4. Braise at 160C for 2.5-3 hours until the pork is falling apart and the sauce is deep and thick. 180 min
  5. Stir in the vinegar and season. Serve with rice, beans, or tortillas.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 39 recipes

  • Where the recipe calls for browning, the faces have the intended colour and the fond is dissolved into the liquid without burnt residue. A pale braise is judged against its own method.
  • The liquid level matches the recipe and is checked during cooking; it has not run dry. The heat is gentle enough for the intended result, without violent agitation.
  • Tenderness is checked in the thickest piece, not assumed from elapsed time. The finished form matches the dish: intact for slicing or lifting, or deliberately yielding to pulling.
  • The cooking liquid is finished to the consistency the dish needs, with excess fat managed and seasoning checked. A light broth and a spoon-coating sauce are different intended results.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The result is tough, dry or falling apart when the dish calls for intact pieces. Check heat, liquid level, cut size and tenderness during cooking; elapsed time alone does not identify the cause, and pulled texture is not a fault when the dish asks for it.

Soaking dried goods back to life 69 recipes

  • Fully submerged with room to swell: the level is well above everything at the start and still above it at the end.
  • Given the full time, and judged by what is in the bowl rather than by the hours on the clock.
  • Tested at the CENTRE of the largest piece, cut or pressed. Anything with a hard dry core will still have one after cooking, because the outside softens first and stops the water getting further in.
  • The soaking water was dealt with on purpose: kept because it holds flavour, or thrown away because it holds what should not be eaten, but never simply left undecided.
  • Drained before it is used, so the dish does not silently gain all the water that came with it.

Where it goes wrong Too little water, or too little time. The pieces swell until they are packed tight against each other and the ones in the middle never fully take up water; they will not soften later in the dish either, so the finished plate has hard pieces scattered through it.

From the pass

Pork marinated and braised in pure red chile: no tomatoes, just chile, garlic, and time. Use whole dried New Mexico chiles, not powder; the long marinade lets the chile penetrate the meat, which is the whole point of adovada.
