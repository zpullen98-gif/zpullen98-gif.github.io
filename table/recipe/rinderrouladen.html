German · Main

Rinderrouladen

  • Advanced
  • 2 h 30 m
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

rich · umami-deep · sweet-edged · herb-fresh Leads with richness that coats and comforts, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 4 wide, thin slices beef topside (ask the butcher for roulade cuts)
  • 4 tsp sharp German mustard; 8 bacon rashers
  • 2 dill pickles, quartered lengthwise; 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 carrots, 1 leek, tomato paste for the braise; 500ml beef stock + 200ml red wine
  • Butter, flour; red cabbage and spätzle alongside

Method

  1. Lay beef flat; paint with mustard; layer bacon, onion, a pickle spear; roll tight and pin or tie.
  2. Brown the rolls hard on all sides; remove; brown the vegetables and paste.
  3. Wine, stock; rouladen back; braise covered at 160°C, 1.5–2 h. 120 min
  4. Lift out; strain and reduce the sauce; whisk in cold butter. Slice one open to admire the spiral.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 39 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

Reducing a sauce 95 recipes

  • A steady simmer with the surface moving, never a rolling boil that throws it up the sides and scorches what is left on the base.
  • Judged by behaviour rather than the clock: it coats the back of a spoon and holds a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • The drop in volume is visible against the side of the pan: there is a line to see, not a guess about whether anything has happened.
  • Seasoned at the end and not the beginning, because salt concentrates at exactly the rate everything else does.
  • Nothing is catching underneath: a spoon drawn across the base comes up clean, with no browned film building where the heat is fiercest.

Where it goes wrong It was seasoned at the start and then reduced by half. The salt concentrated along with everything else, so the sauce reaches perfect body and is too salty to serve. And the only remedy is to dilute it and reduce it all over again.

From the pass

Mustard, bacon, pickle: the trinity inside every German grandmother's roulade, and the pickle is not garnish; its brine keeps the lean beef juicy through the long braise, basting from within. Roll tight, secure well, brown hard: the fond those spirals leave is the gravy's foundation, finished with the strained braising liquid. Sunday food, rotkohl and knödel alongside by law of custom. Thin-cut topside is the correct canvas: the filling is the actual painting.
