Ohn No Khao Swe

  • Intermediate
  • 35 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

aromatic · umami-deep · rich · earthy Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, settling low and earthy.

Ingredients

  • 600 g chicken thigh, diced 2 cm
  • 200 g red shallots
  • 25 g garlic
  • 20 g fresh ginger
  • 100 ml peanut oil
  • 4 g ground turmeric
  • 8 g mild dried red chilli powder
  • 60 g chickpea flour
  • 700 ml chicken stock, cold
  • 400 ml coconut milk
  • 45 ml fish sauce
  • 500 g fresh wheat noodles
  • 3 eggs, hard boiled and quartered
  • 40 g fried shallots
  • 30 ml shallot oil
  • 1 lime, in wedges
  • 10 g crushed dried chilli

Method

  1. Blend shallots, garlic and ginger, then fry in the peanut oil with turmeric and chilli until the paste is brick coloured and oil pools at the sides.
  2. Add the chicken and turn it in the paste until the outsides tighten and go opaque.
  3. Whisk the chickpea flour into the cold stock, pour it in with the fish sauce, and simmer 15 minutes, stirring, until it lightly coats a spoon. 15 min
  4. Pour in the coconut milk and bring it back only to a tremble, where a bubble breaks the surface every few seconds; hold it there five minutes and no hotter.
  5. Boil the noodles, drain them hard, ladle the soup over, and finish each bowl with egg, lime, chilli, then the fried shallots and a spoon of their oil last.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Reducing a sauce 95 recipes

  • A steady simmer with the surface moving, never a rolling boil that throws it up the sides and scorches what is left on the base.
  • Judged by behaviour rather than the clock: it coats the back of a spoon and holds a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • The drop in volume is visible against the side of the pan: there is a line to see, not a guess about whether anything has happened.
  • Seasoned at the end and not the beginning, because salt concentrates at exactly the rate everything else does.
  • Nothing is catching underneath: a spoon drawn across the base comes up clean, with no browned film building where the heat is fiercest.

Where it goes wrong It was seasoned at the start and then reduced by half. The salt concentrated along with everything else, so the sauce reaches perfect body and is too salty to serve. And the only remedy is to dilute it and reduce it all over again.

Salted water & the float test 188 recipes

  • The water was salted before anything went in, and tasting it confirms it: seasoned on the tongue, with the salt dissolved rather than sitting on the pot floor.
  • There is enough water, hot enough, that the pot returns to its boil quickly after the food goes in and never stops moving.
  • Where the dish uses the float as its signal, the signal is obeyed: the pieces rise and they come out then, not several minutes later once something else was finished.
  • Nothing is stuck to the bottom of the pot or welded to itself, and the water has not thickened into a cloudy sludge.
  • Drained on a bite rather than on the clock: resistance at the centre, with no chalky raw core.

Where it goes wrong The pot was too small and the water unsalted, so it never recovered its boil: the food sat in slack water, went gummy at the surface, and comes out bland in a way that no sauce afterwards can fix.

From the pass

The thickener is chickpea flour, not the coconut: the starch is what holds the fat in suspension. Boiling after the coconut goes in breaks that suspension and gives a greasy slick over grainy curds, so the pot never rises past a tremble again. Fried shallots and their oil arrive at the table separately for the reason they are made separately: the oil perfumes the broth, the shallots give crunch, and crunch is gone a minute after it meets hot liquid.
