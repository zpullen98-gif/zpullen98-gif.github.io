Boeuf Bourguignon

  • Intermediate
  • 3 h 30 m
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

umami-deep · rich · herb-fresh Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, richness that coats and comforts, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 1.2kg beef chuck, large cubes
  • 150g lardons
  • 1 bottle Burgundy or pinot noir
  • 500ml stock, 2 tbsp tomato paste
  • Carrots, pearl onions, mushrooms
  • Flour, thyme, bay

Method

  1. Brown beef in batches until crusted; render lardons.
  2. Sauté aromatics, add flour and paste; deglaze with wine.
  3. Add stock and herbs; braise covered at 160°C for 2.5–3 h until fork-tender. 180 min
  4. Sauté mushrooms and onions separately; fold in and reduce sauce to gloss.

How it should come out

  • The sauce is glossy and near-black red, thick enough to coat the back of a spoon and hold a parted line, with no orange fat standing on the surface and no thin purple liquid at the pot's edge.
  • A cube lifted out shows a dark browned exterior rather than pale grey, splits under the side of a fork with no sawing, and still holds its corners. Meat collapsed to threads in the pot went past the mark.
  • Cut one open: moist and uniform to the centre, with no dry grey ring of squeezed fibre inside a tender exterior.
  • Pearl onions are whole with their layers intact and the mushrooms are firm at the centre; bite one of each and it still tastes of itself rather than only of sauce.
  • The wine reads as depth, not acid: round at the finish, no tannic scrape on the teeth. Salt lands square with the sauce at its final thickness, with no flat middle and no hard salty edge from a pot seasoned at the strength it had an hour earlier.

Where it goes wrong Dry, stringy beef inside a good sauce, because the pot ran at a boil instead of a tick. The fibres wrung themselves out long before the collagen melted, so the meat shreds under the fork and still eats like rope.

From the pass

Batches, always: gray meat makes gray stew. A crowded pan drops below searing temperature, the beef exhales its moisture, and everything boils in its own juice instead of browning; the fond those batches leave behind is most of the sauce's spine. Burgundy's farmers built the dish to turn a workhorse cut and an open bottle into something aristocratic, and the finish still matters: mushrooms and pearl onions cooked separately and folded in late, so they arrive as themselves and not as casualties.
