Party Jollof Rice

  • Intermediate
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

fiery · umami-deep · smoky Leads with real fire that builds and stays, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, on a drift of smoke.

Ingredients

  • 500 g long grain parboiled rice
  • 600 g ripe plum tomatoes, or 400 g tinned plum tomatoes
  • 250 g red bell pepper
  • 1 or 2 scotch bonnet chillies
  • 250 g onion
  • 120 ml groundnut oil
  • 60 g tomato paste
  • 8 g curry powder
  • 3 g dried thyme
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 700 ml hot chicken stock
  • 8 g salt

Method

  1. Blend the tomatoes, bell pepper, scotch bonnet and half the onion smooth, then boil the puree hard in a wide pan for 15 to 20 minutes until it turns from scarlet to brick and stops smelling raw and green. 20 min
  2. In a heavy pot fry the remaining onion in the oil until soft, add the tomato paste and fry it 5 minutes until it darkens to rust and the oil floats free around it. 5 min
  3. Add the reduced puree, curry powder, thyme, bay and salt and fry, stirring, for 10 to 15 minutes more, until the oil separates at the edges again and the base tastes sweet rather than sharp. 15 min
  4. Rinse the rice and stir it through until every grain is coated red, then add the hot stock so the liquid sits exactly level with the rice. Cover with foil, then the lid, and cook on the lowest heat for 30 minutes without stirring or peeking. 30 min
  5. Lift the lid, raise the heat for 2 to 3 minutes until the base smells faintly of smoke, then kill the heat, fork the rice through from the sides and leave it covered 10 minutes. 3 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Parboiled rice is steamed in the husk and dried before milling, so its starch is set before it ever meets the pot; it holds its shape through half an hour in acidic tomato where basmati would collapse. Acid also slows softening, which is why the pepper base has to be fried down until the oil breaks out before the rice goes in. Keep the stock level with the rice, never above, and do not stir it.
