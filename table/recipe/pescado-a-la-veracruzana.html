Pescado a la Veracruzana

  • Intermediate
  • 50 min
  • Serves 4
  • $

briny · bright · gently hot · sweet-edged Leads with a briny, maritime salinity, acid holding everything up to the light, trailing gentle sweetness.

Ingredients

  • 4 thick fillets or 1 whole red snapper (huachinango, the classic), salted and rubbed with lime 15 min ahead
  • The sauce: 6 ripe tomatoes chopped, 1 onion, 4 garlic cloves, simmered down
  • THE MEDITERRANEAN-MEXICAN HANDSHAKE: ½ cup green olives, 3 tbsp capers, 2 tbsp raisins, 3–4 pickled güero chilies (or pickled jalapeños), bay, oregano, cinnamon pinch
  • 100ml olive oil; splash of the chile pickling liquid
  • White rice and warm tortillas

Method

  1. Stew onion and garlic in olive oil; tomatoes in; simmer 15 min to a loose, bright sauce. 15 min
  2. Olives, capers, raisins, pickled chilies, herbs and the splash of brine; the sauce should ring salty, sweet, briny and gently hot all at once.
  3. Nestle the lime-cured fish into the sauce; spoon it over; lid on; poach gently 10–12 min until the flesh flakes. 12 min
  4. Rest 5 min in the pan; serve from it, sauce ladled generously, rice catching the excess. 5 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Brining & curing 67 recipes

  • Fully committed either way: in a wet brine nothing floats clear of the liquid, and in a dry cure every face carries salt, seams and cavities included.
  • The time was measured against the weight and the thickness rather than the recipe’s convenience, and it stayed cold for all of it.
  • The change is visible and can be felt: the flesh is firmer than raw and its colour has deepened or gone more translucent, rather than still being slack and pale.
  • Rinsed and dried as the recipe asks, and anything meant to crisp afterwards has a surface dry and tacky to the touch before it goes near heat.
  • Cooked and tasted, it is seasoned right through to the centre rather than merely salty at the surface, and not so salty that the dish cannot be eaten.

Where it goes wrong It sat in the brine well past its time because a few extra hours seemed harmless; the surface is over-salted and the texture has gone from firm to spongy and wet, and no cooking takes salt back out.

Reducing a sauce 86 recipes

  • A steady simmer with the surface moving, never a rolling boil that throws it up the sides and scorches what is left on the base.
  • Judged by behaviour rather than the clock: it coats the back of a spoon and holds a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • The drop in volume is visible against the side of the pan: there is a line to see, not a guess about whether anything has happened.
  • Seasoned at the end and not the beginning, because salt concentrates at exactly the rate everything else does.
  • Nothing is catching underneath: a spoon drawn across the base comes up clean, with no browned film building where the heat is fiercest.

Where it goes wrong It was seasoned at the start and then reduced by half. The salt concentrated along with everything else, so the sauce reaches perfect body and is too salty to serve. And the only remedy is to dilute it and reduce it all over again.

From the pass

Veracruz was Spain's front door into Mexico, and this dish is the meeting minutes: Mediterranean olives, capers and olive oil folded into a New World tomato sauce with pickled chiles casting the deciding vote. The raisins are the old Moorish echo most recipes north of the port forget. It is Mexico's great poached-fish dish (gentle heat, briny-bright sauce) and proof that fusion cooking is not a trend but a four-century-old habit.
