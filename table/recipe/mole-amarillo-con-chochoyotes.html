Mole Amarillo con Chochoyotes

  • Intermediate
  • 55 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

gently hot · rich · earthy · aromatic Leads with a warm current of chile, richness that coats and comforts, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 1.2 kg chicken thighs and drumsticks
  • 1.5 L chicken stock
  • 60 g chilcostle, stemmed and seeded
  • 30 g chile costeño amarillo, stemmed and seeded
  • 20 g chile guajillo, stemmed and seeded
  • 400 g miltomate, husked
  • 120 g white onion
  • 20 g garlic
  • 4 cloves, 6 black peppercorns, 2 g cumin seed
  • 2 large hoja santa leaves
  • 300 g chayote, in batons
  • 200 g ejotes, topped and halved
  • 250 g waxy potatoes, in chunks
  • 250 g fresh nixtamal masa, for thickening
  • 200 g fresh nixtamal masa, for the chochoyotes
  • 25 g lard
  • fine salt

Method

  1. Toast the three chillies on a dry comal 15 seconds a side, until they smell of toast and no darker, soak them in hot water 20 minutes, then blend with the miltomate, onion, garlic and spices and sieve. 20 min
  2. Fry the sieved sauce in a hot dry pot for 8 minutes until it darkens, tightens and no longer smells raw, then add the stock and the chicken and simmer until the meat is just firm. 8 min
  3. Slake the 250 g of masa in 300 ml of cold stock, working it with your fingers until not one lump is left, and pour it into the pot through a sieve while stirring; it thickens in 3-4 minutes and then needs a gentle boil for 5 more to lose its chalky edge. 4 min
  4. Work the lard and a good pinch of salt into the remaining masa, roll walnut sized balls, press a deep dimple into each with your little finger and drop them in; they are ready when they float and the dimple no longer looks wet.
  5. Add the chayote, beans and potato, tuck in the torn hoja santa, and cook 12 minutes until a knife slides into the chayote and the pot smells of aniseed. 12 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Working with masa 13 recipes

  • Pinch a small piece and roll it closed between finger and thumb: hydrated correctly the edge stays smooth and seals over on itself; a fringe of fine cracks opening along the pinched edge is under hydration, and every piece off that batch will split at the rim. In the hand it feels soft and yielding and leaves a matt film on the palm, never a wet skin that strings when the hand lifts away.
  • After the rest it is damp and matt right through, and it has been kept covered the whole time: a pale dried skin over the mass, or grit that grinds under the thumb when a ball is squashed flat, means it stood open to the air or was shaped before the moisture had reached the centre of the ground grain.
  • It lifts off the press as one clean piece, the rim unbroken and the thickness even from side to side at roughly 2 mm, and a hand shaped piece is judged the same way, with no thin translucent windows worn through the middle. A lacy scalloped rim, or a piece that stretches and tears as it is lifted, fails the mark.
  • Held to the face it smells of toasted sweet corn, faintly mineral and clean. A sharp chalky or soapy smell of raw lime, or a bitterness sitting at the back of the tongue when a raw pinch is tasted, means it was under rinsed and no amount of heat afterwards will drive it off.
  • On a surface holding 190 to 200 C (375 to 390 F) it lifts and balloons across most of its face on the last turn, then settles soft and pliable as it cools. Rested a minute and folded in half, it creases without splitting: a piece that never lifts, or that cracks white along the fold, is failed however good its colour.

Where it goes wrong Too little moisture worked in, and no rest to let what there is reach the centre of the ground grain: it handles well while it is being mixed, then tightens as it stands, so the pieces come off the press with cracked scalloped rims, sit flat and pale on the heat and never lift, and go stiff within a minute of coming off, breaking along the crease instead of folding.

Tortillas on the comal 27 recipes

  • It puffs. On the second turn the tortilla lifts into a dome as the trapped water flashes to steam and splits the two faces apart; a tortilla that never puffs was pressed too thick, cooked too cool, or turned too often.
  • Freckled, not blackened: small brown spots across a pale field. An evenly browned tortilla has been on too long and will be a cracker by the time it reaches the table.
  • It folds without cracking while warm, and still folds after five minutes under a cloth. A tortilla that splits along the fold has lost too much water on the comal.
  • The edges are unbroken and roughly round, with no ragged fringe: a fringed edge means the masa was too dry when it was pressed, and no amount of heat will fix it afterwards.
  • It smells of toasted corn rather than of scorch or of raw lime. Raw lime means the nixtamal was under-rinsed; scorch means the comal was above the working heat.

Where it goes wrong The masa was too dry or the comal too cool. A dry masa cracks at the edge on pressing and cannot generate the steam that puffs it; a cool comal dries the tortilla through before the water can flash, so it leaves the heat stiff, pale and already stale.

From the pass

Amarillo is thickened with masa, not fat, so the masa is slaked in cold liquid first: tipped straight into hot broth the outside of every grain gelatinises at once and seals a raw core, which is why lumpy mole never smooths out again. Once in, it wants a gentle boil for five minutes. Pulled off early it tastes chalky and slackens as it stands.
