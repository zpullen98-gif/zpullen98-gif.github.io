Fudgy Brownies

  • Easy
  • 50 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · sweet-edged · floral Leads with richness that coats and comforts, a sweet edge rounding the corners, leaving a faint perfume.

Ingredients

  • 200g dark chocolate + 175g butter, melted; PLUS 30g cocoa powder, the double-chocolate structure of the fudgy school
  • 220g sugar + 60g brown sugar; 3 eggs + 1 yolk; 1 tbsp vanilla
  • 90g flour ONLY: low flour is the fudge line; ½ tsp salt + flaky salt for the top
  • THE SHINY-CRUST SECRET: whisk the eggs and sugars a full 2 minutes until thick; the dissolved sugar is what bakes into that crackled tissue top
  • A 20cm square pan, lined with an overhang; the discipline to underbake

Method

  1. Melt chocolate and butter; cool 5 min. Whisk eggs and sugars the full 2 minutes. Do not negotiate; the crust depends on it. 5 min
  2. Stream in the chocolate; fold in cocoa, flour, salt until JUST combined; into the pan; flaky salt over.
  3. Bake 175°C, 22–26 min: the tester should come out with dark moist crumbs and a smear; a clean tester means you have baked cake. 26 min
  4. COOL COMPLETELY in the pan (hours; the center sets as it cools), lift by the overhang, and cut with a hot wiped knife. Corner people and middle people may now negotiate.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Folding: keeping the air in 34 recipes

  • The air was actually built first and it shows: whatever was creamed or whipped has changed colour and grown in volume, pale and light rather than dense.
  • The two mixtures were brought closer before being combined: a little of the light one worked into the heavy one first, so the rest is not beaten flat trying to move it.
  • Folded and not stirred: a broad implement down through the middle, across the base, up and over, with the bowl turned between strokes.
  • Stopped the moment the streaks disappear. It is still visibly aerated and holds its shape briefly when the spoon lifts away.
  • Into the tin and into the oven straight away: a batter left standing on the bench gives back the air it was built for.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred until it looked perfectly smooth. Every stroke past the point where the streaks vanish knocks out air that cannot be put back, so the bake comes out dense and short of the tin, and often wet through the middle where there was no structure to hold it.

The wobble & the skewer: testing a bake 135 recipes

  • Tested at the true centre and the thickest point, not at the convenient edge: the edge is done long before the answer is.
  • The skewer comes out as the recipe describes, clean and dry or carrying the stated moist crumb, and it does the same on a second test in a different spot.
  • The wobble is read in motion: nudged, the centre moves as one set mass with a faint tremble, rather than a liquid ripple travelling across the surface.
  • The other signs agree with the test: risen and just shrinking from the sides, the top set and dry to a light touch with no wet gloss on it.
  • Pressed lightly at the middle it springs back and leaves no lasting dent.

Where it goes wrong It was judged on the clock and on how the edges looked, so a centre still liquid went to the rack and sank as it cooled, a dense wet seam under a perfectly browned top.

From the pass

The brownie is a hundred-year American argument between cakey and fudgy, and this recipe votes fudgy without apology: low flour, extra yolk, double chocolate. The two techniques that separate bakery brownies from box memories are the long sugar-egg whisk (the sole source of the shiny crackle crust) and the disciplined underbake-then-full-cool: a warm brownie is a delicious failure of nerve; a cooled one slices into the dense squares the argument was always about.
