Shorshe Ilish

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

gently hot · earthy · silky Leads with a warm current of chile, an earthy, grounding base, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 700g ilish (hilsa) steaks, cut 2cm thick, 6 pieces
  • 45g black mustard seeds (kalo shorshe)
  • 15g yellow mustard seeds
  • 6 kancha lonka (green chillies), 2 kept whole
  • 1 tsp salt, for the grinding jar
  • 90ml cold-pressed mustard oil
  • 1.5 tsp turmeric
  • 1.5 tsp salt
  • 150ml water

Method

  1. Rub the steaks with 1 tsp turmeric and 1 tsp salt and leave 15 minutes, until water beads stand on the cut faces and the flesh firms to the touch. 15 min
  2. Soak both mustards in warm water 20 minutes, drain, then grind with 4 green chillies, 1 tsp salt and 3 tbsp cold water in short pulses, stopping the moment the paste smells sharp and clean rather than dull. Push it through a sieve and discard the husk. 20 min
  3. Heat 75ml mustard oil in a kadai until the raw pungency flattens and a thin white wisp lifts off the surface, then take the pan off the flame for 30 seconds.
  4. Return it to a low flame, pour in the sieved paste with the water, remaining turmeric and salt, and cook 4 minutes until it thickens to a loose custard and oil beads yellow around the rim. Never let it gallop. 4 min
  5. Slide the fish in one layer, spoon gravy over each piece, cover and cook 7 minutes until the flesh parts from the backbone under a fork. Trail the last 15ml raw mustard oil and the whole chillies over, cover, and rest 5 minutes off the heat. 7 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Tempering a custard 33 recipes

  • The hot liquid went into the eggs a little at a time with constant whisking, and only then did everything go back to the pot.
  • The heat under it stayed gentle throughout and it was never allowed to boil.
  • Stirred continuously and right into the corners of the pan, where it thickens first and sets unnoticed.
  • Judged by behaviour: it thickens enough to coat the back of a spoon and hold a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • Taken off before it looks finished, and strained. Residual heat carries it the rest of the way, and a custard held on the heat until it looks right arrives past right.

Where it goes wrong The hot liquid went in too fast, or the pot went back over full heat to save time. The yolks set into threads before they could thicken smoothly, and a scrambled custard does not come back; straining hides a little of it and nothing hides the rest.

Straining & passing through a sieve 69 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

From the pass

Mustard pungency is made, not stored: crushing puts myrosinase onto sinigrin and the reaction wants cold water and a minute. Grind long and the jar warms, the volatile isothiocyanate blows off, and the husk gives up bitter phenols in its place. So soak the seeds, pulse briefly with salt and a green chilli, sieve out the husk, and never boil the gravy hard once the paste is in.
