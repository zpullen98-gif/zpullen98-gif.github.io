Pacific Islands · Starter

Kokoda

  • Easy
  • 25 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

bright · rich · fiery · silky Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, richness that coats and comforts, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 600 g walu (Spanish mackerel) or mahi mahi fillet, skinned and pin boned
  • 180 ml fresh lime juice, from about 8 limes
  • 10 g fine sea salt
  • 300 ml first pressing coconut cream
  • 1 red onion, 120 g, sliced paper thin
  • 2 ripe tomatoes, 200 g, seeded and cut into 1 cm dice
  • 1 small cucumber, 150 g, seeded and cut into 1 cm dice
  • 3 bird's eye chillies, deseeded and minced
  • 2 spring onions, thinly sliced
  • a small handful of coriander leaves

Method

  1. Cut the fish across the grain into 15 mm cubes so the pieces stay square instead of shredding, then toss with the salt and lime until every face is wet.
  2. Cure in the coldest part of the fridge until the cubes have gone opaque, then cut one in half and stop while the very centre is still faintly translucent.
  3. Drain the fish in a sieve and press it gently with the back of a spoon; throw away the milky lime, which is now watery and bitter.
  4. Fold through the coconut cream, onion, tomato, cucumber, chilli and spring onion, then taste for salt; it will take more than you expect.
  5. Scatter the coriander over and serve cold, in half coconut shells if you have them.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 56 recipes

  • It came off the heat BEFORE the target temperature, measured with a probe rather than by time, because carryover keeps driving the inside up after it is out.
  • The probe went into the coldest likely part rather than the easiest one to reach: the reading that matters is the lowest one, not the first one.
  • Rested for a time matched to its size, somewhere warm, and not cut into partway through to see how it is doing.
  • The board is nearly dry when it is finally cut. A spreading pool means it was cut too early, and that liquid should have stayed inside.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong It was cut straight off the heat, usually because everything else was ready. The muscle is still contracted and the juice still driven to the centre, so it runs out onto the board; the meat is drier than it ever needed to be, and the flavour that left it is sitting in a puddle under the knife.

Straining & passing through a sieve 148 recipes

  • Nothing solid got through: a finger drawn through what came out finds no fibre, seed, skin, curd, or grit.
  • Where the dish wants body it was worked through rather than left to drip, and the solids left in the mesh are spent and dry rather than still holding liquid worth having.
  • It is judged on the tongue rather than by eye: smooth, with no residual grain left behind after the mouthful has gone.
  • The mesh was fine enough for the job and held its shape, and nothing bypassed it by going over the rim.
  • The yield is close to what went in less the spent solids: not half of it abandoned in the sieve because it stopped running and nobody pushed.

Where it goes wrong It was pushed through a mesh too coarse for it, or the sieve was overloaded and slopped at the rim, so the fibres it was meant to remove are in the finished sauce and it reads grainy on the tongue.

From the pass

Lime does not cook the fish; the acid unfolds its proteins so they knit and go opaque, and it never stops. Twenty to thirty minutes is the window, and past about forty the cubes tighten to chalk. Drain hard before the cream goes in: leftover acid thins coconut cream and curdles it into oil and grit, so cure and dressing must never share a bowl.
