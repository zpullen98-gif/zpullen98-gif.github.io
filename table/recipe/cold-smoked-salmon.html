Cold-Smoked Salmon

  • Advanced
  • 45 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

smoky · gently hot · rich Leads with wood-smoke running underneath, a warm current of chile, on a rich, rounded finish.

Ingredients

  • 1.4 kg salmon side, skin on, pin bones pulled, held at -20C (-4F) for 7 days first
  • 42 g coarse sea salt (3% of the fish weight)
  • 28 g demerara sugar (2%)
  • 5 g black peppercorns, cracked
  • 2 large handfuls oak or alder smoking dust

Equipment

Sheet pan

Method

  1. Mix the salt, sugar and pepper, rub it over the flesh side, and cure skin down in a tray at 4C (39F): 24 hours for a thin side, 36 for a thick one. 600 min
  2. Rinse briefly, pat dry, then leave uncovered on a rack in the fridge for 12 hours until the surface turns tacky and faintly glossy and you can tap it without wetting a finger. 600 min
  3. Cold smoke below 30C (86F) for about 6 hours, in two sessions if the box warms up, until the flesh is the colour of old brass. 360 min
  4. Wrap and rest in the fridge for 48 hours so the smoke moves inward and stops smelling like a bonfire. 600 min
  5. Slice long and thin off the skin with the knife held almost flat.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Low & slow smoking 12 recipes

  • The smoke coming off is thin and blue, not white and rolling. White smoke is unburnt fuel and it deposits soot and acrid phenols; a fire drawing enough air to burn its own smoke is what perfumes rather than tars.
  • The chamber held its temperature within a narrow band for the whole cook, adjusted with the air intake rather than by adding fuel in armfuls. A pit swinging fifty degrees cooks the outside twice and the middle once.
  • The stall was expected and waited out or wrapped through deliberately, not chased with heat. A brisket that sits at 70C (158F) for three hours is losing exactly as much water as it needs to; the cook who turns the fire up renders it dry.
  • Doneness was judged by feel and by probe, never by the clock: the probe slides in with no resistance anywhere, and the number that matters is the one where the collagen has gone, not a time per kilo.
  • It rested long and warm, an hour or more, wrapped and out of any draught. Cut straight off the pit and the juice runs onto the board instead of staying in the slice.

Where it goes wrong The fire was fed for temperature rather than for combustion, so it smouldered: white smoke, a bitter deposit on the surface, and a swing in the chamber every time more fuel went on. Then the stall was read as a stall in progress rather than evaporation doing its work, the heat went up, and the meat finished dry with a hard grey band under the bark.

Resting dough: the pause that does the work 71 recipes

  • It actually rested, covered, for the time stated, not cut short because the rest of the schedule was tight.
  • It rolls and shapes without fighting: it stays where it is put, and it does not spring back or shrink on the bench between passes.
  • The surface is smooth and supple rather than dry, cracked, or skinned over.
  • It is at the temperature the next step needs: cold where the fat must stay solid, cool and relaxed where it must be rolled thin.
  • Baked, it holds the dimensions it was shaped to rather than shrinking back from the edges of the tin or the rim of the dish.

Where it goes wrong The rest was skipped or shortened, so the gluten was still tight: the dough fought the pin and sprang back thick and uneven, then shrank down the sides of the tin in the oven.

From the pass

Smoke sticks to a pellicle, the dry tacky protein film that forms in cold moving air. Smoke a wet fillet and the phenols dissolve in surface water and run off, leaving patchy colour and a sooty edge. Hold the box under 30C (86F): warmer and the proteins set and you have hot smoked fish instead. Lox is the other branch of the family, a salt brined belly cut, never smoked at all.
