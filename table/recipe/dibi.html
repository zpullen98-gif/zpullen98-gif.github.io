Dibi

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

bright · charred · smoky · fiery Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, char used as punctuation, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients

  • 1.4 kg lamb ribs and shoulder, cut into thick slabs on the bone
  • 20 g coarse salt
  • 1 tbsp black peppercorns, coarsely ground
  • 30 g strong Dijon mustard
  • 30 ml groundnut oil
  • 3 onions (450 g), sliced paper thin
  • 60 ml white vinegar
  • 1 scotch bonnet, deseeded and slivered
  • 1 lime
  • tapalapa or a plain baguette, to serve

Method

  1. Salt the lamb all over and leave it uncovered in the fridge for at least four hours so the surface dries and the salt works inward.
  2. Toss the onions with the vinegar, mustard, chilli and a pinch of salt and leave them to slacken while the fire burns down.
  3. Bank the charcoal to one side and wait until the coals are grey and you can hold a hand a palm's width above them for three seconds only.
  4. Rub the lamb with oil and pepper and lay it fat side down over the coals; the moment flames chase the meat, slide it to the bare grate and let it go on rendering there.
  5. Grill until the fat is crisp and translucent and the meat springs back at the thickest point, then rest it five minutes, cut across the grain and bury it under the vinegared onions while it is still hot.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Resting meat & slicing against the grain 56 recipes

  • It came off the heat BEFORE the target temperature, measured with a probe rather than by time, because carryover keeps driving the inside up after it is out.
  • The probe went into the coldest likely part rather than the easiest one to reach: the reading that matters is the lowest one, not the first one.
  • Rested for a time matched to its size, somewhere warm, and not cut into partway through to see how it is doing.
  • The board is nearly dry when it is finally cut. A spreading pool means it was cut too early, and that liquid should have stayed inside.
  • The grain was found and the knife went ACROSS it, so the fibres are cut short, and the blade was sharp enough that the slices hold together rather than tearing.

Where it goes wrong It was cut straight off the heat, usually because everything else was ready. The muscle is still contracted and the juice still driven to the centre, so it runs out onto the board; the meat is drier than it ever needed to be, and the flavour that left it is sitting in a puddle under the knife.

Grilling over live coals 77 recipes

  • The fire was ready before the food was: coals evenly covered in grey ash with no flames licking through and no raw smoke coming off, and a hot zone and a cooler one both available.
  • The grate was hot and clean when the food landed, and the food released cleanly from it when the time came to turn rather than tearing away.
  • Marks are browned, not burnt: deep colour where the metal touched, with no carbon flaking off and no soot transferred onto the food.
  • The interior reaches its target with a thin gradient, not charred outside and cold within, and not driven through by a flare-up.
  • What comes off the fire smells of fire and food only: no raw wood smoke, no fuel, nothing acrid at the back of the nose.

Where it goes wrong It went on over live flames instead of settled coals, so the outside charred bitter in the flare-ups while the middle stayed cold, and everything on the plate tastes of soot.

From the pass

Rib fat is the whole point and the whole danger. It renders from around 60C (140F) and drips, and dripping fat on live coals flares and lays soot on the meat that tastes of paraffin. Build the fire to one side, start fat down to get the render going, then move the slabs to the bare grate as soon as flames reach them. The vinegared onions go on hot meat so the acid cuts the fat before it sets.
