Levantine · Starter

Msabbaha

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

nutty-toasted · gently hot · bright · herb-fresh Leads with toasted, nutty warmth throughout, a warm current of chile, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 300 g dried chickpeas, soaked overnight
  • 5 g bicarbonate of soda
  • 180 g tahini, pale and pourable
  • 90 ml lemon juice
  • 80 ml iced water
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 8 g fine salt
  • 3 g ground cumin
  • 60 ml extra virgin olive oil
  • 5 g Aleppo pepper (pul biber)
  • a handful of flat leaf parsley, chopped

Allergen screen

Found: sesame

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Drain the soaked chickpeas, rub them with the bicarbonate in a dry pan over medium heat for 3 minutes until they squeak and smell toasted, then cover well with water and bring to a simmer. 3 min
  2. Cook, skimming off the loose skins and grey foam, until a chickpea crushes to paste between finger and thumb with no chalky core, 45 to 70 minutes; keep 200 ml of the cooking liquor. 70 min
  3. Pound the garlic to a paste with the salt, stir in the lemon juice and leave it 10 minutes, then whisk in the tahini: it will tighten into stiff putty. 10 min
  4. Add the iced water a spoonful at a time, whisking hard, until the paste loosens, pales to ivory and falls from the whisk in ribbons; season with the cumin.
  5. Fold two thirds of the warm chickpeas through the sauce, slacken with cooking liquor to a spoonable cream, then spread it out and top with the rest, the olive oil, Aleppo pepper and parsley.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Soaking dried goods back to life 29 recipes

  • Fully submerged with room to swell: the level is well above everything at the start and still above it at the end.
  • Given the full time, and judged by what is in the bowl rather than by the hours on the clock.
  • Tested at the CENTRE of the largest piece, cut or pressed. Anything with a hard dry core will still have one after cooking, because the outside softens first and stops the water getting further in.
  • The soaking water was dealt with on purpose: kept because it holds flavour, or thrown away because it holds what should not be eaten, but never simply left undecided.
  • Drained before it is used, so the dish does not silently gain all the water that came with it.

Where it goes wrong Too little water, or too little time. The pieces swell until they are packed tight against each other and the ones in the middle never fully take up water; they will not soften later in the dish either, so the finished plate has hard pieces scattered through it.

Skimming: foam, fat and a clear broth 35 recipes

  • It is held below a rolling boil for the whole time. Agitation drives fat and fine protein into the liquid as an emulsion, and nothing skimmed afterwards will take them back out.
  • The grey foam that rises in the first minutes is lifted off and discarded rather than stirred back in.
  • Fat is taken from the surface where it gathers, the pot moved off the centre of the heat if that is what it takes to collect it in one place.
  • The liquid is visibly clearer at the end than it was at the start: it is possible to see down into it.
  • Nothing lifted off goes back in. What was skimmed is discarded, not tipped back once the ladle is full.

Where it goes wrong The pot was allowed to boil, usually while something else was being attended to. Fat and protein emulsify into the liquid under agitation and cannot be lifted off afterwards, so the finished broth stays cloudy and greasy however patiently it is skimmed from then on.

From the pass

Tahini is a fat continuous paste. Adding acid or a trickle of water flips it to a water continuous emulsion, and at the crossover it seizes to putty. That stage is correct, not a failure, so keep whisking and keep adding water until it thins and pales; stop early and you serve cement. The bicarbonate raises the cooking pH so the skins dissolve and the pulses collapse; without it they stay chalky and the dish reads gritty.
