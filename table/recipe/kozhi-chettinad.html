Tamil & the South · Main

Kozhi Chettinad

  • Advanced
  • 55 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

warm-spiced · aromatic · earthy · smoky Leads with warm spice humming through the middle, aromatics blooming from the first bite, on a drift of smoke.

Ingredients

  • 1 kg chicken on the bone, cut into small pieces
  • 8 dried gundu chillies
  • 20 g coriander seeds
  • 10 g fennel seeds (sombu)
  • 8 g black peppercorns
  • 5 g cumin seeds
  • 2 g kalpasi (stone flower)
  • 1 star anise, 3 cm cassia, 4 cloves, 3 green cardamom
  • 60 g fresh grated coconut
  • 200 g small onions (chinna vengayam), sliced
  • 25 g ginger and 25 g garlic, pounded to a paste
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 60 ml gingelly oil
  • 3 sprigs curry leaves
  • 1 tsp turmeric
  • Salt

Equipment

Mortar & pestle · Grater / zester

Method

  1. Dry roast on low heat: coriander seeds until they crack and darken a shade, then fennel, pepper, cumin, cassia, cloves, cardamom and star anise for a minute until fragrant. Kill the heat and drop the kalpasi into the residual warmth for 10 seconds only, until it smells of woodsmoke.
  2. Roast the coconut separately, stirring without pause, until it is the colour of dark toast and smells nutty. Grind everything with a splash of water to a fine paste.
  3. Heat the gingelly oil and brown the small onions until collapsed and sweet, add the ginger garlic until the raw edge lifts, then the tomatoes until they break to a jam that pulls away from the pan.
  4. Fry the ground masala in that oil for 6 to 8 minutes, stirring, until the paste darkens and a rim of red oil pulls away at the sides of the pan. 8 min
  5. Add the chicken and turmeric, coat and sear 5 minutes until every piece is lacquered and no pink shows, then 100 ml water, lid on, 20 minutes until the meat pulls from the bone. 5 min
  6. Tear in the curry leaves and simmer uncovered until the gravy clings to the chicken instead of pooling under it.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Roasting in a hot oven 32 recipes

  • The oven was fully up to heat before anything went in, given time beyond the indicator light rather than judged by it.
  • Every piece has air around it. Nothing touches its neighbour and nothing is packed to the edge of the tray, because crowded food steams itself in its own moisture and never browns.
  • Colour is even across the whole tray, the tray having been turned at least once: no pale row at the back and no dark row at the front.
  • It came out BEFORE the target temperature, not at it. Carryover keeps driving the inside up by several degrees after it leaves the oven, and that rise is part of the cooking rather than an error in it.
  • The temperature was read at the coldest part rather than the easiest one to reach, and read again after the rest.

Where it goes wrong The tray was crowded, or the oven was not truly up to heat. The food releases steam faster than a cool box can carry it away, so it sits pale and slack, and the extra minutes spent chasing colour take the inside well past where it should have stopped.

Searing: the hard crust 113 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Kalpasi is the signature and the trap. It is a lichen, so its aroma sits in a resin that dissolves into fat but scorches to flat bitterness within seconds of dry heat: roast the coriander first, the fennel and pepper after, and give the kalpasi only the last few seconds off the flame. Roast the coconut to the colour of dark toast as well, because pale coconut grinds into a sweet thin gravy that never thickens.
