Vidalia Onion Casserole

  • Easy
  • 60 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · umami-deep · warm-spiced · silky Leads with richness that coats and comforts, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 4 large Vidalia onions, sliced thick
  • 55g butter
  • 250ml cream
  • 2 eggs
  • 100g grated Parmesan
  • 100g crushed crackers
  • 1/2 tsp nutmeg
  • Salt and white pepper

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Sweat the onions slowly in the butter until soft and sweet but not browned, 20 min; the sweetness of a Vidalia is the whole point. 20 min
  2. Whisk the cream, eggs, half the Parmesan, nutmeg, salt, and pepper.
  3. Spread the onions in a buttered dish and pour the custard over.
  4. Top with the cracker crumbs and remaining Parmesan.
  5. Bake at 175C for 30-35 min until set and golden. 35 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Tempering a custard 59 recipes

  • The hot liquid went into the eggs a little at a time with constant whisking, and only then did everything go back to the pot.
  • The heat under it stayed gentle throughout and it was never allowed to boil.
  • Stirred continuously and right into the corners of the pan, where it thickens first and sets unnoticed.
  • Judged by behaviour: it thickens enough to coat the back of a spoon and hold a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • Taken off before it looks finished, and strained. Residual heat carries it the rest of the way, and a custard held on the heat until it looks right arrives past right.

Where it goes wrong The hot liquid went in too fast, or the pot went back over full heat to save time. The yolks set into threads before they could thicken smoothly, and a scrambled custard does not come back; straining hides a little of it and nothing hides the rest.

Sweating aromatics: soft, never browned 198 recipes

  • No colour anywhere: tipped under the light the pieces are translucent to pale gold, with no tan edges, no brown tips, and no browned flecks suspended in the fat.
  • The pan is quiet: a low steady hiss, not a crackle. A crackle means the moisture has gone and browning has already started.
  • The largest piece pressed against the side of the pan collapses without resistance and with no crunch left at its centre.
  • The fat is still clear and loose in the pan and the pan floor is clean: nothing caught, nothing browned on to deglaze, because there was never any fond to build.
  • It tastes sweet and mild with no raw allium bite at the back of the throat, and none of the roasted sweetness that belongs to the next technique up the heat scale.

Where it goes wrong The heat was too high and nothing covered the pan, so the pieces took colour before they softened; the finished dish carries a roasty sweetness where it wanted a clean one, and the pieces are still firm at the centre under browned edges.

From the pass

Vidalias are sweet only because of the low-sulfur soil around Vidalia, Georgia; they are a legally protected crop. Cook them low and slow so their sugar comes forward without browning to bitterness.
