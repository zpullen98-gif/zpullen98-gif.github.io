Celeriac Baked in Salt Dough

  • Advanced
  • 30 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

earthy · bright · herb-fresh · nutty-toasted Leads with an earthy, grounding base, acid holding everything up to the light, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 1 celeriac, about 800 g, scrubbed hard, root hairs left on
  • 500 g plain flour
  • 250 g fine sea salt
  • 10 g thyme leaves
  • 260 ml cold water
  • 50 g hazelnuts
  • 45 ml cold pressed rapeseed oil
  • 15 ml sherry vinegar
  • 8 g Dijon mustard
  • 10 g flat parsley, chopped
  • black pepper

The skills inside

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · nuts · celery · mustard · alcohol

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Work the flour, salt and thyme with the water to a stiff, smooth dough, adding the last of the water only if it will not come together; rest it 15 minutes so it rolls without tearing. 15 min
  2. Roll to 8 mm, wrap the celeriac completely with no thin patches, pinch the seams and patch any split with a scrap: every crack is a steam leak.
  3. Bake at 200C (390F) for 2 to 2 hours 15 minutes, until a skewer pushed through the crust meets no resistance at the centre and comes out steaming. 120 min
  4. Rest the sealed parcel 20 minutes so the heat evens out, then crack the crust with the back of a knife and lift the celeriac clear, brushing off any salt that clings. 20 min
  5. Toast the hazelnuts until they smell of biscuit, crush them and whisk with oil, vinegar, mustard and parsley; peel the celeriac, cut into thick wedges and spoon the dressing over while hot.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 26 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

Steaming: basket, leaf and lid 79 recipes

  • Steam was running before the food went in: the water at a rolling boil and vapour coming steadily through the basket, rather than the whole thing warming up together.
  • Nothing touches the water at any point: the food sits clear of it throughout, and the level neither drops enough to boil dry nor rises enough to wet it.
  • The lid stayed on. Every lift costs the chamber its heat, and the number of lifts should be as close to none as the dish allows.
  • Lifted out, the surface is glossy and plump with its colour heightened rather than dulled: greens brighter than they went in, not olive.
  • Cooked through with the shape intact: it yields at the centre without having slumped or split, and there is no more than a spoonful of clean condensate beneath it.

Where it goes wrong The lid was loose or dripping and the condensate fell back onto the food, so it arrives waterlogged and pale with its seasoning washed down into the bottom of the dish.

From the pass

The salt crust seasons almost nothing: it is a sealed ceramic shell that turns the celeriac into its own steamer, holding it just above 100C in its own moisture so the sugars concentrate instead of leaching into a pan of water. A cracked crust vents that steam and the outer centimetre dries to cork, so patch every split before it goes in and rest the parcel before you break it open.
