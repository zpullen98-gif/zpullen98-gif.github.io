Senegalese · Main

Domoda

  • Intermediate
  • 35 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

bright · umami-deep · rich · fiery Leads with acid holding everything up to the light, savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients

  • 700 g beef chuck, in 4 cm cubes
  • 200 g unsweetened roasted groundnut paste
  • 80 g double concentrate tomato paste
  • 40 g tamarind pulp, soaked in 150 ml hot water and sieved
  • 2 onions (300 g), chopped
  • 60 ml groundnut oil
  • 1 scotch bonnet, left whole
  • 400 g pumpkin or butternut, in large chunks
  • 250 g sweet potato, in large chunks
  • 1 tsp netetou, crushed
  • 1 tbsp black pepper, coarsely ground
  • 1.2 litres water
  • salt

Method

  1. Brown the beef hard in the oil, then lift it out and soften the onion in the fat until translucent and sweet smelling.
  2. Stir in the tomato paste and netetou and fry until the paste goes brick dark and the oil runs clear at the edge of the pan.
  3. Return the beef with the water and the whole chilli. Slacken the groundnut paste in a ladle of the hot broth until it pours like cream, then stir it back in.
  4. Simmer uncovered, stirring the base every few minutes, until the meat gives to a spoon and orange oil beads across the surface.
  5. Add the pumpkin and sweet potato and cook until a knife slides through the squash, then stir in the tamarind liquor and salt and simmer five minutes more.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

Searing: the hard crust 141 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Groundnut paste is an emulsion of oil in roasted protein. Spooned straight into a hot pot it seizes into pellets, the oil breaks out and scorches on the base, and the stew tastes burnt for the rest of its life; slacken it with hot broth first. Hold the tamarind back too. Acid sets pectin, so squash added to an already sour pot stays firm however long it simmers.
