Carapulcra Limeña

  • Advanced
  • 40 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

warm-spiced · earthy · nutty-toasted · aromatic Leads with warm spice humming through the middle, an earthy, grounding base, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 300 g papa seca, dried potato
  • 600 g pork shoulder, in 3 cm dice
  • 60 g ají panca paste
  • 30 g ají amarillo paste
  • 80 g roasted peanuts, ground fine
  • 1 red onion, finely diced
  • 6 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 5 g ground cumin
  • 1 litre light stock
  • 45 ml neutral oil
  • 3 cloves
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 10 g salt

Method

  1. Toast the papa seca in a dry heavy pan over medium heat, shaking constantly, until it smells of popcorn and has gone a shade darker; tip it out at once, cover with warm water and leave it to swell.
  2. Brown the pork hard in the oil in the same pan until the cubes release from the metal on their own and the base is mahogany, then lift them out.
  3. Drop the heat, sweat the onion and garlic in the fat until soft, then add both ají pastes and cook, stirring, until the oil splits out of the paste and runs red.
  4. Return the pork with the cumin, cloves, bay and stock, cover, and hold at a bare tremble until the meat gives to the edge of a spoon.
  5. Drain the papa seca, stir it in with the ground peanuts and cook uncovered until the potato is tender and the pot holds the mark of the spoon; loosen with stock if it seizes.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Dry-pan toasting: spices, seeds and nuts 70 recipes

  • A single layer on dry metal with nothing added: no fat, and nothing else sharing the pan or the tray and competing for the heat.
  • It is kept moving: shaken, stirred, or turned. Anything left still scorches on the face touching the metal while the rest of it is barely warm.
  • It is judged by smell before sight: once it is fragrant there are seconds left, not minutes, and the nose reaches that point well before the colour does.
  • It leaves the pan the instant it is done, onto a cold surface. A hot pan keeps toasting whatever stays in it, and carryover here is the difference between done and ruined.
  • The colour is even and light rather than patchy, with nothing black at the edges: one scorched piece taints the whole quantity it is ground into.

Where it goes wrong It was left alone for a moment while something else was attended to. Dry heat has no margin: the pieces against the metal go from fragrant to acrid inside a minute, and the bitterness cannot be picked back out of a ground mixture.

Searing: the hard crust 141 recipes

  • The pan keeps a hard, steady sizzle from the moment the food lands and does not fall quiet; silence within the first half minute means the pan lost its heat and the surface is now steaming rather than browning.
  • It releases itself when tested: slide a thin edge under and it lifts without tearing. Anything that grips the pan has not formed a crust yet and is not ready to be moved.
  • Deep even brown across the whole face, edge to edge, when it is turned under the light: no pale ring left around a browned centre, and no black patches that taste bitter on their own.
  • The pan floor carries a dry browned layer that dissolves and lifts the moment liquid hits it: not a wet grey film, and not black flecks that stay put and stay bitter.
  • Cut into it: the browned band is thin, a few millimetres at most, sitting straight on top of correctly cooked interior; a thick grey collar under the crust means the heat was too low for too long.

Where it goes wrong The pan was not hot enough, or too much went in at once, so the food released its water and sat in it: the surface goes grey and steams, it sticks when moved, and the crust that eventually forms arrives over an interior already cooked through.

From the pass

Papa seca is potato boiled, peeled and sun dried. Toast it in a bare dry pan until it smells of popcorn before soaking, or it stays gritty and tastes of dust; give it an hour in warm water. It drinks three times its weight, so hold back stock to loosen the pot at the end. Fry the ají panca in fat over medium heat until the oil runs red: a dry hot pan scorches its sugars and the whole dish turns bitter.
