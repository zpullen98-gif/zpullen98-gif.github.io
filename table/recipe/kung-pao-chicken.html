Kung Pao Chicken

  • Intermediate
  • 30 min
  • Serves 4
  • $$

herb-fresh · nutty-toasted · fiery · bright Leads with fresh herbs arguing happily on top, toasted, nutty warmth throughout, with a clean, bright snap.

Ingredients

  • 500g chicken thigh, diced, velveted (1 tsp each soy, wine, cornstarch)
  • Dried chilies, 1 tsp Sichuan peppercorns
  • Roasted peanuts, scallion whites
  • Sauce: 1 tbsp each soy, black vinegar, sugar; 1 tsp dark soy; slurry

Method

  1. Sizzle chilies and peppercorns in oil until fragrant, not black.
  2. Add chicken; stir-fry until golden at the edges.
  3. Add scallions, then the sauce; it should thicken in seconds.
  4. Toss in peanuts off heat.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the technique it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Wok technique 26 recipes

  • Everything went in dry and went in in order. Wet protein or wet vegetables drop the wok below the heat that browns, and what follows is a stew in a round pan however hard the burner is working.
  • The wok stays loud from the first moment to the last. A wok that falls quiet has been overloaded, and the food in it is now boiling in its own liquid.
  • Every piece is coated and glossy with no pooled sauce in the bottom of the dish: the sauce should cling by reduction and starch, not sit under the food.
  • Vegetables keep their colour and a bite at the centre. Grey-green greens or a limp stem mean too long in the pan, and nothing later in the dish recovers them.
  • It carries the faint scorched note that only comes from food touching metal above about 200C, distinct from bitterness, which means the aromatics were burnt and is a different failure entirely.

Where it goes wrong Too much went in at once, or it went in wet. The wok loses its heat, the food releases water and simmers, the sauce thins instead of clinging, and the dish arrives pale and slack with liquid pooling under it.

From the pass

Kung pao is sweet, sour and numbing in balance: taste the sauce before it hits the wok, because there is no adjusting a stir-fry after the fact. The chilies are sizzled until fragrant and mahogany, never black; scorched chilies turn the whole dish acrid. Velveting the thigh meat in soy, wine and cornstarch is what keeps it silk under high heat. The dish carries a Qing governor's title (gongbao, palace guardian), and the name survived even the era when politics tried to erase it.
