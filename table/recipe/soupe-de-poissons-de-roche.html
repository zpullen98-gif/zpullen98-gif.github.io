Provence & the Midi · Soup

Soupe de Poissons de Roche

  • Advanced
  • 55 min
  • $$$$ roughly 4 of 4 for cost

floral · aromatic · briny · gently hot Leads with a floral thread lifting the aroma, aromatics blooming from the first bite, with warmth in the tail.

Ingredients

  • 2 kg small rock fish (rascasse, weever, small wrasse, gurnard, a green crab or two), gutted
  • 150 ml fruity olive oil
  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 1 fennel bulb, sliced, plus 1 tsp fennel seed
  • 6 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 800 g very ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 wide strips dried orange peel, white pith scraped away
  • 1 g saffron threads
  • 2 litres water
  • 1 tsp fine sea salt, plus more to finish
  • For the rouille: 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 waxy potato (150 g), boiled soft in the soup and lifted out
  • 1 egg yolk at room temperature
  • 1 small hot dried red chilli, soaked and skinned
  • 200 ml olive oil for the rouille
  • A pinch of saffron for the rouille
  • 1 thin baguette, sliced and dried out in the oven

Method

  1. Sweat the onions, fennel and garlic in the olive oil over low heat until translucent and quite without colour, then add the fennel seed and wait until the pan smells of aniseed.
  2. Raise the heat, turn the whole fish in the hot oil until the flesh whitens and begins to fall off the frames, then add tomatoes, orange peel and saffron and stir until the tomatoes slump.
  3. Pour on the water, bring it to a bare shiver and hold it there for 30 minutes; the surface should tremble, never roll. 30 min
  4. Pass everything, bones and all, through the coarse plate of a food mill, pressing until only dry pulp remains, then push that liquid through a conical sieve and salt it harder than feels polite.
  5. For the rouille, pound the garlic and chilli to a smooth paste with salt, work in the boiled potato and saffron, then the yolk, then dribble in the oil until it stands up around the pestle.
  6. Reheat the soup to just under a boil and serve it with the dried bread, letting each person melt rouille off a croûton into the bowl.

How to tell it is going right

  • Smooth to the degree the dish asks for, judged on the tongue rather than by eye: no lumps, no fibres, no grit, unless texture is the point.
  • No gluey elasticity: a spoonful drops cleanly off the spoon rather than stretching in a rope behind it.
  • The consistency holds: it mounds on a spoon and stays there, neither slumping flat nor standing so stiff that a spoon has to cut it.
  • Nothing separates on the plate: no water or fat weeping out into a ring within a minute of serving.

The bare simmer: holding liquid below the boil 183 recipes

  • The surface is read rather than assumed: bubbles break slowly in one or two places, or the surface merely shivers, and it does not reach a rolling boil at any point.
  • It was held there: the pot was watched and the heat adjusted rather than set once and left, and there is no tide mark up the pot wall from a boil-over.
  • The liquid stayed as clear as it should be: no clouding from agitation, and no fat churned into an emulsion through it.
  • Whatever is in the liquid is still intact: nothing shredded, broken up, or worried apart by movement.
  • The level is roughly where the elapsed time should have left it; a large drop is the evidence that it ran hotter than this while nobody was looking.

Where it goes wrong It was brought to a boil and then left on the same heat, so it churned for the whole cook: the liquid clouded, the fat emulsified into it, and the solids broke apart into the broth.

From the pass

Saffron, fennel and dried orange are the spine of this soup, not its garnish; without them you have made fish broth. The body is gelatine milled out of small bones, held in suspension by the oil, which is why a hard boil ruins it: it shears the fat out and the soup turns grey and greasy. Keep it shivering, and use a food mill, never a blender, which grinds bone to grit.
