Bengali · Main

Rui Macher Jhol

  • Intermediate
  • 35 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

earthy · gently hot · aromatic · nutty-toasted Leads with an earthy, grounding base, a warm current of chile, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 800g rui (rohu) steaks, cut 2cm thick
  • 2 tsp turmeric
  • 2 tsp salt
  • 90ml mustard oil
  • 1 tsp kalojeere (nigella seeds)
  • 4 kancha lonka (green chillies), slit
  • 300g potato, cut into thick lengthwise wedges
  • 200g potol (pointed gourd), peeled in stripes and halved
  • 1 tbsp ginger paste
  • 1.5 tsp ground cumin
  • 1 tsp ground coriander
  • 0.5 tsp Kashmiri chilli powder
  • 800ml hot water
  • a handful of coriander leaves

Method

  1. Rub the fish with 1.5 tsp turmeric and 1.5 tsp salt and leave 15 minutes until the surface goes tacky. 15 min
  2. Heat the mustard oil until the raw smell flattens and a haze lifts, then fry the steaks over a high flame, 2 minutes a side, and do not touch them until each piece releases itself from the pan and shows a set tan crust. Lift out. 2 min
  3. Fry the potato and potol in the same oil until the cut edges turn gold and translucent, then lift them out.
  4. Drop the kalojeere and slit chillies into the oil and wait for the seeds to crackle and the pan to smell of toasted onion, about 20 seconds. Add the ginger paste and ground spices with 2 tbsp water and fry until the oil separates and runs orange.
  5. Pour in the hot water with the vegetables and remaining salt, boil 10 minutes until a knife slides into the potato, then slide the fish back in and simmer 4 minutes until the jhol is thin, clear and faintly gold. Scatter coriander and rest 5 minutes. 10 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Tadka: blooming spices in fat 22 recipes

  • The fat is at tempering heat before anything goes in, around 180C (350F): one test seed dropped in surfaces and cracks within two or three seconds. A seed that sinks and sits quiet means the fat is cold and everything will steep rather than bloom; fat already hazing and thinning has gone past 200C (390F), and the first thing in will be black before the last thing is in.
  • Ask what went in and in what order, then read it back off the pan: the hardest seeds lead, with ten to twenty seconds before the next addition, and the softest seeds, any leaf and any powdered aromatic go last with only seconds left. Everything tipped in on one drop is a fail even when the pan smells right, because the most delicate thing in it cannot survive the time the hardest one needs.
  • The pan gave a hard scatter of popping that rose, peaked and thinned before it left the heat. A pan that stayed silent never reached popping heat and the seeds are still hard and raw against the teeth; seeds still cracking loudly as it is poured finished blooming in the dish instead of in the fat, where nothing was watching them.
  • Cumin is exactly one shade darker than it went in and no more, a warm even brown that smells nutty and sweet; anything near coffee colour, or smelling of scorch, has already carried bitterness into the fat. Fenugreek is pale gold, checked under the light with nothing copper or brown among it, and this is the mark to judge first: past gold it turns the whole pot bitter, and no salt, sugar or souring will pull it back.
  • The leaf is blistered and gone translucent, still green at the edge and crisp rather than black or limp, and it cracked sharply when it met the fat. It reached the dish still at full heat and answered with a violent hiss, and it reads on the surface afterwards as bright separate fat with whole spices suspended through it, not a dull dark sediment settled on the bottom.

Where it goes wrong The fat was pushed past hazing before anything went in, or every spice was tipped in together, so the hardest and the most delicate cooked on one clock: in the seconds the hardest seeds need to pop, fenugreek goes from gold to brown and the leaf goes black. Nothing can be corrected after that. The tempering pours in dark and smells scorched over the top of the dish, the specks read black rather than brown when the surface is turned to the light, and a flat acrid bitterness arrives at the back of the palate after the swallow and stays there through the next mouthful.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Carp is soft-fleshed and its connective tissue is slack, so raw steaks dropped into liquid shred and cloud the jhol. The turmeric and salt rub pulls surface moisture out, and hot oil then sets a thin denatured protein crust that holds the steak together through the simmer. The failure is impatience: move the fish before that crust forms and it tears and welds to the pan. Wait until it lets go by itself.
