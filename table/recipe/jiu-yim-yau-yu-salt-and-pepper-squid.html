Jiu Yim Yau Yu (Salt and Pepper Squid)

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

aromatic · fiery · warm-spiced · nutty-toasted Leads with aromatics blooming from the first bite, real fire that builds and stays, closing toasted and warm.

Ingredients

  • 600 g squid, cleaned, tubes opened flat and tentacles left whole
  • 20 ml Shaoxing wine
  • 5 g fine salt, for the squid
  • 80 g potato starch
  • 20 g cornflour
  • 1 litre groundnut oil, for frying
  • 6 cloves garlic, finely chopped
  • 3 red chillies, sliced
  • 2 green chillies, sliced
  • 4 spring onions, finely sliced
  • 8 g fine salt, for the seasoning
  • 6 g ground white pepper
  • 3 g five spice powder
  • 5 g caster sugar

Method

  1. Score the inside of the tubes in a 5 mm crosshatch two thirds of the way through, cut into 4 cm pieces, toss with the wine and salt for 10 minutes, then dry every piece hard between cloths until the surface is matte and takes a fingerprint. 10 min
  2. Toast the seasoning salt, white pepper, five spice and sugar in a dry pan for 40 seconds, until the pepper turns sharply floral, then tip it straight out so it stops cooking.
  3. Dredge the squid in the mixed potato starch and cornflour, then shake it in a sieve until only a chalk-thin coat is left clinging.
  4. Fry at 190C (375F) in two batches for 60 to 75 seconds, until the coating goes from white to pale gold and the pieces have curled and float high in the oil.
  5. Drain, then flash the garlic, chillies and spring onion in a nearly dry wok for 20 seconds until fragrant but uncoloured, return the squid, shower with the toasted seasoning and toss three times before it hits the plate.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Wok technique 28 recipes

  • Everything went in dry and went in in order. Wet protein or wet vegetables drop the wok below the heat that browns, and what follows is a stew in a round pan however hard the burner is working.
  • The wok stays loud from the first moment to the last. A wok that falls quiet has been overloaded, and the food in it is now boiling in its own liquid.
  • Every piece is coated and glossy with no pooled sauce in the bottom of the dish: the sauce should cling by reduction and starch, not sit under the food.
  • Vegetables keep their colour and a bite at the centre. Grey-green greens or a limp stem mean too long in the pan, and nothing later in the dish recovers them.
  • It carries the faint scorched note that only comes from food touching metal above about 200C, distinct from bitterness, which means the aromatics were burnt and is a different failure entirely.

Where it goes wrong Too much went in at once, or it went in wet. The wok loses its heat, the food releases water and simmers, the sauce thins instead of clinging, and the dish arrives pale and slack with liquid pooling under it.

Scoring 28 recipes

  • Lift one cut open with a fingertip: it parts through the skin and stops in the fat beneath, showing a pale wall at its base. Any cut showing red or fibrous flesh has gone too deep; any that has not reached the fat has left the skin tethered, and it will tighten under heat.
  • The lines run parallel at even spacing, roughly a finger's width apart, each carried the full length of the face and stopped the same margin short of the edge. Sight down them: lines that converge, wander or crowd at one end cook at different rates and read as careless on the plate.
  • Each line is one clean draw of the blade held at a slant, not a sawn or doubled track, and it leaves a bevelled lip standing slightly proud rather than a vertical slot. The surface should be dry and cold, near 4C (39F), when it is cut; worked warm and slack it drags, and the blade tears where it should part.
  • After cooking the cuts have opened and set, each a clear gap with firm browned walls. Lines closed back to faint scratches were cut too shallow or too straight down, and the surface has shrunk over them instead of through them.
  • The piece lies flat and true when it is set down, with no cupping through the middle and no lifted, curled edge. A bowed piece means the tightening surface was never released, so it pulled the whole thing up and left the underside pale where it stood off the heat.

Where it goes wrong The blade was pushed rather than drawn, through a surface left warm and slack, so it went past the fat and into the flesh. The cuts bleed as they cook and the juices flood the surface, so it steams instead of crisping: the lines gape into wet ragged trenches, the flesh between them dries into hard ridges, and the skin stays leathery and pale where it should have blistered and set.

From the pass

Squid has two edible windows: under about 2 minutes, or over 30. In between, the collagen in the mantle shrinks and squeezes the water out and the flesh goes to rubber. Frying at 190C (375F) keeps you inside the first window, but only if the squid is bone dry. Wet squid drops the oil temperature, so it sits far longer and crosses into the rubber zone while the crust is still pale.
