Ethiopian · Main

Dulet

  • Advanced
  • 35 min
  • $$$ roughly 3 of 4 for cost

fiery · rich · sweet-edged · warm-spiced Leads with real fire that builds and stays, richness that coats and comforts, fading in warm spice.

Ingredients

  • 250 g beef honeycomb tripe
  • 250 g lamb liver
  • 250 g lean beef, top round
  • 60 g niter kibbeh
  • 150 g red onion, chopped very fine
  • 8 g mitmita
  • 3 g korerima (Ethiopian cardamom), ground
  • 20 ml lemon juice
  • 5 g fine salt
  • 1 green chilli, sliced
  • 4 sheets injera, to serve

Method

  1. Scrub the tripe with salt and lemon, rinse it, then simmer it in fresh water until a knife enters without that faint squeak, about 90 minutes, and cool it before you cut it. 90 min
  2. Hand-chop the tripe, liver and beef separately to an even 5 mm dice; a machine smears the liver and the dish loses its grain.
  3. Sweat the onion in the niter kibbeh over low heat until it collapses and the butter runs clear at the edge of the pan.
  4. Raise the heat, add the beef and tripe and stir until the beef loses its red, then the liver for one final minute, taking everything to 71C (160F).
  5. Off the heat, fold in the mitmita, korerima, salt, lemon and chilli, because those spices scorch and turn bitter in a hot dry pan.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

  • Take any three pieces from anywhere in the pile: they match closely enough in every dimension that you would have to measure them to argue otherwise.
  • The cut faces are flat and clean rather than ragged or crushed; leaves and herbs are cut through, not bruised, and the board carries no dark smear where they were.
  • The stated size is the actual size, held against a rule or a known reference rather than called about right.
  • Everything in the pile is the shape that was asked for: no wedges, no half pieces, and no offcuts swept in to avoid waste.
  • They cook evenly, which is the entire point: at the end of cooking nothing is still raw and nothing has collapsed.

Where it goes wrong The knife was dull, or the vegetable was never squared off first, so the pieces vary: the small ones are mush by the time the large ones are cooked, and the dish has two textures where it wanted one.

  • No colour anywhere: tipped under the light the pieces are translucent to pale gold, with no tan edges, no brown tips, and no browned flecks suspended in the fat.
  • The pan is quiet: a low steady hiss, not a crackle. A crackle means the moisture has gone and browning has already started.
  • The largest piece pressed against the side of the pan collapses without resistance and with no crunch left at its centre.
  • The fat is still clear and loose in the pan and the pan floor is clean: nothing caught, nothing browned on to deglaze, because there was never any fond to build.
  • It tastes sweet and mild with no raw allium bite at the back of the throat, and none of the roasted sweetness that belongs to the next technique up the heat scale.

Where it goes wrong The heat was too high and nothing covered the pan, so the pieces took colour before they softened; the finished dish carries a roasty sweetness where it wanted a clean one, and the pieces are still firm at the centre under browned edges.

From the pass

Tripe and liver run on opposite clocks. Tripe is smooth muscle sheathed in collagen and wants 90 minutes of wet heat; liver sets in under a minute and then turns to sand. Cooking the tripe ahead and cutting everything to the same 5 mm dice is what lets three minutes in the pan finish the dish. Niter kibbeh is clarified, so it takes that heat without milk solids burning. Hand-chopped meat is cut surfaces throughout, so it goes to 71C (160F), not the traditional barely warm.
