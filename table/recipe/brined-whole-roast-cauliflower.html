Brined Whole Roast Cauliflower

  • Intermediate
  • 25 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

umami-deep · tangy-ferment · briny · fiery Leads with savory depth stacked from the pantry’s bass notes, ferment tang with real depth behind it, on a slow, honest burn.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole cauliflower, 900 g to 1.1 kg, leaves trimmed, stem left whole
  • 2 litres water
  • 100 g fine salt
  • 20 g caster sugar
  • 2 bay leaves
  • 8 g coriander seed
  • 60 ml olive oil
  • 25 g white miso
  • 15 ml lemon juice
  • 3 g Aleppo pepper or mild chilli flakes

Method

  1. Bring the water, salt, sugar, bay and coriander seed to the boil in a pot deep enough to submerge the head, then lower the cauliflower in stem down and weight it with a small plate so it stays under.
  2. Simmer gently 12 to 15 minutes, until a thin knife slides into the thickest part of the stem with only slight resistance and the florets have gone from chalk white to faintly translucent. 15 min
  3. Lift it out onto a rack, stem up so the hollows drain, and leave 20 minutes until the surface is dry to the back of a finger; wet cauliflower will steam, not colour. 20 min
  4. Rub all over with 40 ml of the olive oil, set on a tray and roast at 220C (425F) for 30 to 40 minutes, turning once, until the florets are mahogany with black tips. 40 min
  5. Whisk the miso, lemon, remaining oil and chilli, brush over the head for the last 8 minutes only, and carve at the table in thick vertical slabs. 8 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Roasting in a hot oven 56 recipes

  • The oven was fully up to heat before anything went in, given time beyond the indicator light rather than judged by it.
  • Every piece has air around it. Nothing touches its neighbour and nothing is packed to the edge of the tray, because crowded food steams itself in its own moisture and never browns.
  • Colour is even across the whole tray, the tray having been turned at least once: no pale row at the back and no dark row at the front.
  • It came out BEFORE the target temperature, not at it. Carryover keeps driving the inside up by several degrees after it leaves the oven, and that rise is part of the cooking rather than an error in it.
  • The temperature was read at the coldest part rather than the easiest one to reach, and read again after the rest.

Where it goes wrong The tray was crowded, or the oven was not truly up to heat. The food releases steam faster than a cool box can carry it away, so it sits pale and slack, and the extra minutes spent chasing colour take the inside well past where it should have stopped.

Brining & curing 88 recipes

  • Fully committed either way: in a wet brine nothing floats clear of the liquid, and in a dry cure every face carries salt, seams and cavities included.
  • The time was measured against the weight and the thickness rather than the recipe’s convenience, and it stayed cold for all of it.
  • The change is visible and can be felt: the flesh is firmer than raw and its colour has deepened or gone more translucent, rather than still being slack and pale.
  • Rinsed and dried as the recipe asks, and anything meant to crisp afterwards has a surface dry and tacky to the touch before it goes near heat.
  • Cooked and tasted, it is seasoned right through to the centre rather than merely salty at the surface, and not so salty that the dish cannot be eaten.

Where it goes wrong It sat in the brine well past its time because a few extra hours seemed harmless; the surface is over-salted and the texture has gone from firm to spongy and wet, and no cooking takes salt back out.

From the pass

A whole head cannot be salted from the inside once it is in the oven, so the seasoning has to arrive by diffusion: a 5% brine, simmered, carries salt to the core and softens the pectin there. Dry heat alone blackens the outer florets while the stem stays squeaky and raw. Cook it through in the brine, then dry the surface properly. The miso goes on late because it is mostly sugar and salt and will burn.
