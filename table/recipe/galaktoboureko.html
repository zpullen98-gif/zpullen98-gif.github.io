Greek · Dessert

Galaktoboureko

  • Intermediate
  • 45 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost
  • Vegetarian

rich · bright · floral · silky Leads with richness that coats and comforts, acid holding everything up to the light, melting away silken.

Ingredients

  • 1 litre whole milk
  • 150 g fine semolina
  • 200 g caster sugar
  • 4 eggs
  • 100 g unsalted butter
  • 1 vanilla pod, split
  • zest of 1 lemon
  • 450 g phyllo pastry
  • 200 g unsalted butter, melted, for the sheets
  • 400 g caster sugar, for the syrup
  • 300 ml water
  • 1 wide strip lemon peel
  • 15 ml lemon juice

Equipment

Grater / zester

Method

  1. Boil the 400 g sugar, water and lemon peel for 5 minutes, stir in the lemon juice and leave the syrup to go completely cold. 5 min
  2. Bring the milk with the vanilla and zest to a simmer, rain in the semolina while whisking, cook 5 minutes to a thick trail, then beat in the 100 g butter and 200 g sugar. 5 min
  3. Cool the semolina to 60C (140F), then whisk in the eggs one at a time so they thicken it instead of setting into threads.
  4. Butter seven phyllo sheets into a 30 cm by 22 cm tin at least 5 cm deep, overhang hanging out, pour in the custard, fold the overhang back, layer seven more buttered sheets on top and score the top layers into diamonds.
  5. Bake at 180C (350F) for 50 minutes to 55 minutes until deep gold and puffed, then pour the cold syrup over the pastry straight from the oven and leave it 4 hours. 50 min

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Scoring 28 recipes

  • Lift one cut open with a fingertip: it parts through the skin and stops in the fat beneath, showing a pale wall at its base. Any cut showing red or fibrous flesh has gone too deep; any that has not reached the fat has left the skin tethered, and it will tighten under heat.
  • The lines run parallel at even spacing, roughly a finger's width apart, each carried the full length of the face and stopped the same margin short of the edge. Sight down them: lines that converge, wander or crowd at one end cook at different rates and read as careless on the plate.
  • Each line is one clean draw of the blade held at a slant, not a sawn or doubled track, and it leaves a bevelled lip standing slightly proud rather than a vertical slot. The surface should be dry and cold, near 4C (39F), when it is cut; worked warm and slack it drags, and the blade tears where it should part.
  • After cooking the cuts have opened and set, each a clear gap with firm browned walls. Lines closed back to faint scratches were cut too shallow or too straight down, and the surface has shrunk over them instead of through them.
  • The piece lies flat and true when it is set down, with no cupping through the middle and no lifted, curled edge. A bowed piece means the tightening surface was never released, so it pulled the whole thing up and left the underside pale where it stood off the heat.

Where it goes wrong The blade was pushed rather than drawn, through a surface left warm and slack, so it went past the fat and into the flesh. The cuts bleed as they cook and the juices flood the surface, so it steams instead of crisping: the lines gape into wet ragged trenches, the flesh between them dries into hard ridges, and the skin stays leathery and pale where it should have blistered and set.

Tempering a custard 59 recipes

  • The hot liquid went into the eggs a little at a time with constant whisking, and only then did everything go back to the pot.
  • The heat under it stayed gentle throughout and it was never allowed to boil.
  • Stirred continuously and right into the corners of the pan, where it thickens first and sets unnoticed.
  • Judged by behaviour: it thickens enough to coat the back of a spoon and hold a clean line when a finger is drawn through it.
  • Taken off before it looks finished, and strained. Residual heat carries it the rest of the way, and a custard held on the heat until it looks right arrives past right.

Where it goes wrong The hot liquid went in too fast, or the pot went back over full heat to save time. The yolks set into threads before they could thicken smoothly, and a scrambled custard does not come back; straining hides a little of it and nothing hides the rest.

From the pass

Two temperatures decide this. The eggs go into semolina below 65C (150F), because above that they set on contact and you get threads running through the custard. And the syrup must be cold onto hot pastry: steam is still leaving the phyllo and pushing outward while the cool syrup soaks in, so the leaves stay crisp. Hot syrup on hot pastry, or cold on cold, gives you paste.
