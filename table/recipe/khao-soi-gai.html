Khao Soi Gai

  • Intermediate
  • 60 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

rich · aromatic · gently hot · umami-deep Leads with richness that coats and comforts, aromatics blooming from the first bite, finishing long and savory.

Ingredients

  • 4 chicken legs or 6 thighs, bone-in
  • The paste: 3 tbsp red curry paste + 1 tbsp curry powder + ½ tsp turmeric + 2 crushed cardamom pods (the Burmese accent) + minced ginger
  • 800ml coconut milk (thick cream separated and reserved) + 400ml chicken stock; 2 tbsp fish sauce, 1 tbsp palm sugar
  • 500g fresh egg noodles: two-thirds boiled for the bowls, ONE-THIRD DEEP-FRIED into crispy nests for the crowns
  • The ritual plate: pickled mustard greens, shallot wedges, lime, roasted chili oil (naam prik pao)

Method

  1. Fry the paste in the thick coconut cream until the oil splits red and fragrant.
  2. Chicken in, turned in the paste; add remaining coconut milk and stock; simmer 30–35 min until tender; season with fish sauce and palm sugar: the broth should be rich, gently sweet, curry-deep. 35 min
  3. Fry a third of the raw noodles in hot oil until puffed and golden; drain.
  4. Boil the rest; bowl them; chicken and broth over; crown with the crispy nest. Serve the pickle-shallot-lime-chili plate alongside: each eater cuts the richness to taste.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Pounding a curry paste 23 recipes

  • Smeared thin across a pale surface it reads as one substance, edge to edge: no pale gritty specks standing proud of the smear, no shard that drags a line through it as the smear is pulled. Flecks mean something hard went in after something wet and skated instead of shattering.
  • Rubbed between finger and thumb it feels like wet clay, dense and smooth, and takes the print of the thumb. Anything that rolls into a hard grain or grits like sand has not been broken down, and the order of work was wrong.
  • The fibrous aromatics have surrendered: pinch a little and pull, and nothing draws out as a thread or a hair. Listen to the last minutes of work as well, which should be a dull wet slap in the mortar; a dry knock or rattle still present means hard pieces are being chased around rather than crushed.
  • One even colour throughout when it is turned under the light, deep and saturated, with no marbling and no streak in which a single component can still be read. A dull grey cast over the surface says it was worked long past its point.
  • Heaped up and left alone it stands in a soft peak and holds the mark of the pestle for a full minute: no clear or coloured liquid creeps out at the base, no oily film separates on top. It should also come to the pass cool, no warmer than about 25 C (77 F); a mortar warm to the back of the hand means friction has driven off the top aroma and the paste will smell flat.

Where it goes wrong Everything went into the mortar together, or the wet ingredients arrived before the hard, dry ones had been reduced to dust, so the moisture cushioned every blow and the hard pieces slid rather than broke. What comes to the pass is a wet mince rather than a paste: hard flecks and fibrous threads through a loose slurry, liquid weeping out of the heap within a minute, colour marbled instead of single, and on the plate it reads as separate raw items sitting next to each other rather than one flavour.

Frying the paste until the oil splits 95 recipes

  • It is being fried, not simmered: the paste is in fat over live heat and moving, holding a steady sizzle throughout rather than bubbling as a wet mass.
  • The colour has deepened and darkened from raw to cooked, and the volume has visibly reduced and tightened.
  • The split, the signal the whole technique is named for, is unmistakable when it comes: clear fat separates out and pools at the edges and on the surface, and the paste pulls away from the pan floor in one mass.
  • The smell has turned from sharp and raw to round and fragrant, with no raw bite left from any of the aromatics that went into it.
  • Nothing has caught: no black specks through the paste, no bitterness, and a pan floor that is browned but not burnt.

Where it goes wrong It was taken off as soon as it smelled good, before the fat separated; the raw edge of the aromatics is still in it, and the finished dish tastes thin and sharp no matter how long it simmers afterwards.

From the pass

Chiang Mai's signature: a Burmese-Yunnanese caravan dish that settled in the north and became the city's one-bowl résumé. The soft-and-crispy double noodle is the identity: the fried nest wilts slowly into the coconut broth, giving every stage of the bowl a different texture. The sour pickled greens and raw shallot are structural, not optional; khao soi without them is sweet and unfinished.
