Sarmale în Foi de Varză Murată

  • Intermediate
  • 55 min
  • $$ roughly 2 of 4 for cost

smoky · gently hot · rich · herb-fresh Leads with wood-smoke running underneath, a warm current of chile, lifted green at the very end.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole soured cabbage, about 1.5 kg (varza murata)
  • 600 g pork shoulder, coarsely minced, about a quarter fat
  • 100 g short grain rice
  • 250 g onion, finely diced
  • 60 g lard
  • 15 g sweet paprika
  • 2 tsp dried thyme
  • 3 tbsp chopped dill
  • 300 g smoked pork ribs
  • 500 ml tomato passata
  • 500 ml water or cabbage brine
  • 3 bay leaves
  • 1 tsp black peppercorns
  • salt and black pepper

Method

  1. Peel the cabbage into whole leaves and taste one; if it is fiercely salty, soak the leaves in cold water for 20 minutes. Shave the thick spine off each leaf so it will roll. 20 min
  2. Sweat the onion in the lard until translucent and sweet, pull the pan off the heat, stir the paprika through for 20 seconds, and let it cool.
  3. Mix the pork, rice, cooled onion, thyme, dill and pepper by hand until just combined; the rice should read as a scatter through the meat, not a mass.
  4. Roll walnut sized amounts in the leaves, snug but never compressed, tucking the ends in. Line the pot with shredded cabbage and the smoked ribs, then pack the rolls seam down in tight layers.
  5. Add the passata, brine, bay and peppercorns to just cover, weight with an upturned plate, and hold at the barest tremble on the hob or at 150C (300F) for about 3 hours, until a leaf yields to the side of a spoon with no resistance. 180 min
  6. Cool them in the pot and reheat the next day; the liquid should taste rounded rather than sharp.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Folding: keeping the air in 34 recipes

  • The air was actually built first and it shows: whatever was creamed or whipped has changed colour and grown in volume, pale and light rather than dense.
  • The two mixtures were brought closer before being combined: a little of the light one worked into the heavy one first, so the rest is not beaten flat trying to move it.
  • Folded and not stirred: a broad implement down through the middle, across the base, up and over, with the bowl turned between strokes.
  • Stopped the moment the streaks disappear. It is still visibly aerated and holds its shape briefly when the spoon lifts away.
  • Into the tin and into the oven straight away: a batter left standing on the bench gives back the air it was built for.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred until it looked perfectly smooth. Every stroke past the point where the streaks vanish knocks out air that cannot be put back, so the bake comes out dense and short of the tin, and often wet through the middle where there was no structure to hold it.

Brining & curing 88 recipes

  • Fully committed either way: in a wet brine nothing floats clear of the liquid, and in a dry cure every face carries salt, seams and cavities included.
  • The time was measured against the weight and the thickness rather than the recipe’s convenience, and it stayed cold for all of it.
  • The change is visible and can be felt: the flesh is firmer than raw and its colour has deepened or gone more translucent, rather than still being slack and pale.
  • Rinsed and dried as the recipe asks, and anything meant to crisp afterwards has a surface dry and tacky to the touch before it goes near heat.
  • Cooked and tasted, it is seasoned right through to the centre rather than merely salty at the surface, and not so salty that the dish cannot be eaten.

Where it goes wrong It sat in the brine well past its time because a few extra hours seemed harmless; the surface is over-salted and the texture has gone from firm to spongy and wet, and no cooking takes salt back out.

From the pass

Rice takes up around two and a half times its weight in liquid, so a heavy hand bursts the rolls and still leaves a dry pellet inside. The soured leaf sets the other limit: lactic acid slows pectin from breaking down, so soured cabbage wants a far longer, gentler cook than a fresh leaf. Let it boil and the rolls split and the fat breaks out greasy. Barest tremble, a weight on top, a night to settle.
