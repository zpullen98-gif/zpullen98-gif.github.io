Mississippi Roast

  • Intermediate
  • 5 h
  • Serves 4
  • $$$

rich · herb-fresh · aromatic Leads with richness that coats and comforts, fresh herbs arguing happily on top, opening aromatic and staying there.

Ingredients

  • 1.8kg chuck roast
  • 3 tbsp flour
  • 2 tbsp oil
  • 1 packet ranch seasoning (or 2 tbsp dried herbs, buttermilk powder, garlic)
  • 1 packet au jus mix (or 2 tbsp beef base and cornstarch)
  • 115g butter
  • 8 pepperoncini peppers, plus 60ml of their brine
  • Black pepper

Equipment

Dutch oven

Allergen screen

Found: gluten · dairy

Screened for: gluten, dairy, egg, nuts, peanuts, fish, shellfish, molluscs, soy, sesame, celery, mustard, lupin, alcohol. Not screened: sulphites.

Method

  1. Dust the roast with flour and sear all sides hard in the oil in a Dutch oven.
  2. Scatter the ranch and au jus seasonings over the meat.
  3. Lay the stick of butter on top and arrange the pepperoncini and their brine around.
  4. Cover and braise at 150C for 4 hours, or in a slow cooker on low 8 hours, until it shreds. 240 min
  5. Shred the meat, stir it through the buttery pepper juices, and serve on rolls or over mashed potatoes.

How to tell it is going right

This dish has no standard of its own yet. It is judged on the techniques it exercises: checked at the pan, while there is still something to be done about it.

Braising 35 recipes

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

Brining & curing 67 recipes

  • Fully committed either way: in a wet brine nothing floats clear of the liquid, and in a dry cure every face carries salt, seams and cavities included.
  • The time was measured against the weight and the thickness rather than the recipe’s convenience, and it stayed cold for all of it.
  • The change is visible and can be felt: the flesh is firmer than raw and its colour has deepened or gone more translucent, rather than still being slack and pale.
  • Rinsed and dried as the recipe asks, and anything meant to crisp afterwards has a surface dry and tacky to the touch before it goes near heat.
  • Cooked and tasted, it is seasoned right through to the centre rather than merely salty at the surface, and not so salty that the dish cannot be eaten.

Where it goes wrong It sat in the brine well past its time because a few extra hours seemed harmless; the surface is over-salted and the texture has gone from firm to spongy and wet, and no cooking takes salt back out.

From the pass

A viral 2010s recipe from Ripley, Mississippi, and genuinely good despite the packet mixes. The pepperoncini brine is the secret: it cuts the butter and richness with just enough tang to keep the whole thing from being heavy.
