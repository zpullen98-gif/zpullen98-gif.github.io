The French Board

After this you can Bloomy and washed rind on their home ground: you can warn a table that Époisses smells worse than it tastes, explain Morbier's ash line, and stop a guest cutting the nose off a wedge of Comté.

  1. Brie de Meaux Cheese Atlas

    THE brie, raw cow's milk, AOP, from the Île-de-France: a wide wheel under a bloomy Penicillium candidum rind, ripening from a chalky core outward to a supple, mushroomy, faintly ammoniated cream at peak. The supermarket 'brie' bears the name the way a postcard bears Paris. Serve at full room temperature (a cold brie is a mute brie); the rind is part of the composition. À point (perfectly ripe, bulging at the cut but not running away) is the cheesemonger's phrase worth learning and the moment worth waiting for.

  2. Camembert de Normandie Cheese Atlas

    Brie's smaller, wilder Norman cousin. Raw milk, AOP, ladled by hand into its iconic poplar box, which is not packaging but equipment: bake the whole thing in it (rind scored, garlic and thyme tucked in) and the box becomes a fondue vessel. Deeper, funkier, more cabbage-and-mushroom than brie's polite cream. The pasteurized export version is a different, tamer animal. With Normandy cider or calvados it is a regional sentence completing itself.

  3. Roquefort Cheese Atlas

    The monarch of blues: raw SHEEP'S milk, cave-aged in the natural fissures of Mont Combalou where Penicillium roqueforti lives in the air, AOP since 1925 (France's first). Ivory paste, emerald-blue veining, a flavor that is salty, spicy, and lanolin-sweet all at once. Sheep's fat melts below body temperature, which is why Roquefort dissolves on the tongue where cow blues crumble. Butter it onto walnut bread, melt it over steak, or pair it with Sauternes, the salt-sweet pairing that defines the whole category.

  4. Comté Cheese Atlas

    France's most-eaten AOP cheese and the connoisseur's desert-island pick: raw cow's milk from Jura mountain herds, cooked-curd wheels aged 4 to 36+ months in fort-like caves. Young Comté is milky-hazelnut; old Comté goes broth, toffee, and tropical fruit with tyrosine crunch: one name spanning a whole flavor journey, which is why affineurs sell it by age and fruitiness scores. Melts impeccably (fondue's backbone with Gruyère), boards impeccably, cooks impeccably. If a cheese program stocks one Alpine wheel, this is the argument.

  5. Époisses Cheese Atlas

    Burgundy's infamous washed-rind: bathed in marc de Bourgogne brandy until the rind glows terra-cotta and the aroma precedes it into the room (the 'banned on French trains' legend is folklore, and the cheese has never denied it). Inside: a spoonable, savory, saline custard that converts everyone brave enough for a first taste. Serve with a spoon, crusty bread, and Burgundy, or the counterintuitive classic, a sweet-edged white. The loudest smells in cheese hide the gentlest hearts; Époisses is the proof text.

  6. Reblochon Cheese Atlas

    Savoie's supple raw-milk wheel, born of tax evasion: farmers 're-milked' (reblocher) after the assessor left, and the rich second milking became this: nutty, fruity, gently funky under a rosy washed rind. It exists in the global mind for one dish: TARTIFLETTE: potatoes, lardons, onions, and a whole Reblochon melted over the top, the ski-lodge casserole that justifies winter. Export versions are pasteurized; in Savoie, the real thing tastes of the alpage. Melts like it was designed for it, because it was.

  7. Morbier Cheese Atlas

    The cheese with the black line: Jura farmers once protected morning curds with a layer of ash until evening curds topped the wheel; today the vegetal-ash stripe is signature, not necessity. Supple, morning-milk mild with a hay-and-fruit nose that outpaces its gentle taste. The stripe makes it a cheese-board storyteller and the raclette family's arty cousin (it melts beautifully). A reminder that half of cheese history is farmers solving yesterday's problem so deliciously it became tradition.

  8. Crottin de Chavignol Cheese Atlas

    The Loire's little goat drum: sold at every age from fresh-and-lemony to aged, dense, and piercingly goaty; one cheese teaching the entire chèvre aging curve. Warm it on toast over bitter greens (chèvre chaud) and you have written a French bistro's most-ordered salad. Its terroir marriage is exact: Sancerre, same village hillsides, sauvignon blanc's cut against lactic tang, the textbook 'what grows together goes together.' The Loire's other goats (Valençay's ash pyramid, Sainte-Maure's straw-pierced log) are siblings worth boarding beside it.

  9. Munster Cheese Atlas

    Alsace's washed-rind heavyweight (no relation whatsoever to the mild American 'muenster' that borrowed the name and dropped the character): monk-made origins, brine-washed to a sticky orange rind, with a barnyard boom and a supple, sweet-milk interior. Locals eat it the peasant way: with boiled potatoes and cumin or caraway seeds, and a Gewürztraminer whose lychee sweetness meets the funk head-on, one of the great regional pairings in all of cheese. Serve it last on the board; nothing follows Munster.
