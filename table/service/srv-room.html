The Room's Vocabulary

After this you can You can act on "86 the halibut", say "behind" without thinking, and name who is working the pass. The guide keeps MENU FORMAT and SERVICE SCHOOL as two separate axes (a room runs one of each, not one of seven) so you can state both for your room. It does not teach ticket anatomy: 'dupe' is glossed in four words inside a single definition, and there is no fire or all-day sequencing anywhere in the guide, so reading a ticket is something your room teaches you and this track does not pretend to.

  1. Service Styles & Front-of-House Fluency The Professional Kitchen

    À LA CARTE: dishes priced and fired individually. PRIX FIXE / TABLE D'HÔTE: set menu, set price. TASTING MENU (dégustation): the chef's sequenced argument, wine pairings optional. FAMILY-STYLE: platters to share. The historic service schools: FRENCH (finished tableside from the guéridon, crêpes Suzette's home), RUSSIAN (plated individually and served formally, what most fine dining actually does), AMERICAN (plated in the kitchen, served directly; the modern default). Front-of-house vocabulary a chef-owner must speak: covers, turns (seatings per table per night), the dupe (order ticket), comps and voids, mise of the dining room (silver, glassware, the greeting). The kitchen makes the food; the room makes the restaurant.

  2. Line Slang: 86, In the Weeds & Family Meal The Professional Kitchen

    '86': an item is dead: out of stock or pulled; write it on the board, tell the floor, engineer the menu so it rarely happens on your top seller. 'IN THE WEEDS': buried past capacity; the professional response is triage and communication, never silence. 'BEHIND / SHARP / HOT': the safety chorus of moving bodies. 'COVERS': guests served (the night's scoreboard). 'STAGE' (stahj): an unpaid/trial kitchen apprenticeship; 'trail': a working interview. 'FAMILY MEAL': the staff dinner before service: economically, use-it-up cooking; culturally, the truest read on how a restaurant treats its people. Restaurateurs are judged by their family meal more than their tasting menu, by the people who matter.

  3. The Pass & Expediting The Professional Kitchen

    THE PASS is the counter where plates leave the kitchen and the chef's standards are enforced one plate at a time; whoever runs it (chef or EXPEDITER/'expo') is air-traffic control: calling orders, sequencing stations so a table's dishes land together, wiping rims, checking temps, and bouncing anything sub-standard. The call-and-response language: 'FIRE table 12' (cook it now), 'ALL DAY' (the running total per item, 'three salmon all day'), 'HANDS!' (runners needed), 'ON THE FLY' (emergency remake, jump the queue), 'HEARD' (acknowledged, the one-word oath of the line). A silent kitchen at full tilt is a myth; a kitchen with ONE clear voice is the actual goal.

  4. The Brigade de Cuisine The Professional Kitchen

    Escoffier's military-inspired org chart still structures serious kitchens: CHEF DE CUISINE (the general), SOUS CHEF (second, runs the pass day-to-day), CHEFS DE PARTIE (station chiefs): SAUCIER (sauces/sautés, historically the most prestigious), GARDE MANGER (cold kitchen: salads, charcuterie, terrines), PÂTISSIER (pastry), POISSONNIER (fish), RÔTISSEUR/GRILLARDIN (roasts and grill), ENTREMETIER (vegetables, soups, eggs), TOURNANT (the swing cook who works every station, often the best pure cook in the building), plus COMMIS (juniors) and PLONGEUR (dish: the kitchen's unsung keel). Modern kitchens flatten the chart but keep the logic: clear stations, clear ownership, one voice at the pass.
