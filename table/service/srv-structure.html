How a Wine Is Described

After this you can You can answer "is it dry?" and "is it heavy?" correctly, and you know off-dry is not sweet. You can say what tannin does to your mouth and what acid does, so that when a guest says "something smooth" you know which lever they mean.

  1. Acidity (The Backbone) Wine & Beverage

    The mouthwatering freshness that makes wine feel alive, and the single most food-friendly trait a wine can have: acid cuts fat, lifts salt, and resets the palate between bites (high-acid wines are the kitchen's squeeze of lemon in a glass: Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Chablis, Champagne, Barbera, Chianti). The governing rule: THE WINE MUST BE AT LEAST AS ACIDIC AS THE DISH: a vinaigrette or tomato sauce makes a soft wine taste flabby and dead, while a bright wine sails through. When a pairing 'just works,' acid is usually the quiet engineer; when in doubt at the pass, reach for the higher-acid bottle.

  2. Tannin Wine & Beverage

    The drying, grippy sensation from grape skins, seeds, stems, and oak, texture, not taste: it binds your saliva's proteins, which is exactly why tannic reds (young Cabernet, Nebbiolo, Syrah) transform beside fatty, proteinous food: the steak's fat and protein occupy the tannin, the wine turns silkier, and both improve. The first pairing law hides here: BIG TANNIN WANTS FAT AND PROTEIN. The corollary warnings: tannin + fish oil can turn metallic; tannin + chili heat amplifies burn; tannin + salt fights. When a guest says a red is 'too dry,' they usually mean tannic. And the fix is on the plate, not in the cellar.

  3. Body, Alcohol & Weight-Matching Wine & Beverage

    Body is the wine's weight in the mouth, skim milk to cream, driven mostly by alcohol, plus extract and residual sugar. The oldest pairing law is a weight-class rule: LIGHT WITH LIGHT, RICH WITH RICH. Delicate sole is bulldozed by oaky Chardonnay but flattered by Muscadet; braised short ribs make a light Pinot vanish but meet Syrah as an equal. Alcohol matters twice: it carries body AND it inflames capsaicin; high-alcohol wines with spicy food pour gasoline on the guest's palate (the fix is low-alcohol, off-dry, cold: Riesling's entire job description). 'What's the weight of the dish?' is the first question of every pairing.

  4. Dry, Off-Dry & Residual Sugar Wine & Beverage

    DRY means the yeast ate the sugar: nothing to do with tannin's drying grip, and the two are confused daily at every table in the world. Residual sugar (RS) runs from bone-dry through off-dry (a few grams: much German Riesling, Vouvray, Alsace) to fully sweet. The professional rehabilitation project: off-dry is a pairing superpower, not a beginner's crutch: sugar tames chili heat, loves salt, and flatters fried food; the sweet-wine law is absolute: THE WINE MUST BE SWEETER THAN THE DISH (dry wine after dessert tastes of ash and regret: hence Sauternes with the tarte, Port with the chocolate). Fruity ≠ sweet: a dry wine can smell of peaches; teach your floor staff the difference and refunds drop.

  5. Oak, Malolactic & Lees Wine & Beverage

    The winemaker's texture toolkit, tasted daily and named rarely. OAK (new barrels especially, French subtle/American loud) adds vanilla, spice, toast, and tannin: Rioja's coconut warmth, Napa Chardonnay's butterscotch. MALOLACTIC CONVERSION turns sharp malic acid (green apple) into soft lactic (cream): the 'buttery' in buttery Chardonnay is literally diacetyl, butter's own molecule; crisp whites (most Riesling, Albariño) skip it. LEES AGING (sur lie, resting on spent yeast) builds bready, creamy breadth: Champagne's brioche, Muscadet's texture. Read a wine's richness through these three levers and 'I taste vanilla-cream-toast' becomes 'new oak, full malo, long lees': sommelier fluency in one entry.

  6. Terroir & Old World vs. New World Wine & Beverage

    TERROIR: the argument that place is tasteable: soil, slope, climate, and tradition expressed in the glass; the entire logic of European labeling, where wines are named for WHERE (Chablis, Barolo, Sancerre) on the assumption you know the grape. NEW WORLD labels name the GRAPE (Napa Cabernet, Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc), historically riper, fruitier, oakier styles, though the stylistic border blurs more every vintage. For the restaurateur the distinction is a translation service: guests order grapes, lists reward places, and a server who can say 'Chablis IS Chardonnay: this is what it tastes like without the oak' sells the bottle a printed list cannot.

  7. Vintage, NV & Minerality Wine & Beverage

    VINTAGE is the harvest year: in marginal climates (Burgundy, Germany, Piedmont) it genuinely reshapes the wine; in reliable sun (much of the New World) it matters less than marketing implies. NV (non-vintage) Champagne is a HOUSE-STYLE blend across years: consistency as the craft, not a compromise. MINERALITY ('wet stone, chalk, flint') is the tasting world's most contested word (vines don't literally pump slate into grapes) but describes something real drinkers recognize in Chablis, Sancerre, and Mosel; use it as a flavor family, defend it as poetry, and never let a guest feel wrong for tasting it. Vintage charts are a tool; the vintage on YOUR list only matters if someone can speak to it.
