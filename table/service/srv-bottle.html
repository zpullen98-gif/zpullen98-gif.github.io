The Bottle at the Table

After this you can You can present, open, and pour a bottle without ceremony, and you know the taste pour exists to check for FAULTS and not preference. You can identify corked (wet cardboard), oxidized (bruised apple), and reduced (struck match: wait, it may blow off), and you replace a corked bottle graciously without arguing. You serve whites at 7-13°C and reds at 15-18°C.

  1. Wine Faults: Corked, Oxidized, Reduced & Friends Wine & Beverage

    The service-critical defects. CORKED (TCA taint): wet cardboard, musty basement: fruit stripped mute; affects a few percent of natural-cork bottles, and the only response is a gracious immediate replacement (never argue; the guest's nose is the instrument). OXIDIZED: browned color, bruised-apple, sherry-gone-wrong flatness: bad storage or a dead bottle (deliberate oxidation in Sherry and Madeira is a style, not a flaw; know the difference). REDUCED: struck-match or rotten-egg sulfur notes that often BLOW OFF with air or a decant: wait before condemning. VOLATILE ACIDITY: nail-polish lift; BRETT: barnyard (fault or 'character' depending on the drinker and the dose). Train every server on corked wine alone and your program's credibility survives its worst night.

  2. The Sommelier's Service Ritual & Floor Fluency Wine & Beverage

    The table choreography, demystified for the owner who must train it: present the bottle LABEL-FIRST to the orderer (confirming the vintage aloud: wrong-vintage disputes end careers), cut the capsule below the lip, cork drawn quietly and laid down without ceremony (sniffing corks is theater; the cork's job is visual: soaked-through or crumbled warns you first), pour the TASTE to the host, which exists to check for FAULTS, not preference (coach guests kindly: 'making sure the bottle's healthy'), then pour guests before host, women-then-men tradition yielding to simple clockwise in modern rooms, filling to the bowl's widest point. Refill without hovering; a dying bottle upside-down in an ice bucket is a sales signal, not a garnish. Train the floor on three sentences per wine (what it is, what it tastes like, what dish it loves) and the list starts selling itself; fluency at the table converts inventory into memory, which is the entire business.

  3. Decanting, Temperature & Glassware Wine & Beverage

    The three service levers that cost nothing and change everything. DECANT for two different reasons: aeration (young, tight, tannic reds soften with a splashy pour) and sediment (old bottles, poured gently off the crust, candle-under-neck theater optional). TEMPERATURE is the great everyday failure in BOTH directions: whites served ice-dead (aroma needs 7–13°C, not the fridge's 4°) and reds served room-hot ('room temperature' meant a French farmhouse: 15–18°C; fifteen minutes in the fridge fixes most restaurant reds). GLASSWARE: one good universal stem outperforms a cabinet of shapes; what matters is a bowl to swirl, a rim that doesn't pour, and crystal-thinness where budget allows. Fill to the widest point, never beyond: the empty space is where the aroma lives.
