The field guide

Look closely. Learn the difference.

A representative study selection, not a map of native or exclusive crops. Cultivar, grower, weather, storage and imports change availability; confirm the local harvest with the supplier.

  1. 1

    Sweet Corn

    Tender kernels arranged in rows on an ear, harvested while still juicy.

    Food form
    Immature kernels on a cob.
    Notice
    Kernels at harvest release milky juice.

    Remember Sweet corn is harvested before dry grain corn.

  2. 2

    Tomato

    A fleshy, seed-bearing fruit used as a vegetable in the kitchen.

    Food form
    Fruit of a warm-season plant.
    Notice
    Cultivars vary greatly in size and color.

    Remember Botanical fruit and culinary vegetable describe different things.

  3. 3

    Potato

    An underground stem tuber with buds called eyes.

    Food form
    Stem tuber.
    Notice
    The eyes are growing points.

    Remember A sweet potato is a storage root, not this kind of tuber.

  4. 4

    Green Bean

    A common bean harvested as a tender pod before its seeds become prominent.

    Food form
    Immature pod.
    Notice
    Green, yellow and purple pod types exist.

    Remember Snap beans differ from beans grown for mature dry seeds.

  5. 5

    Cabbage

    A compact head made of leaves wrapped tightly around one another.

    Food form
    Leaf head.
    Notice
    Heads can be round, flattened or pointed.

    Remember The head is leafy, unlike broccoli's flower buds.

  6. 6

    Winter Squash (Butternut)

    A mature, hard-rinded squash with a long neck and a rounded seed cavity.

    Food form
    Mature squash fruit.
    Notice
    Orange flesh within a tan rind.

    Remember Winter squash is harvested mature; summer squash is picked young.

Recorded correction notes

  • Broccoli prints “Season: April to October”. Midwest broccoli is not cut in April, when transplants are only going into the ground; the local heads arrive from about June, with the best crops in early summer and again in September and October after the heat passes.Broccoli needs roughly two months from transplant to head and Midwest transplants go out after the soil thaws in April, so the first local heads come in June, and midsummer heat makes the buds loosen and bolt.
  • Collard Greens prints “Season: October to March”. In the Midwest collards are cut from early summer until the ground freezes, roughly June through November or early December, and a harvest running to March is a Southern window, not a Midwestern one.Collards take light frosts and sweeten after them, but Midwest fields in USDA zones 3 to 6 are frozen or under snow from December through March, so there is no field harvest in those months on the twelve states this plate maps.
  • Kale prints “Season: September to March”. Midwest kale is cut from June until the ground freezes, roughly December, sweetest after the first frosts, and the outdoor harvest does not carry through to March in this region.Kale is the hardiest of the brassica greens and can be pulled from under early snow, but the frozen Midwest ground ends the field harvest by December, so a season ending in March describes the Southeast or the Pacific coast rather than the heartland.
  • Spinach prints “Season: April to October”. Midwest spinach comes in two windows, spring from April to June and fall from September to October, with a gap in July and August when the heat makes it bolt.Spinach is a cool weather leaf that flowers and turns bitter once days are long and hot, so Midwest growers stop cutting it in midsummer and local spinach should not be expected in July or August.
  • Acorn Squash prints “Nutty, sweet, dense”. Acorn squash is the least sweet of the common winter squashes and its flesh runs fibrous and mildly nutty rather than sweet and dense, so the sweetness on the plate comes from the butter and maple baked in the cavity.The Lexicon's Acorn Squash entry teaches that dry matter is around 12 percent, the flesh is fibrous and mildly nutty, and sugar never climbs the way it does in kabocha or delicata, declining rather than improving in storage.
