The field guide

Look closely. Learn the difference.

A representative study selection, not a map of native or exclusive crops. Cultivar, grower, weather, storage and imports change availability; confirm the local harvest with the supplier.

  1. 1

    Asparagus

    Tender young shoots harvested as pointed spears.

    Food form
    Young stem shoots.
    Notice
    A perennial vegetable.

    Remember The spear is a shoot, not a root.

    Read the Lexicon: Asparagus
  2. 2

    Beet

    A swollen, usually rounded root crop with edible leafy tops.

    Food form
    Root and greens.
    Notice
    Cultivars include red and golden forms.

    Remember The root and the greens offer different textures.

  3. 3

    Broccoli

    A branching green head made of tightly packed, unopened flower buds.

    Food form
    Flower buds and stalk.
    Notice
    The head is harvested before flowers open.

    Remember The head is not a compact ball of leaves like cabbage.

    Read the Lexicon: Broccoli
  4. 4

    Carrot

    A fleshy, tapered taproot, commonly orange.

    Food form
    Taproot.
    Notice
    Other root colors also occur.

    Remember A carrot is a root, unlike a potato stem tuber.

  5. 5

    Onion

    An edible bulb with layers beneath a dry outer skin.

    Food form
    Bulb and green tops.
    Notice
    Bulbs and tops can be used raw or cooked.

    Remember A bulb differs from a solid root or stem tuber.

  6. 6

    Winter Squash (Butternut)

    A mature, hard-rinded squash with a long neck and a rounded seed cavity.

    Food form
    Mature squash fruit.
    Notice
    Orange flesh within a tan rind.

    Remember Winter squash is harvested mature; summer squash is picked young.

Recorded correction notes

  • Cabbage prints “September to November”. Northwest cabbage stands in the field and in storage through the winter, so the season should run September to March.The plate's own Seasonal highlights box lists Cabbage under Winter while the card stops in November, and the Lexicon puts the cabbage family in the cold season's vegetable aisle, October to March in the north, with green cabbage built to sit in storage for months.
  • Mushrooms prints “September to November, drawn as brown-capped cremini and portobello”. The September to November window belongs to the Northwest's wild mushrooms such as chanterelle, porcini, matsutake and hedgehog; the cultivated cremini and portobello drawn on the card are farmed year round and have no season.The card's art shows brown Agaricus caps with thick pale stems, and the Lexicon's Wild Mushrooms entry opens with the line that cultivated mushrooms have no season while the Floor Deck's Cremini card says it is farmed year round.
  • Fennel prints “Tunnel”. The card heading should read Fennel; there is no vegetable called tunnel.The drawing shows white fennel bulbs with feathery fronds and the flavor line reads Anise, crisp, aromatic, the anethole scent the Lexicon's Fennel Bulb entry describes.
  • Leek prints “September to November”. Leeks are a fall and winter crop in the Northwest, harvested from September into March, so the season should run September to March.The plate's own Seasonal highlights box lists Leeks under Winter while the card stops in November, leeks are frost hardy and overwinter in Washington and Oregon fields, and the Kale card on the same plate already runs September to March.
  • Sweet Potatoes prints “Sweet Potatoes, September to November”. Sweet potatoes are not a Northwest crop and do not belong on a plate whose region line is Washington, Oregon, Idaho and Montana.Ipomoea batatas needs a long hot frost free season and warm soil, and the American crop comes from North Carolina, California, Mississippi and Louisiana, with no commercial acreage in the four Northwest states.
