The field guide

Look closely. Learn the difference.

These are finished product identities, not home-curing instructions. Ingredient and process details can vary; use the producer's specification for the product served.

  1. 1

    Prosciutto di Parma

    A dry-cured whole-muscle ham made from the rear leg of the pig.

    Cut
    Pork hind leg.
    Method
    Salted and matured.

    Remember Prosciutto di Parma is a cured ham, not a cooked ham or a ground sausage.

  2. 2

    Pancetta

    Cured pork belly, often presented as a slab or rolled into a cylinder.

    Cut
    Belly.
    Appearance
    Alternating lean and fat.

    Remember Its belly origin distinguishes it from cheek-based guanciale.

    Practise in the deck: Pancetta
  3. 3

    Guanciale

    A cured pork cheek or jowl cut with a substantial proportion of fat.

    Cut
    Cheek and jowl.
    Kitchen
    Used to flavor pasta dishes.

    Remember The anatomical cut differs from pancetta even when both are used for their fat.

    Practise in the deck: Guanciale
  4. 4

    Salame Milano

    A cured sausage recognized by its fine, rice-grain-like distribution of meat and fat.

    Form
    Ground meat sausage.
    Texture
    Fine grain.

    Remember The small fat particles differ from mortadella's larger white cubes.

  5. 5

    Mortadella Bologna

    A cooked, finely ground pork sausage containing distinct cubes of pork fat.

    Method
    Ground, stuffed and cooked.
    Appearance
    Pink interior with visible white fat cubes.

    Remember Charcuterie includes cooked products as well as dry-cured ones.

  6. 6

    Bresaola della Valtellina

    A cured whole-muscle beef product made from selected hindquarter cuts.

    Meat
    Beef.
    Form
    Whole muscle, seasoned and matured.

    Remember It differs from the pork products elsewhere on this board.

Recorded correction notes

  • Jamón Ibérico prints “Acorn-fed, rich, nutty, complex”. Only the bellota grades (black and red label) are acorn fed, while cebo de campo (green label) and cebo (white label) Ibérico hams are raised on feed, so say acorn fed only when the label reads de bellota.The Spanish Ibérico quality standard grades the ham by the pig's diet, and most Ibérico ham sold in the United States is cebo, not bellota.
  • Salami prints “Finely ground, fermented, versatile”. Salami is the family name for fermented, dried sausages and its grind varies by style, fine in Milano but coarser in Genoa and loose in Felino, so it should not be taught as finely ground.The plate lists soppressata, itself a coarse salami, as a separate card, and only the Milano style is notably fine.
  • Salumi Misti prints “Salumi Misti, Italy, Mixed cured meats, variety of flavors”. Salumi misti is not a product but the Italian menu phrase for a mixed platter of cured meats, so it is a name for the whole board rather than an item on it.Salumi is the family name for all Italian cured meats and misti means mixed, which the card's own caption states.
  • Venison Salami prints “Country: Wild Game”. Wild Game is not a country or origin, and venison salami sold in the United States is made from farm raised deer, often New Zealand red deer, so tell guests it is farmed venison rather than hunted.United States federal and state rules bar the commercial sale of wild harvested game, so any venison a restaurant buys is farmed and inspected.
  • Duck Breast prints “Duck Breast, France, Silky, rich, unique”. Plain duck breast names the fresh cut that is seared and served rosy, while the cured product on this board is duck prosciutto (magret séché), a salted and air dried duck breast sliced thin with its fat cap.Every other card on the plate names a preserved product, and the picture shows thin cured slices with a fat cap, which is duck prosciutto or smoked duck breast rather than a raw breast.

Original side panels

The art of charcuterie

  • A world of tradition, craftsmanship and flavor.
  • Charcuterie is the art of preserving meat through curing, smoking, drying and aging, creating unique textures, rich flavors and a lasting legacy across cultures.

Global charcuterie styles

  • Italian, Spanish
  • French, German
  • American, Portuguese
  • Greek, Scandinavian
  • Eastern European

Common preservation methods

  • Curing: Salt, spices, time
  • Smoking: Adds flavor & preserves
  • Drying: Reduces moisture
  • Aging: Develops depth & complexity

Popular pairings

  • Wine: Red, white, sparkling
  • Cheese: Soft, hard, blue
  • Olives & nuts: Briny, earthy, rich
  • Bread: Crusty, artisanal
  • Fruit & jams: Sweet, tart, complementary
