Rubbing fat into flour

47 dishes across 37 chapters · 0 marked cooked

From the Lexicon, Blind Baking & Pie Doctrine

BLIND BAKING: baking a crust empty (lined with parchment and weighted with beans/rice/sugar) before a wet filling arrives: partial for quiches that bake again, full for cream and citrus tarts. DOCKING: pricking pastry so steam escapes flat where puff isn't wanted. The pie-crust catechism: cold fat in visible pieces (steam pockets = flake), minimal water, minimal handling (gluten = toughness), rest before rolling, chill before baking (cold pastry into a hot oven holds its architecture). A 'soggy bottom' is the genre's public shame; the defenses are blind baking, a hot steel or stone below, egg-wash waterproofing, and fillings added no wetter than they must be.

How to tell it is going right

  • The fat is cold when it starts and still firm when the rubbing stops, and the bowl has not warmed under the hands.
  • Stopped early, while it still looks like coarse crumbs with pieces of fat visible in it, not worked on to a smooth even sand, which means the pieces have gone.
  • Nothing is greasy or glossy and nothing has smeared into a paste against the side of the bowl.
  • Liquid added last and only enough to bring it together: pressed, it just holds, with no wet patch and nothing sticking to the hand.
  • Rested cold before it is rolled or shaped, so the gluten relaxes and the fat firms again after being handled.

Where it goes wrong The fat was too warm, or it was rubbed until the mixture looked uniform. Once the fat has melted into the flour there are no solid pieces left to steam apart in the oven, so the bake is sandy or tough instead of flaky, and nothing done later puts those layers back.

A chosen film How to Rub Butter into Flour | Baking Mad Silver Spoon Watch for: The fingertips only, lifting the mixture high and letting it fall: watch for the moment it reads as breadcrumbs with pea-sized lumps still in it, because those unrubbed lumps are the flakiness.
