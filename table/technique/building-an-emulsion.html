On the Path of Study: Semester 9: Seafood Mastery

From the Lexicon, Emulsions

Fat and water convinced to coexist as droplets, held by an emulsifier (egg-yolk lecithin, mustard, starch, or sheer agitation): the physics behind mayonnaise, hollandaise, vinaigrette, beurre blanc, aioli, and glossy pan sauces. The universal method: add fat SLOWLY to the water phase while shearing hard; the universal failure: too fast, too hot, or too cold, and it 'breaks' into grease. The universal rescue: start fresh with a new yolk (or a spoon of water/mustard) and whisk the broken sauce INTO it. Pasta's starchy water emulsifying oil and butter into clinging sauce is the same trick; Italian kitchens just never named it.

Read it in the Lexicon →

How to tell it is going right

  • It visibly thickens as the fat goes in, heavier and more opaque than either part was alone, which is the emulsion announcing itself.
  • At no point is there a pool of free fat sitting ahead of the work: fat went in no faster than it disappeared into the body.
  • Glossy and homogeneous, with no slick floating on top and no visible droplets standing apart in it.
  • It clings: coating what it is put on and holding a line, rather than running off and splitting into a puddle at the plate’s edge.
  • It survives standing: minutes later there is no ring of weeping liquid underneath and no film of fat breaking out on the surface.

Where it goes wrong The fat went in faster than the shear could break it up, or the temperature drifted out of the range the method needs: it breaks into grease over a thin liquid, and whisking the broken thing harder does nothing. The rescue is a fresh base, with the broken sauce worked into it slowly, as if it were the fat.

Hollandaise: build, recognise, recoverIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Follow the yolk foam into an emulsion, then distinguish a smooth sauce from curdling and separation.

Use the linked recipe for the method. The last panel illustrates the fresh-base rescue for a separated sauce; it does not show cooked egg curds turning back into uncooked yolk.

  1. 1
    Whisk the sabayon

    The yolk mixture becomes pale and airy, leaving a ribbon briefly on the surface. The bowl sits clear of the water beneath it.

  2. 2
    Add butter gradually

    Work warm clarified butter into the yolks in a fine stream. Each addition should disappear into the sauce.

  3. 3
    A stable sauce

    Look for a smooth, glossy body that coats the whisk, without a separate oily slick.

  4. 4
    Too hot

    Graininess and cooked yolk lumps are different from a smooth emulsion.

  5. 5
    Separated

    Free butter over thin liquid signals a broken emulsion. Temperature and the pace of adding fat both matter.

  6. 6
    A fresh base

    The recipe’s rescue starts with a fresh yolk in a clean bowl, then works the separated sauce into it gradually.
