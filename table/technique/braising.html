Braising

39 dishes across 31 chapters · 0 marked cooked

On the Path of Study: Semester 4: The Braise, Semester 7: Fire & Smoke

From the Lexicon, Braising & Stewing

Combination cooking: sear for Maillard flavor, then simmer partially submerged (braise, big pieces) or fully submerged (stew, small pieces) in a covered pot at a bare tremble (85–95°C liquid, oven around 150°C) until collagen converts to gelatin. The conversion needs time above ~70°C, which is why tough cuts get tender and lean cuts get dry: choose collagen-rich muscle always. Universal laws: never boil (boiled braises are grainy), skim honestly, rest overnight when possible (flavor marries, fat lifts), and the strained liquid IS the sauce: reduce and mount it, waste nothing.

Read it in the Lexicon →

How to tell it is going right

  • The pieces went into the liquid already deep brown on their faces, and the pot floor gave up its fond to that liquid rather than keeping it.
  • The liquid comes partway up the pieces and stays there for the whole cook (not submerged, not run dry), and it never boils: the surface trembles and no more.
  • It went long enough for the collagen to convert: a fork twists in it with almost no resistance, and the piece still lifts out whole rather than falling into shreds.
  • The liquid has become a sauce: it has body, it coats the back of a spoon, and it tastes concentrated rather than thin and washed out.
  • The meat is moist and seasoned through to its centre, not dry fibres sitting inside a good sauce.

Where it goes wrong The oven or the burner ran too hot and the liquid boiled, so the fibres tightened and squeezed their moisture out: it shreds dry into a thin greasy liquid, and more time makes it worse rather than better.

A chosen film The Foolproof Formula for Braising Beef | Techniquely with Lan Lam America's Test Kitchen Watch for: Lan Lam on why the liquid must never boil and why the pot comes only partway up the meat, which is what turns a braise from a covered simmer into collagen becoming gelatin.
