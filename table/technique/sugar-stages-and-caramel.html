On the Path of Study: Semester 4: The Braise

From the Lexicon, Caramel Stages & Sugar Work

Sugar syrup's temperature IS its destiny: thread 110°C (syrups), soft ball 113–116 (fudge, pralines, Italian meringue), firm/hard ball 120–130 (caramels, nougat), soft/hard crack 132–154 (toffee, brittle, spun sugar), then true CARAMEL 160–180°C: sugar itself breaking into hundreds of bitter-sweet compounds, from blond to mahogany to one shade before ruin. Wet caramel (sugar + water) is safer; dry caramel is faster and riskier. Crystallization is the saboteur: don't stir once boiling, brush down stray crystals, or insure with corn syrup/glucose. Add cream and butter and it's sauce; add nothing and stop it dark and it's the top of a crème brûlée's ancestry.

Read it in the Lexicon →

How to tell it is going right

  • Nothing is stirred once it is boiling, and any crystals clinging to the side of the pan are brushed down rather than left to fall in.
  • Judged by temperature or by a tested stage rather than by time: consecutive stages are a few degrees apart and look identical in the pan.
  • Colour is read off the heat and in good light, remembering that sugar keeps darkening after the flame is out.
  • Stopped decisively at the point wanted: off the heat and into the next liquid or onto a cold surface, rather than left to coast in a hot pan.
  • The result is clear and smooth rather than grainy, cloudy, or seized into a solid mass.

Where it goes wrong It was stirred after coming to the boil, or a stray crystal from the side of the pan fell back in. One crystal seeds the whole pan and the syrup turns grainy within seconds, and once sugar has crystallised the only cure is more water and starting the whole thing again.

Sugar: cooled texture and caramel colourIllustrated study · 6 numbered views

Read two different tests: cooled syrup samples across the top, then caramel colours across the bottom.

The upper samples represent fully cooled cold-water tests. Never touch or taste hot syrup. The listed ranges are sea-level guides from the Exploratorium; use the recipe, an accurate thermometer and altitude adjustment. The caramel row is a colour comparison, not a temperature scale.

One numbered view at a time. No grade or change to your learning record.

Whole illustration. Choose a view for a closer look.

  1. 1
    Soft-ball test

    At roughly 235–240°F (113–116°C), a cooled syrup sample forms a soft ball that flattens readily. Its shape, rather than amber colour, identifies the test.

  2. 2
    Firm-ball test

    At roughly 245–250°F (118–121°C), the cooled sample holds a ball but remains pliable. Compare its retained shape with the softer sample.

  3. 3
    Hard-crack test

    At roughly 300–310°F (149–154°C), cooled syrup forms brittle threads that snap. This is a texture check, not the name for dark caramel.

  4. 4
    Pale caramel

    A light golden colour marks early browning in this comparison. Watch against a pale background and follow the recipe’s intended flavour.

  5. 5
    Amber caramel

    The deeper amber sample shows more browning. Caramelisation creates flavours beyond simple sweetness.

  6. 6
    Dark caramel

    The darker sample illustrates a stronger colour, not a universally better result. Control residual heat and stop before the batch develops a burnt flavour.

Observe
Separate the upper row’s cooled shapes from the lower row’s colours. A clear syrup can already have a useful confectionery structure.
Explain
Water loss changes syrup concentration and its cooled texture. Caramel browning adds a different set of changes; the two observations are not interchangeable.
Apply
Connect New Orleans Bread Pudding’s pralines and whiskey caramel with two different sugar preparations. Confirm the house formulas before describing a precise stage.
Check your understanding

Would you wait for amber colour when a recipe asks for soft ball?

Reveal the explanation

No. Use the specified temperature and cooled-texture test. Amber colour belongs to the separate caramel comparison, and would not be the soft-ball endpoint.
