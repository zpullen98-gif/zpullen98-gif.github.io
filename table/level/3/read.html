  1. Dishes

    13 at this level

    At Sous Chef a dish is a whole station in miniature: live fire, the wok, a long ferment or a build in parts, cooked and logged, then cooked again until you could teach it.

    These plates are fire and smoke, the wok, seafood, and the Advanced plates with a long wait or a build in parts. The long waits are the Birria de Res con Consomé, which runs from a marinade to a sear to a braise to the griddle, and the breads: the Pizza Margherita, the Baguette de Tradition and the Country Sourdough Loaf live on fermentation and proofing. The builds in parts are pastry: the Tarte au Citron asks for a blind-baked crust, a lemon cream over a water bath and, if you crown it, a meringue, while the Profiteroles (Pâte à Choux Master) take one pastry technique to a finish.

    Take them in the order of the Path of Study, which is the order the doors follow, and start with the birria. It rewards the braise you already know from Chef de Partie and runs well over four hours, so give it an open afternoon. That week, feed a sourdough starter into life as preparation: the Country Sourdough Loaf leans on one, and a new starter needs time before it can raise a loaf. Then take the breads: the Pizza Margherita first, then the Baguette de Tradition, where proofing and steam decide the crust, then the loaf.

    In pastry, take the Profiteroles before the tart. Choux is one technique carried to a finish, and one is easier to log honestly than three. The Tarte au Citron stacks the crust, the cream and the meringue, and each part can fail on its own.

    The fire plates teach the coals. Take Huli Huli Chicken first: butchery basics before the fire, then the turn, since huli means turn and the sugar in the baste burns if the chicken sits. Jerk Chicken comes next, once the two-zone fire is second nature, because jerk brings a technique of its own to the coals. Memphis Dry-Rub Ribs follow, and low and slow smoking is hours of holding the fire steady on thin blue smoke.

    The wok plates are the fastest cooking in the guide and the least forgiving: maximum heat, total mise and small batches leave no second look. Cook the Rau Muong Xao Toi first and often, until the garlic turns fragrant and the greens follow before it scorches, then the Pad Krapow Moo, where the pork is pressed into the wok to sear, not stew, then Char Kway Teow, the Advanced plate of the three. The Whole Grilled Fish with Ladolemono comes last and brings back the coals: a whole fish over the fire, filleting, and the oil and lemon emulsion that dresses it.

    A dish is met when it has been cooked and logged. Read the semester opens the Path of Study at the semester teaching the first dish you have not cooked, and Cook the next dish goes straight to that recipe; both keep the order above, so they open on the birria until it is logged. The library at this level is the recipe grid filtered to Advanced, which leaves out the fire plates, the fish and the two quicker wok plates placed here. Log each plate the day you cook it, then cook it again until you could talk a commis through it at the pass. The Level test at the foot of the page ends on what you missed, with the right answers.

  2. Techniques

    11 at this level

    At Sous Chef a technique is read from the pass: the fire, the wok, the fold and the knife.

    A grill run on flames, a crowded wok or butter that shatters in the fold shows on the plate, so a sous has to know each of these well enough to see the fault from across the kitchen and teach the fix. Take Grilling over live coals first. It carries a written standard, sits under more recipes than anything else here, and its marks teach the fire before the food: coals evenly under gray ash with no flames licking through, a hot zone and a cooler one both ready, a hot clean grate the food releases from, marks browned rather than burnt, and an interior at its target with a thin gradient. Low & slow smoking comes next, with its own standard: thin blue smoke, never white and rolling, a chamber held steady with the air intake, the stall waited out or wrapped through on purpose, and doneness read by probe and feel, never by the clock.

    Asado fire and Jerk carry no written standard, and they are not the same fire. For asado, wood burns down in a separate fire and its embers are raked under the grill as the cook needs them. Jerk cooks over pimento wood, often covered, so it grills and smokes at once. Take them once the two-zone fire is second nature.

    Wok technique carries the third standard. Everything goes in dry and in order, the wok stays loud from first moment to last, every piece is glossy with no sauce pooled under it, and the vegetables keep their color and a bite at the center. Its fault is nearly always too much at once or too wet. Take Velveting alongside it: meat coated in egg white and cornstarch, rested and precooked briefly, which is where stir-fry silk comes from.

    Lamination, Letter-folding dough and Tempering chocolate are temperature disciplines with no written standard, so the recipe is the teacher. In lamination the dough and the butter must match, cold but bendable; the dough rests between folds, and the turns are counted, a letter fold being one single turn. Butter too cold shatters and leaks; too warm, it smears in and bakes like brioche. Tempering chocolate is melting, cooling and reheating along a set curve so the cocoa butter sets in its stable form: gloss and snap instead of dull bloom.

    Butchery basics and Filleting fish are where cooking starts before there is heat: frenching a rack, butterflying to an even thickness, tying a roast for even cooking and clean slices, stripping silverskin, and drawing a flexible fillet knife along the spine. A sous reads the yield in the trim bin, and yield follows the edge.

    A technique with a written standard is met when you cook one of its recipes and are graded met against its marks. For the rest, cooking any one of its recipes meets it. Open Read the techniques for the index at this level, and The next technique takes you to the first one not yet met.

  3. The Lexicon

    36 at this level

    At the pass the words turn specialist and the numbers arrive: the fire, the fold, what a plate really costs and the rare thing a guest asks you to explain.

    This is the vocabulary you teach from as well as cook from, and it runs in bands: the fire and the fold whose standard a whole service answers to, the numbers that run the building, then the spices and the specialist produce a guest asks about because it is on tonight's board. Take them in that order.

    Start with the fire and the fold. Grilling vs. Barbecue vs. Smoking separates three fires by their timing: grilling is direct high heat for minutes over a two-zone grate, barbecue is indirect low heat and wood smoke for hours on collagen cuts, and smoking either cooks as it flavors or, cold, flavors cured food without cooking it. Keep the two wok words apart: Wok Hei & Stir-Fry Discipline names the aroma, the smoky, singed-air breath of food and oil vaporizing against hot carbon steel, and The Wok names the vessel, whose sloped walls give a searing base and cooler shoulders to stage ingredients. Lamination is the layered dough itself, butter and dough folded into alternating sheets that oven steam jacks apart. The method behind all three belongs to the Techniques reader.

    Then the numbers, and Menu Economics: Food Cost, Yield & Par first, because the rest lean on it. Cost a fish at its raw invoice price instead of its price after trim and cooking, and the plate costs more than you think. Prime Cost adds total labor to cost of goods and wants it weekly, not only at month-end. COGS Control sets actual against theoretical cost and reads the variance as the leak; Labor schedules to forecast, not habit; KPIs & the Daily Dashboard keeps few numbers on one page. Health Inspections & Crisis Management is the entry the Food Safety page reads clause by clause at this level, so read it there.

    The spice entries teach a behavior more than a taste. Asafoetida (Hing) goes into hot fat first, a pinch, and blooms into savory allium depth; Panch Phoron keeps its seeds whole and uses their crackle as the timer; The Three Heats sorts chile, pepper and mustard heat into linger, hum and flash. The larder is learned by the menu, the dish on the board first: Seville Orange for marmalade and bigarade, Treviso grilled as precoce or raw as tardivo, Black Truffle carried in fat, Nettles blanched on arrival in gloves, Salsify peeled into lemon water, Lovage counted in leaves.

    Open Read the terms, then Flashcards and Quiz over this level's terms. A term is met when you have recalled it met or close on the flashcard ladder, or in the quiz; a miss alone is never met. Then check yourself: can you explain yield to a new cook, or tardivo to a guest, without the card?

  4. The Floor Deck

    80 at this level

    At Sous Chef the deck is the classical menu word and the specialist product behind it, known well enough to teach.

    These cards are the words a fine-dining menu prints without a gloss and expects the floor to carry. Most fall into three families. First, the classical methods, sauces and preparations: À la Plancha, Salt-Baked and Lacquered; Beurre Blanc, Velouté, Bordelaise, Gribiche and Soubise; Meunière, En Papillote, En Croûte, Torchon and Quenelle. Second, specialist product: the cuts, Bavette, Coulotte, Denver Steak, Teres Major and Chateaubriand; the cured meats from Guanciale and Lardo to Speck, Bresaola and Jamón Ibérico; the fish case of Turbot, Dover Sole, Langoustine and Uni; the wild Black Trumpet and White Truffle, and the Summer Truffle, which is also farmed. Third, the pastry section's words, from Pâte à Choux and Canelé to Sabayon, Crémeux and Posset.

    Take the pairs first, because at this level the question is less what a word means than what it is not. Gastrique and Agrodolce are both sugar and vinegar, one a smooth syrup built on caramel, the other often chunky with fruit and onion. Crudo and Escabeche are both set against ceviche: one raw and dressed at the last moment, the other always cooked before it meets the vinegar. En Papillote is a paper parcel torn open; En Croûte is a pastry wrapper you eat. Meunière is browned butter and lemon, where Beurre Blanc is pale butter whisked into a wine reduction. Learn them in twos.

    Then the product, read with its note, because this is where a guest's question comes back to the pass. Bavette and Denver Steak are best at medium rare and sliced across the grain. Chateaubriand is one roast, so both guests share one doneness. Squab breast is classically medium rare, so ask before it fires. Dover Sole is filleted from the bone at the table, so a stray fine bone is possible. Tell Speck from prosciutto, Tasso from andouille and Stracciatella from burrata the way the cards do.

    Take the pastry and the room's language next, and do not skip their warnings. A pale Canelé is the failure, not a dark one. Kouign-Amann folds its sugar inside a yeasted dough. Clafoutis may keep its cherry pits, so ask the kitchen. Garde Manger is the cold station, and its crudo and oysters go out raw. Mignardises close the meal where an amuse-bouche opens it. Omakase prints no menu, so anything a guest needs to avoid is said before the first course, and Regenerative has no legal definition, so the farm's name is what counts.

    Start at the deck landing, which shows what is owed today, then Flip cards at this level, then Say it back, recall before recognition and the one deck mode that counts as a round. A card is met when it has been graded met or close in the deck's modes; a miss alone is never met. The written test for this level is untimed and ends on what you missed with the right answers and no number, so treat its misses as tomorrow's flips.

  5. The Plates

    1 at this level

    One folio at Sous Chef: the charcuterie board by cut and by process, six products a sous must explain without notes.

    Sous Chef holds a single folio, Charcuterie: cut and process. Like every folio it teaches six subjects with a guide and sources, and it is read, never graded; nothing about it is recorded. It sits here because a board is judged by the difference between its products, and a sous is the one asked to explain that difference to a guest and to a new cook.

    Read it by cut first. Prosciutto di Parma is a dry-cured whole-muscle ham from the rear leg of the pig. Pancetta is cured belly and guanciale is cured cheek or jowl, so the two are different cuts even when both are used for their fat. Bresaola della Valtellina is the beef on the board, a cured whole muscle from the hindquarter, and it is the reminder that not everything on a board is pork.

    Then read it by process. Salame Milano is a cured sausage whose fat sits in fine flecks the size of rice grains; Mortadella Bologna is a cooked, finely ground pork sausage set with larger white cubes of fat. That pair carries the folio's main point: charcuterie takes in cooked products as well as dry-cured ones. The guide is a map of product identities, not a curing method, and the specification of the product you serve belongs to its producer, so ask the kitchen which ones the house buys.

    Read the first plate opens this folio, and The wall shows all twenty, the folios read at Commis and Chef de Partie among them; read those again when the menu calls for them. The folio is the picture, not the drill: every subject on it but guanciale is a term in The Italian Salumi, the Service module at this level, and that drill is where they are met. Beneath the guide sits the original poster archive with its recorded correction notes, kept for reference, while the six-subject guide is the lesson.

    Named here Charcuterie: cut and process

    Next Chef holds no folio of its own. At that level the wall is read again with the menu in hand, and the folios become the way you teach the brigade the distinctions learned here.

  6. The Palate

    2 at this level

    Bitter and rich are the two faults placed here, and neither reads in a moment: one stays after the swallow, the other only shows by the sixth bite.

    The repair table opened at Commis on flat and salty, and Chef de Partie added sour and sweet. Those four faults are read in the moment of tasting. The two placed here ask for more time. Bitter is a hard edge at the back of the palate that stays after swallowing, so you judge it after the spoon is down, not only during. Rich is delicious for two bites and fatiguing by the sixth, so a single taste says the plate is good. That is why these two sit at the sous chef's level: the sous is the one who tastes a portion through, not a spoon.

    Take Bitter first, because it has four levers and asks you to choose between them. Salt comes first: it suppresses bitterness outright, real chemistry and not folklore. Sweetness buffers it. Fat rounds it off. Acid brightens past it rather than removing it. Three of the four soften the edge itself; only acid leaves it in place and brightens past it. So when a charred dish or a bitter green comes up hard, salt before you reach for honey, butter or lemon, and taste again after each move. One lever at a time, or you cannot tell which one worked.

    Rich is the simpler fault with the stricter answer. The lever is acid, always acid, and there is no second answer to this one. After the acid, and not instead of it, comes something crunchy-fresh. A crunchy garnish on its own gives the guest one bite of contrast and then the fatigue returns; the acid is what resets the palate so the sixth bite tastes like the first. When a braise, a cream sauce or a fatty cut comes up heavy, adjust with acid, taste past the second bite, and only then add the crunch.

    The two faults teach each other. Salt, whose excess you named as salty at Commis, is now the first lever for bitter. Acid, which you named as sour at Chef de Partie, is now the only answer to rich. The chemistry is not new at this level; reading a plate over time instead of in a moment is. Spend your first sessions on plates that hold up on one taste and fail on six, or that leave an edge after the swallow, and say which fault it is before you pick the lever.

    Two doors open from this subsection. The repair table holds the faults with their levers, in order, and a fault is met when you have named it on a plate there. Calibrate holds the tasting ladder, which at this level asks each of the six tastes, salt, sweet, acid, bitter, umami and seasoned, to rung 3, and a taste is met when its ladder is cleared to this rung. Bitter is on both doors, as a fault to repair and as a taste to calibrate; rich is a fault only, and the ladder does not carry it.

    Named here Bitter·Rich

    Next Chef takes the palate to spicy and muddy, the faults with the least obvious levers. The slow reading you built here, to the sixth bite and not the first, is what you carry up.

  7. Food Safety

    10 at this level

    At Sous Chef the inspector is not an event to prepare for: the kitchen you run every day is the one that gets photographed.

    Commis gave you the danger zone and FIFO, Chef de Partie the cooling law and HACCP as a system. Sous Chef moves the subject from your station to the whole room, because a sous is the one standing there when the inspector walks in and the one who writes up what happens after. Placed here are the Inspections clause, The principle beneath it, the facts drawn from them, and one gap. Read the Inspections clause first and in full, because every fact at this level is a line lifted out of it.

    The first group is the standard itself. The guide says to run the kitchen so ANY moment is inspection-ready, and its capitals are the point: there is no prep week. The daily standard it names is temps logged, FIFO labeled, hand sinks stocked and USED, sanitizer buckets titrated, and USED carries as much weight as ANY, since a stocked sink nobody touches is a finding waiting to be written. Take this group before the other two, because it is the part you can walk the line and check tonight.

    The second group is the visit. During it, accompany professionally, correct on the spot what can be, never argue, get every finding in writing. After it, fix, document, retrain at pre-shift, and treat repeat findings as management failures, not staff ones. That last phrase is the sous chef's line in the whole subsection: a finding that comes back is yours, not the commis's. Read these two facts together and in that order, because the second only works if the first left you with paper.

    The third group is one of the crisis playbooks, written before it is needed. On a foodborne-illness complaint, take it seriously and kindly, gather specifics, preserve records and product. Then call the insurer and, if credible, the health department yourself, because the cover-up is always worse than the event. The principle beneath the Inspections clause says why: crises are inevitable, scandals are optional, and the difference is preparation, documentation, and guests first, reputation second, ego never. Read the principle twice.

    One gap is placed here on purpose. Sanitizer buckets titrated is a discipline the guide names, but to what concentration it never says. The app does not fill that in, and neither does this page. Knowing which lines the guide states and which it only names is itself a Sous Chef skill, and the level above makes a subject of it.

    There are two doors. Read opens the Food Safety page at this level's first slice, and The whole page opens all of it, worth doing once here so the playbook sits on the Commis and Chef de Partie material. Met is simple: food safety is read, never graded, and nothing about it is recorded.

  8. Service

    105 at this level

    Service at Sous Chef is the board and the list: the grapes behind the glass, the cheeses and cured meats by country, and what is in the well and call bottles behind the bar.

    Chef de Partie gave you how a wine is described and the board's formula. Sous Chef asks you to sell off the list and the board from depth. The modules here fall in four groups: one grape atlas, four cheese boards, six charcuterie modules and one bar shelf. Take them in that order unless you work the bar, where the bottle comes second.

    Take The Grape Atlas (Deep Reference) first, because the floor uses it every night. Seventeen varietals, Riesling to Zinfandel, and for each you can say where it comes from, what it smells like and what it does at the table. Work the grapes in pairs, Chardonnay against Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir against Gamay, so each has a neighbor.

    The cheese boards come next, in the order the page lists them. The Italian Board and The French Board come first and carry the most cheeses: why the crunch in aged Parmigiano is a feature, Gorgonzola dolce against piccante, why Époisses smells worse than it tastes, and how to stop a guest cutting the nose off a wedge of Comté. Alpine and Iberian sells the melting cheeses on their physics and places Cabrales as the blue that outguns Roquefort. Britain, the Low Countries and the Cold Edge closes the boards with Stilton, aged Gouda, and the four that behave unlike any of them: feta, halloumi, brunost, sulguni.

    Charcuterie is the largest group and it has an order. Start with The Charcuterie Board, the two churches of whole muscle and fermented salami on one slicer, because its outcome is the standard you hold the pass to: 3 to 5 meats spanning silk, salame, spreadable and a wildcard, 60 to 90 g a head, whole-muscle sliced translucent and salame in 2 to 3 mm coins on a slight bias, everything at room temperature 20 to 30 minutes out and cut as close to service as possible.

    After that the modules go by region. The Italian Salumi tells Parma from San Daniele and coppa from culatello. The Spanish Cure gives you the bellota grades and separates Spanish chorizo from Mexican before a guest orders the wrong one. The French Charcuterie is pâté and terrine cut with a hot wet knife, boudin noir explained to a hesitant table, and the foie gras order handled without improvising. Central Europe, America and the Wider Cure places Black Forest ham, the kielbasa family and country ham's salt. Read How It Was Cured (Reference) last: it answers the nitrite question without a myth, and tells you when to hand it to the chef.

    What Is in the Bottle stands apart from the boards: for any well or call order, what the spirit is made from, roughly what it tastes like and what it substitutes for. The level page opens three doors: the first module, the track of all 27 modules, and Drill, over this level's modules with the whole track as the field. A module is met through its terms: every term recalled met or close in the drill. So take one module, then the drill, and move on when none of its terms is missed. The Level test at the foot of the page is untimed and ends on what you missed with the right answers.
