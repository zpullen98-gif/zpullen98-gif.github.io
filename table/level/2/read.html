  1. Dishes

    16 at this level

    At this station a dish is a set of moves you repeat until they hold: the pan sauce, the braise, the dough and the fish.

    Most plates here carry several techniques at once, and the braises and the bread ask you to hold a pot or a dough steady over hours. They run from the opening semesters of the Path of Study through the sauté station, the braise, bread and pastry fundamentals to Seafood Mastery, with one plate from the wok.

    Start at the pan and the soup pot. Cacio e Pepe is toasted pepper, pecorino and the pasta's own starchy water brought into a sauce, and it tells you at once whether your salted water and your timing are right. Sole Meunière is the sauté station in one pan: dredge, sear, then brown butter. Bulgogi, from the wok semester, is the same hard sear on thin-cut meat that has been marinated first. Soupe à l'Oignon teaches the onion's patience, caramelizing slowly and deglazing before the broiler takes the top. Barszcz asks for a clear beet broth, strained and passed through a sieve.

    Then the braise, where most of this level's hours go. Boeuf Bourguignon, Coq au Vin, Ragù alla Bolognese and Jamaican Oxtail Stew each build their base in the pan first, fat rendered or meat seared for color, before low heat and time do the rest. Take Bourguignon first because it sets the method. Coq au Vin adds reducing the sauce to finish, the ragù adds a fine dice for its base and a long, low simmer, and the oxtail adds skimming a long pot clear and taking sugar to caramel for the color of the gravy.

    Then bread and pastry. Rosemary Focaccia is one technique, proofing, and the habit of reading the dough rather than the clock. Crème Brûlée is a custard tempered, strained and baked in a water bath, then finished with the torch. Crêpes look easy and are quick, but an even pan and a thin, even swirl take repetition before every one comes out the same.

    Then the fish case. Cantonese Steamed Fish is steaming and a fine julienne. Miso-Glazed Black Cod is a marinade and the broiler. Linguine alle Vongole is clams steamed open and picked, their juices and the pasta water brought into an emulsion. Calamari Fritti is the coat, the deep fry and draining on a rack, never paper.

    A dish is met when it has been cooked and logged: not read, not watched, cooked. The door "Read the semester" opens the Path at the semester that teaches the first dish you have not cooked; "Cook the next dish" goes straight to its recipe page; "The library at this level" shows the recipe grid at this level's difficulty when you want another plate. The Level test at the foot of the page asks across the subsections and ends on what you missed.

  2. Techniques

    51 at this level

    Chef de Partie technique is the station's work: the braise, the emulsion, the custard and the dough, done the same way every service.

    The techniques placed here are what running a station asks, and most fall into four families: the sauce bench, the slow pot, the pastry and bread base, and the flavor builders. Béchamel sits on the sauce bench but is not an emulsion: it is milk thickened with a white roux. Confit, Rendering fat and The tagine share the braise's low, patient heat. Pie crust & blind baking and Icing & frosting open and close the pastry run.

    Read Braising and Building an emulsion before the others, then cook them when the dishes list reaches the plates that carry them. Both carry written standards, and they teach two habits much of this level leans on: heat held below the boil, and nothing added faster than the pot can take it. A braise goes in deep brown, the liquid comes partway up and only trembles, a fork twists in with almost no resistance while the piece still lifts out whole, and the liquid ends as a sauce that coats a spoon. An emulsion thickens as the fat goes in, and fat never pools ahead of the work. When one breaks, whisking harder does nothing: start a fresh base and work the broken sauce into it slowly. Hollandaise and Beurre blanc are that same rule on the sauce station.

    Then the pastry and bread base, each read by its own mark. Rubbing fat into flour stops early, while pieces of cold fat still show. Creaming butter & sugar takes minutes, not seconds, until the mix is pale and grown in volume. Folding stops the moment the streaks go. Kneading dough ends smooth and barely tacky, Resting dough lets it roll without springing back, and Proofing is judged on volume and the poke test, never by the hour. Tempering a custard and The water bath share one discipline: gentle heat, no boil, and doneness judged by how it behaves. Sugar stages & caramel adds the last: nothing stirred once it boils, every stage read by temperature or test.

    Tadka and Pounding a curry paste are about order: the hardest seeds into hot fat first and the delicate ones last, the hard dry ingredients pounded down before anything wet arrives. Fermentation is a rate, not a duration, so taste it rather than trust the hours. Marinating is the one technique where longer is a different result, not a safer one: keep the stated time and keep it cold. Pleating dumplings and Working with masa carry standards of their own: a seam closed its whole length, masa that seals smooth when a pinch is rolled shut. Pierogi, Handling phyllo and Fresh pasta are other kitchens' dough work, and Risotto method, Congee, Shucking shellfish and The seafood boil are narrower methods, each learned on its own recipes.

    A technique with a written standard is met when you cook one of its recipes and are graded met against that standard; one without a standard is met by cooking any of its recipes. The Read the techniques door opens the index at this level. The next technique door takes you to the first one you have not met. The Level test at the foot of the page runs across the subsections and ends on what you missed, with the right answers.

  3. The Lexicon

    268 at this level

    At this station a word is a method: the sear, the emulsion, the cut, the fish case, the baker's math and the spice rack, each owed a sentence when the menu names it.

    Start with the words your station cooks by. Take The Maillard Reaction first: browning between amino acids and sugars, whose enemies are water and crowding, and a different thing from caramelization, which is sugar alone. Once you can say why a wet steak steams, the sear in Braising & Stewing explains itself. Then Emulsions: fat and water held together as droplets by an emulsifier such as egg yolk or mustard, the physics behind mayonnaise, hollandaise, vinaigrette and beurre blanc, and the same trick as the starchy pasta water that turns oil and butter into a clinging sauce. Confit and Consommé & Clarification finish the group: fat as a gentle cooking medium, and a raft of egg white, ground meat and mirepoix that filters a stock clear.

    The cuts and the fish case come next, because a guest asks about them by name. Read Marbling first: the grades are largely measures of it, and more of it forgives a higher doneness. Flank, Skirt & Hanger share one law: marinate or salt, high heat to medium-rare at most, rest, and slice thin against the grain. Dry-Aging buys flavor through moisture loss and the meat's own enzymes. At the fish case, Round Fish vs. Flat Fish tells you two fillets or four, Fatty vs. Lean Fish tells you smoke or a gentle poach, and Smoked Fish, hot or cold, names two products sharing one word.

    Pastry and baking science sit here at their base. Take Gluten & Hydration first: hydration is water measured against the flour's weight, and baker's math, everything written relative to the flour, is the language every formula scales in. Chemical Leaveners follows: baking soda needs an acid partner and also speeds browning, while baking powder carries its own acid. Caramel Stages & Sugar Work closes the group: the syrup's temperature decides what it becomes, from thread and soft ball through crack to true caramel, and a wet caramel is more forgiving while a dry one is faster and riskier, with crystallization as the saboteur.

    Then the spice rack and the less common produce. Building the Working Spice Pantry names the core dozen, and Toasting, Blooming & the Tadka Doctrine explains why the pan matters: most spice flavor dissolves in fat, not water, which is why raw curry powder tastes dusty and chili crisp works. Acid Architecture and Sweetness as Seasoning are the palate's sour and sweet in words; read them side by side. Open the produce with Chile Fluency (Fresh & Dried), because in Mexican kitchens a dried chile takes a new name: the ripe red poblano, dried, is Ancho. Then read the Seasonal Larder entries, Citrus in Winter among them, by what your menu handles this month, framed by Seasonality & Ripeness.

    The first door, Read the terms, is the Lexicon at this level: take one group at a sitting and say each word aloud as the sentence a server would need. Then Flashcards and Quiz, both over this level's terms. A term is met when you have recalled it met or close on the flashcard ladder or in the quiz; a miss alone is never met.

  4. The Floor Deck

    122 at this level

    At Chef de Partie the deck is the menu's second layer: the words a guest points at and expects a sentence back, from confit to 'nduja to pot de crème.

    Chef de Partie hands you the cards the floor asks about: what the thing is, how the kitchen handles it and what it is not. Most carry a note or a not-this line, and that is where the misses happen: coppa is not prosciutto, ghee is not clarified butter, a macaron is not a macaroon. Wherever a card has a pair, learn the pair.

    Take the six methods first, they describe everything else: confit, sous vide, blanched, fermented, rendered and pan-roasted. Confit and rendered pull opposite ways, meat under fat against fat out of meat. Then the sauces, the mother sauces the level page names first, and their children: béchamel is the white base, demi-glace the roasted veal stock reduced by half and the base of bordelaise, béarnaise a child of hollandaise, and emulsion explains why aioli and remoulade hold. The cold, sharp ones travel together, mignonette, chimichurri and salsa verde; coulis is the sweet strained purée under dessert.

    Cuts come in two kinds of card: the steaks, hanger steak, skirt steak, flank steak, flat iron steak and ribeye cap, plus wagyu, a breed and not a cut, most giving the doneness the kitchen sends, and the braises, cheek and oxtail, with no temperature to ask for. Meats are the lean, farm-raised ones to explain, guinea hen against pheasant, quail, rabbit, venison, and the plates that need an honest word, foie gras, chicken liver, bone marrow, beef tongue. Charcuterie learns in contrasts: pancetta against bacon, coppa against prosciutto, pâté against terrine against rillettes, soppressata against 'nduja. The fish case is branzino against black sea bass, skate, monkfish, arctic char, blue crab and soft-shell crab, roe against caviar.

    Dairy is a ladder of clarified butter, brown butter and ghee, then compound butter, chèvre, burrata, mascarpone and crème fraîche. Starches split into shapes and grains, tagliatelle against pappardelle, cavatelli against orecchiette, agnolotti and gnocchi, then farro, bulgur, polenta and arborio. Mushrooms are foraged, chanterelle, morel, porcini, or farmed, king trumpet, hen of the woods, and the notes on morel and enoki say cooked through. The preparations split the raw plate, carpaccio against tartare, ceviche, tataki and gravlax, from the cooked one: ragout, croquette, roulade, amandine.

    Pastry runs from beignets, galette, pavlova and soufflé to the custards, crème anglaise against pastry cream, panna cotta and pot de crème, and the frozen four, gelato, granita, parfait and semifreddo. The pantry is the seasoning rack: miso, fish sauce, harissa, pimentón, sherry vinegar, preserved lemon, finishing salt. Language holds the menu's claims, dry-aged, pasture-raised, heritage breed and day-boat, and the not-this line says what each word is not.

    Flip cards first, the whole card, note and not-this included. Say it back is the one deck mode that counts as a round, recall before recognition, so give it the most time. The written test is untimed and ends on what you missed with the right answers and no number, so take it after a run of Say it back. The deck's landing shows what is owed today. A card is met when it has been graded met or close in the deck's modes; a miss alone is never met, so take a missed card through Say it back again.

  5. The Plates

    16 at this level

    The folios for running a station: the two big animals, the three fish cases, the vegetables and fruits of five regions, and the cheese board.

    Chef de Partie holds most of the wall. Each folio teaches six subjects with a guide, a line to remember for each and sources to read further; they are read, never graded, and nothing about them is recorded. Read them in four groups, in the order a station meets them: the meat, the fish, the produce and the cheese.

    Start with Pork: six useful distinctions and Beef: where the cut begins, because a station orders by cut. The pork folio runs from the Boston butt of the shoulder through the loin and the tenderloin to the fresh belly, the spare ribs and the hock. The beef folio places the chuck roast, the ribeye, the strip steak, the tenderloin, the brisket and the hanger steak on the animal, and its lines to remember are the ones a server gets wrong: marbling is fat within the muscle, the strip loin and the tenderloin are neighboring but separate muscles, and the hanger is not the flank.

    Then the three fish cases. Pacific: six seafood identities asks you to read body shape and fins before a market nickname: king is another name for Chinook, and the fish sold as black cod is sablefish, not a cod. Atlantic: fish and shellfish sets four finfish beside the sea scallop and the American lobster, and Gulf Coast: reef, open water and shore runs from red snapper and grouper to mahi mahi, yellowfin tuna, white shrimp and blue crab.

    The ten produce folios read in pairs by region: Northwest vegetables and Northwest fruits, Southwest vegetables and Southwest fruits, Midwest vegetables and Midwest fruits, Northeast vegetables and Northeast fruits, Southeast vegetables and Southeast fruits. Each teaches the part of the plant you are holding, the shoot, root, bud, pod, leaf or fruit, rather than a season, because availability moves with the grower and the weather. Take your own region first and read the rest as the menu brings them in.

    Finish with Cheese: milk, make and maturation, which reads a cheese through its milk, texture and method before its color: the crystals in Parmigiano Reggiano are not mold, and an orange Cheddar is not an older one for its color alone. Read the first plate opens the first folio at this level, and The wall shows all twenty. Each folio keeps the original poster archive beneath its guide, with the correction notes recorded against the poster; the guide is the lesson.

  6. The Palate

    2 at this level

    Sour and sweet are the Chef de Partie faults: the ones a station's own reductions and pan sauces make when the ratio slips, and each one is a lever against the other.

    Commis opened the repair table on flat and salty, the faults on either side of the salt cellar. Chef de Partie moves to sour and sweet. What they share: unlike flat, neither is fixed by adding what is missing. Both are faults of excess, like salty, and excess does not pour back out of a pot. The work at this level is learning what stands against each, in what order, and what each lever costs.

    Take sour first, the order the repair table keeps. Its symptom is exact: the acid arrives first and is still there after everything else has gone. Fat buffers rather than removes, so butter mounted in or a spoon of cream softens the edge without touching the acid itself. Sweetness is the other buffer, held below the threshold of tasting sweet: you are covering a fault, not making a sweet sauce. Baking soda neutralizes chemically, but pinches only, because it eats brightness along with the fault. Time mellows on its own, if you have any. Read that list as a lesson in restraint: two levers hide the fault, one changes the pot at a cost, and the last is time, which a service never has.

    Then sweet. The symptom is cloying by the third mouthful, with nothing to cut through it. Acid cuts. Salt reframes: the same sugar reads as seasoning rather than dessert. Bitterness opposes it directly, and the table names the sources: coffee, cocoa, char. Heat, the chile kind, distracts from it. Side by side the pair is a mirror: sweetness is the second lever for sour, acid is the first lever for sweet. A fault is a taste that leads and stays; the fix is a measured dose of its lever, tasted after every addition.

    The subsection opens two doors. The repair table holds the faults with their levers, in order: read sour and sweet there lever by lever, then find a real sauce that shows the symptom and name the fault before you reach for anything. Calibrate is the tasting ladder, and at Chef de Partie it asks every taste, salt, sweet, acid, bitter, umami and seasoned, to rung 2. The ladder climbs all six, not only the faults placed here: it trains the tongue that will catch them.

    Met here means two things: a fault is met when it has been named on a plate in the repair table, and a taste when its ladder is cleared to this level's rung. For this subsection that is sour and sweet each named on a plate, and the six tastes brought to rung 2 on Calibrate. Do the repair table first, because you cannot name what you have not read, and take Calibrate in short sittings between services, since the palate tires.

  7. Food Safety

    4 at this level

    Food safety at Chef de Partie is the cooling law and the way you actually cool, HACCP as a system, and the allergen protocol, which the guide calls critical twice.

    Commis gave you the danger zone and the two-hour rule. Chef de Partie asks you to own what happens to the stockpot after service, because a station makes more hot food than one service eats. The entries here sit in two pairs. The cooling law and the cooling method are one practice, stated as a number and as a technique. HACCP and the last entry are two systems a station runs inside. Take the cooling pair first.

    The cooling law is the guide's own figure: hot food to 21 C (70 F) within 2 hours, then to 4 C (39 F) within 4 more. The guide gives the two windows and nothing more; how your code counts them is the code's to say. How you get there is the cooling method: shallow pans, ice baths, never a stockpot straight into the walk-in. A full pot holds its heat for hours in a cold room and warms everything shelved around it, the slow cooling the two-hour rule at Commis already governs. Ladle the stock into shallow pans, or stand the pot in an ice bath and stir, and the law becomes something a station can meet.

    HACCP is the systematized version of what Commis taught as habit: identify hazards, control critical points, document. Those three verbs are the whole of the entry at Chef de Partie. So read HACCP here as a way of seeing your station, the moments where a time or a temperature decides whether the food is right and the log that proves you watched them. The figures come from your code and your chef, not from the guide.

    The last entry is the shortest and the heaviest: the allergen protocol, which the guide calls service-critical and legal-critical. The guide says so twice, and that is the whole of the Chef de Partie entry. What you can act on at Chef de Partie is knowing where your house keeps its protocol, following it as your house set it, and never improvising one.

    Nothing here is met or missed: food safety is read, never graded, and nothing about it is recorded. The level page opens two doors. The first is Read: the Food Safety page at this level's first slice. The second is The whole page. Take the first slice first, this level's entries, then read the whole page once for the gaps, what the guide names without stating.

  8. Service

    41 at this level

    Service at Chef de Partie is the glass explained: how a wine is described, the two pairing games, the bottles you will pour, and the cheese board's formula.

    Commis gave you the room's vocabulary and the bottle at the table. Chef de Partie asks you to say what is in the glass and put it next to a plate on purpose. The modules here fall into four groups: the grammar of wine, the cellar you will pour from, everything that is not wine, and the cheese board.

    Take How a Wine Is Described first, because every module after it uses its seven terms: acidity, tannin, body, dry and off-dry, oak, terroir, vintage. You have it when you answer "is it dry?" and "is it heavy?" correctly, know that off-dry is not sweet, and can hear which lever a guest means by "something smooth". Then The Two Games and the Switches. Every pairing is congruent, echoing the dish, or contrast, opposing it, and one switch does the work: salt loves acid and bubbles, fat wants cutting acid or matching richness, food acid demands equal wine acid, chile is inflamed by alcohol and tannin, sweet dishes need sweeter wine, bitter stacks with tannin, umami makes tannin harsh. Name the game and the switch in one sentence: that is what you say at the table.

    The cellar comes next, whites before reds. The Whites You Will Pour leads with three grapes, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling, three sentences each, with the aromatic whites beside Sauvignon Blanc. Two facts matter most: Riesling is the answer to chile heat, and Chardonnay's oak level must match the butter level. The Reds You Will Pour runs from the Bordeaux blend and Pinot Noir through the Rhône school and the food-wine trinity to the New World crowd-pleasers; its test is steering a table off Cabernet when the plate is chile or delicate fish. Bubbles, Sherry and the Fortified End sells Champagne as the rescue for almost any plate, not a toast, tells Prosecco from Cava from Pét-Nat, sets Fino beside Manzanilla, and matches Port or Madeira to cheese or chocolate.

    Everything That Is Not Wine covers the rest of the list. Beer pairs by its two families, sake climbs a polishing ladder and has a right serving temperature, tea and coffee are a service and not an afterthought, and a guest who is not drinking deserves a genuine non-alcoholic pairing. Take it straight after the cellar, while the pairing games are still fresh.

    The cheese board closes the service track at Chef de Partie, and its spine is a formula. The Board: Formula and Families asks for 3 to 5 cheeses spanning milk, texture and volume, 30 to 50 g a head, always at room temperature an hour out, and asks you to place any cheese in one of five families: fresh, bloomy, washed, semi-hard to hard, or blue. It also says why halloumi holds its shape under heat and gruyère flows. The Levant, South Asia and the Americas then covers the cheeses outside the classical board: paneer, akkawi and nabulsi, labneh, the Mexican trio and the American four, including why American cheese is engineered the way it is. Serve them without apology or condescension.

    Now the doors. Open the first module and work down the list in this order. The track shows all 27 modules and where this level's modules sit. The drill runs over this level's modules with the whole track as the field, and it is where met is decided: a module is met through its terms, every term of the module recalled met or close in the drill.
