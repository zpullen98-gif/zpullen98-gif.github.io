  1. Dishes

    18 at this level

    Eighteen plates that run a station: the pan sauce, the braise, bread and custard, live fire and the fish case.

    Level II holds eighteen dishes, every one a difficulty 2 plate: more than one technique, a wait or a build that no Commis plate asked of you. They come from eight semesters of the Path of Study, so take them by semester unless your station is putting one of them up tonight.

    Take Cacio e Pepe first: twenty minutes, and the test is toasting the pepper in a dry pan, then bringing starch, fat and cheese together without the sauce breaking. Then the soups. Soupe à l'Oignon is ninety minutes of caramelizing onions, a deglaze and the broiler; Barszcz is the same clock spent on the pot and then the sieve, a clear soup that shows every shortcut. Then Sole Meunière, fifteen minutes and the sauté station's exam: dredge, sear for the hard crust, brown the butter, plate.

    Four braises sit in the middle of this level. Boeuf Bourguignon, Coq au Vin, Ragù alla Bolognese and Jamaican Oxtail Stew share one idea: browned meat, low heat and time until it gives, and for the beef and the oxtail, until the collagen gives. Take Coq au Vin first, the shortest at 110 minutes and the full sequence of render, deglaze and reduce. Bourguignon runs three and a half hours; the ragù three, and its lesson is keeping the cooking liquid. The oxtail stew is four hours, opens on a caramel, is skimmed as it goes and closes on a reduced sauce.

    Rosemary Focaccia and Crème Brûlée stand alone in their semesters and are baking and pastry science at its base. The focaccia is one technique, proofing, stretched over 150 minutes: reading the dough rather than the timer. The brûlée is precision: temper the custard, pass it through a sieve, set it in a water bath, finish under the torch. Neither forgives a rushed hand.

    Jerk Chicken, Huli Huli Chicken and Memphis Dry-Rub Ribs are Fire & Smoke: the two chickens marinate, then grill over live coals; the ribs are rubbed dry and smoked low and slow for five hours, so take the chickens first. Then the fish case. Cantonese Steamed Fish and Miso-Glazed Black Cod are thirty minutes each at the stove, once the cod has had its days in the miso, and teach the courage to serve fish barely touched, one under the lid, one under the broiler. Linguine alle Vongole and Whole Grilled Fish with Ladolemono both mount an emulsion, one from clam liquor and pasta water, one from oil and lemon over a fish you fillet off the grill. Calamari Fritti closes the level: coat, deep fry, drain on a rack, never paper.

    A dish is met when it has been cooked and logged: not read, not watched, cooked, and the log kept. Read the semester opens the Path of Study at the semester that teaches the first dish you have not yet cooked. Cook the next dish opens that recipe's page. The library at this level is the recipe grid filtered to this level's difficulty. The Level test at the foot of the page runs untimed across the subsections and ends on what you missed with the right answers.

  2. Techniques

    50 at this level

    Fifty techniques, and every one of them is what a station is judged on: the sauces, the braise, the dough, the sugar and the spice rack.

    Fifty sit here, twenty-one with a written standard; the other twenty-nine are met by cooking any one of their recipes, and the fast way through is by family.

    Start with the sauces. Béchamel is the mother sauce on the roux you already own; beurre blanc is reduce, season, mount, plate; hollandaise and building an emulsion are one physics, fat into the water phase slowly under hard shear, and one rescue: a fresh base with the broken sauce whisked into it. Building an emulsion carries the standard, so take it first of the emulsions: no free fat sits ahead of the work, and at the plate it clings and holds a line.

    Then the braise. Braising has 39 recipes: deep brown before the liquid, liquid partway up and trembling, never boiling, a fork that twists with almost no resistance while the piece lifts out whole. Confit is the same patience in fat; rendering fat is the step before it, cold pan and slow rise. Marinating carries a standard on 70 recipes and one hard lesson: it works at the surface and no further, and longer is a different result, not a better one. Curing gravlax is salt and time doing the work, ceviche is acid doing it; keep the stated time and the stated cold. Tadka is judged on order and sound, hardest seeds first and delicate last; pounding a curry paste on the smear, hard and dry pounded before the wet goes in.

    The bread bench is the biggest family. Kneading dough, resting dough, proofing, scoring, working with masa and pleating dumplings carry standards; fresh pasta and handling phyllo are met by any of their recipes. Take the loaf in its own order: knead to satin and barely tacky; rest until it rolls without springing back, and let fermentation run in that rest as a rate, not a duration: steady warmth chosen on purpose, visible activity, a clean smell; then proof by volume and the poke test, never by the clock. Scoring's standard is written on skin over fat, not on a loaf: one clean draw at a slant on a cold dry surface, parallel lines a finger's width apart, through the skin and stopping in the fat, never the flesh. Masa is corn, not a gluten dough: the pinch that seals, the covered rest, the smell of toasted corn. Dumplings: a dry rim, a closed seam, even pleats.

    Pastry carries the last standards. Rubbing fat into flour and pie crust & blind baking share one catechism, cold fat in visible pieces and a cold shell into a hot oven; tempering a custard and the water bath are the egg held gently, a spoon that holds a line and a center that trembles as one piece; sugar stages & caramel go by a thermometer or a tested stage, never by time. Three more are air and a finish: creaming butter & sugar to pale, grown and still faintly gritty; folding stopped the moment the streaks vanish; icing & frosting onto a bake fully cool. The other two dozen, arepas to the tagine, risotto method to ramen broth, carry no standard: cook one recipe and it is met.

    Met is the app's one rule: a technique with a written standard is met when a cook of one of its recipes was held met against it; one without a standard is met by any cook of one of its recipes. Read the techniques is the index at this level; The next technique opens the first one not yet met.

    Next Level III asks for the techniques whose standard governs a whole service: lamination and letter-folding dough on the bench you built here, tempering chocolate beside the custard you tempered, velveting as the coat beyond the marinade, and then fire and the knife: grilling over live coals, asado fire, low & slow smoking, jerk, wok technique, butchery basics, filleting fish and shucking shellfish.

  3. The Lexicon

    257 at this level

    Level II is the station's vocabulary: heat and product first, then the case, the bake and the spice rack, each word a sentence you can say at the pass.

    Level II puts 255 terms in front of you, and the count is not the point. Most sit in the atlases: the spice rack, the herb and chile shelf, the vegetable, fruit, fungi, grain and pantry shelves, taken as the menu reaches for them. A smaller core, heat and product, runs every plate on your station, so start there.

    Take the heat words first. The Maillard Reaction, Braising & Stewing and Emulsions are the three to explain before service: why a wet surface never browns, why a boiled braise turns grainy, why a sauce breaks. Then Confit and Sous Vide, gentle heat and patience, confit being the ancestral sous vide, and Consommé & Clarification, the raft that filters a stock clear. Velveting & Marinades, Pressure & Slow Cooking, Fermentation & Pickling, Tempering (Eggs & Chocolate) and Infusion round out the set.

    The case comes next. On beef, Marbling is the word under all the others: the grading systems are marbling in different costumes, and more of it forgives higher doneness. Flank, Skirt & Hanger obey one law: salt or marinate, high heat to medium-rare at most, a rest, then slice thin against the grain. Dry-Aging is what the guest pays for in the trimmed loss and the funk. On fish, take Fatty vs. Lean Fish first, since matching method to fat solves half of fish cookery, then Round Fish vs. Flat Fish for the fillet count, and Mother Sauce Fish Stock: Fumet, the one stock where speed is the discipline.

    Baking science sits at the level's base. Gluten & Hydration is the language the rest are written in: flour choice, fat shortening the strands, water as a share of flour weight. Chemical Leaveners and Proofing, Oven Spring & the Crumb follow, one for the quick batter and one for the loaf, and Caramel Stages & Sugar Work is why the station owns a thermometer.

    The atlases are the long tail, and each has a map. For the spice rack, read Toasting, Blooming & the Tadka Doctrine before the single spices and blends, and keep Building the Working Spice Pantry, the atlas's capstone, open as the map: one gives you the sentence to keep, fat carries spices, the other the core dozen. For the Flavor Atlas, start on Tasting Vocabulary & Palate Training, Building Flavor in Layers and Acid Architecture, the three you act on tonight. Across the produce and pantry shelves, Seasonality & Ripeness is the rule the rest illustrate, and menu writing begins at the market calendar. Salt Types & When Each Wins settles arguments: read it with the heat words.

    The doors: Read the terms, then Flashcards and Quiz, both over this level's terms. A term is met when it has been recalled met or close on the flashcard ladder, or in the quiz, and a miss alone is never met, so run the ladder and let the quiz catch the rest. When you can give the sentence at the pass without the card, the level has done its job.

    Next Level III asks for the words that govern a whole service: Lamination, The Wok and Wok Hei & Stir-Fry Discipline, Grilling vs. Barbecue vs. Smoking, the specialist product behind a plate such as Guanciale, Jowl & Lardo or Black Truffle, and the numbers a sous reads in Menu Economics: Food Cost, Yield & Par and Prime Cost.

  4. The Floor Deck

    122 at this level

    At Level II the deck is the menu's second layer: the words a guest points at and expects a sentence back, from confit to 'nduja to pot de crème.

    Level II hands you 122 cards the floor asks about: what the thing is, how the kitchen handles it and what it is not. Most carry a note or a not-this line, and that is where the misses happen: coppa is not prosciutto, ghee is not clarified butter, a macaron is not a macaroon. Wherever a card has a pair, learn the pair.

    Take the six methods first, they describe everything else: confit, sous vide, blanched, fermented, rendered and pan-roasted. Confit and rendered pull opposite ways, meat under fat against fat out of meat. Then the sauces, the family the level is named for on its page, the mothers and their children: béchamel is the white base, demi-glace the roasted veal stock reduced by half and the base of bordelaise, béarnaise a child of hollandaise, and emulsion explains why aioli and remoulade hold. The cold, sharp ones travel together, mignonette, chimichurri and salsa verde; coulis is the sweet strained purée under dessert.

    Cuts come in two kinds of card: the steaks, hanger steak, skirt steak, flank steak, flat iron steak and ribeye cap, plus wagyu, a breed and not a cut, most giving the doneness the kitchen sends, and the braises, cheek and oxtail, with no temperature to ask for. Meats are the lean, farm-raised ones to explain, guinea hen against pheasant, quail, rabbit, venison, and the plates that need an honest word, foie gras, chicken liver, bone marrow, beef tongue. Charcuterie learns in contrasts: pancetta against bacon, coppa against prosciutto, pâté against terrine against rillettes, soppressata against 'nduja. The fish case is branzino against black sea bass, skate, monkfish, arctic char, blue crab and soft-shell crab, roe against caviar.

    Dairy is a ladder of clarified butter, brown butter and ghee, then compound butter, chèvre, burrata, mascarpone and crème fraîche. Starches split into shapes and grains, tagliatelle against pappardelle, cavatelli against orecchiette, agnolotti and gnocchi, then farro, bulgur, polenta and arborio. Mushrooms are foraged, chanterelle, morel, porcini, or farmed, king trumpet, hen of the woods, and the notes on morel and enoki say cooked through. The preparations split the raw plate, carpaccio against tartare, ceviche, tataki and gravlax, from the cooked one: ragout, croquette, roulade, amandine.

    Pastry runs from beignets, galette, pavlova and soufflé to the custards, crème anglaise against pastry cream, panna cotta and pot de crème, and the frozen four, gelato, granita, parfait and semifreddo. The pantry is the seasoning rack: miso, fish sauce, harissa, pimentón, sherry vinegar, preserved lemon, finishing salt. Language holds the menu's claims, dry-aged, pasture-raised, heritage breed and day-boat, and the not-this line says what each word is not.

    Flip cards first, the whole card, note and not-this included. Say it back is the one deck mode that counts as a round, recall before recognition, so give it the most time. The written test is untimed and ends on what you missed with the right answers and no number, so take it after a run of Say it back. The deck's landing shows what is owed today. A card is met when it has been graded met or close in the deck's modes; a miss alone is never met, so take a missed card through Say it back again.

  5. The Plates

    16 at this level

    Sixteen plates to read between services: two cut charts, three fish plates, ten regional larders and one cheese board, each with a list of what it gets wrong.

    The plates are the posters a kitchen hangs: a drawing, a run of names, a season line or a flavor line under each. At Level II they are the ones a station cook is expected to give a menu sentence on: the cuts, the fish case, the larder by region and the board. Each plate comes with its transcription and a list of what it gets wrong, and that list is the point: read the plate, then read what it gets wrong.

    Take Pork Cuts and Beef Cuts first, because the standard for this level names the cuts and grades and the butcher's order comes up at every station. Pork Cuts runs shoulder, belly, loin, rib, leg and shank; Beef Cuts runs from chuck through brisket, rib, short loin, short plate, flank, sirloin and round. Read the corrections slowly: sliced fresh belly is not bacon until it is cured, the jowl is the cheek and comes off the head, short ribs are one cut printed twice, and hanger steak lives with skirt in the short plate, not the flank.

    Pacific Fish, Atlantic Fish and Gulf Coast Fish Cuts & Flavor Profiles come next; take them as one case in three waters. The corrections here are identity, texture and the word a server reaches for: sablefish is soft and oily with a large silky flake, halibut is lean and clean and takes its richness from the sauce, grouper is lean and firm with a faintly sweet flavor and chunky flakes, not buttery. Two cards are printed twice: the tuna card repeats yellowfin and black cod is sablefish under another name. Tilapia is farmed freshwater and not an Atlantic fish at all. Learn each fish by what it does in the pan, not by the adjective on the poster.

    The larder by region is ten plates: Northwest Vegetables and Northwest Fruits, Southwest Vegetables and Southwest Fruits, Midwest Vegetables and Midwest Fruits, Northeastern Vegetables and Northeastern Fruits, Southeastern Vegetables and Southeastern Fruits. Start with the region you cook in and read the rest as a map. Their corrections are seasons, geography and what a thing is: Midwest broccoli arrives from June, not April; huckleberries run July to September; Northeastern cranberries come from the bogs of Massachusetts and New Jersey; a plantain has no Midwest season; pecans are a nut, mesquite is a legume pod milled to flour, and prickly pear is mild and floral, not tart. A few are plain misprints, fennel spelled tunnel, currants spelled current, and you read past them.

    Finish on The World's Great Cheeses, the board's vocabulary before Service at Level II asks for the board's formula. Its corrections are the kind a guest catches: Comté has no blue veining, Époisses is washed rind and never smoked, and Parmigiano Reggiano is pale straw yellow with a crystalline grain, not orange. The doors are two. Read the first plate opens one plate; The wall shows all twenty plates on the ladder, every level's, each with its transcription and what it gets wrong, and that is where you check a name before service. Met here is simple: the plates are read, never graded, and nothing about them is recorded.

    Named here Pork Cuts·Beef Cuts·Pacific Fish·Atlantic Fish·Gulf Coast Fish Cuts & Flavor Profiles·Northwest Vegetables·Northwest Fruits·Southwest Vegetables·Southwest Fruits·Midwest Vegetables·Midwest Fruits·Northeastern Vegetables·Northeastern Fruits·Southeastern Vegetables·Southeastern Fruits·The World's Great Cheeses

  6. The Palate

    2 at this level

    Sour and sweet are the Level II faults: the two a station's own reductions and pan sauces make when the ratio slips, and each one is a lever against the other.

    Level I opened the repair table on flat and salty, the two faults on either side of the salt cellar. Level II moves to sour and sweet. What they share: unlike flat, neither is fixed by adding what is missing. Both are faults of excess, like salty, and excess does not pour back out of a pot. The work at this level is learning what stands against each, in what order, and what each lever costs.

    Take sour first, the order the repair table keeps. Its symptom is exact: the acid arrives first and is still there after everything else has gone. Fat buffers rather than removes, so butter mounted in or a spoon of cream softens the edge without touching the acid itself. Sweetness is the other buffer, held below the threshold of tasting sweet: you are covering a fault, not making a sweet sauce. Baking soda neutralizes chemically, but pinches only, because it eats brightness along with the fault. Time mellows on its own, if you have any. Read that list as a lesson in restraint: two levers hide the fault, one changes the pot at a cost, and the last is time, which a service never has.

    Then sweet. The symptom is cloying by the third mouthful, with nothing to cut through it. Acid cuts. Salt reframes: the same sugar reads as seasoning rather than dessert. Bitterness opposes it directly, and the table names the sources: coffee, cocoa, char. Heat, the chile kind, distracts from it. Side by side the pair is a mirror: sweetness is the second lever for sour, acid is the first lever for sweet. A fault is a taste that leads and stays; the fix is a measured dose of its lever, tasted after every addition.

    The subsection opens two doors. The repair table holds the faults with their levers, in order: read sour and sweet there lever by lever, then find a real sauce that shows the symptom and name the fault before you reach for anything. Calibrate is the tasting ladder, and at Level II it asks every taste, salt, sweet, acid, bitter, umami and seasoned, to rung 2. The ladder climbs all six, not only the two faults placed here: it trains the tongue that will catch them.

    Met here means two things: a fault is met when it has been named on a plate in the repair table, and a taste when its ladder is cleared to this level's rung. For this subsection that is sour and sweet each named on a plate, and the six tastes brought to rung 2 on Calibrate. Do the repair table first, because you cannot name what you have not read, and take Calibrate in short sittings between services, since the palate tires.

  7. Food Safety

    4 at this level

    Level II food safety is the cooling law and the way you actually cool, HACCP as a system, and the allergen protocol, which the guide calls critical twice.

    Level I gave you the danger zone and the two-hour rule. Level II asks you to own what happens to the stockpot after service, because a station makes more hot food than one service eats. Four entries sit here in two pairs. The cooling law and the cooling method are one practice, stated as a number and as a technique. HACCP and the fourth entry are two systems a station runs inside. Take the cooling pair first: a thermometer tests you on it tonight.

    The cooling law is the guide's own figure: hot food to 21 C (70 F) within 2 hours, then to 4 C (39 F) within 4 more. The guide gives the two windows and nothing more; how your code counts them is the code's to say. How you get there is the cooling method: shallow pans, ice baths, never a stockpot straight into the walk-in. A full pot holds its heat for hours in a cold room and warms everything shelved around it, the slow cooling the two-hour rule at Level I already governs. Ladle the stock into shallow pans, or stand the pot in an ice bath and stir, and the law becomes something a station can meet.

    HACCP is the systematized version of what Level I taught as habit: identify hazards, control critical points, document. Those three verbs are the whole of the entry at Level II. So read HACCP here as a way of seeing your station, the moments where a time or a temperature decides whether the food is right and the log that proves you watched them. The figures come from your code and your chef, not from the guide.

    The fourth entry is the shortest and the heaviest: the allergen protocol, which the guide calls service-critical and legal-critical. The guide says so twice, and that is the whole of the Level II entry. What you can act on at Level II is knowing where your house keeps its protocol, following it as your house set it, and never improvising one.

    Nothing here is met or missed: food safety is read, never graded, and nothing about it is recorded. The level page opens two doors. The first is Read: the Food Safety page at this level's first slice. The second is The whole page. Take the first slice first, these four entries, then read the whole page once for the gaps, what the guide names without stating.

    Named here The cooling law·Shallow pans, ice baths: never a stockpot straight into the walk-in·HACCP is the systematized version: identify hazards, control critical points, document·Allergen protocol is service-critical and legal-critical

    Next Level III asks you to run the kitchen so any moment is inspection-ready: temps logged, FIFO labeled, hand sinks stocked and used, sanitizer buckets titrated. It asks what you do during a visit and after one, and with an illness complaint: take it seriously and kindly, gather specifics, preserve records and product, and remember that the cover-up is always worse than the event.

  8. Service

    41 at this level

    Level II service is the glass explained: how a wine is described, the two pairing games, the bottles you will pour, and the cheese board's formula.

    Level I gave you the room's vocabulary and the bottle at the table. Level II asks you to say what is in the glass and put it next to a plate on purpose. Eight modules sit here in four groups: the grammar of wine, the cellar you will pour from, everything that is not wine, and the cheese board.

    Take How a Wine Is Described first, because every module after it uses its seven terms: acidity, tannin, body, dry and off-dry, oak, terroir, vintage. You have it when you answer "is it dry?" and "is it heavy?" correctly, know that off-dry is not sweet, and can hear which lever a guest means by "something smooth". Then The Two Games and the Switches. Every pairing is congruent, echoing the dish, or contrast, opposing it, and one switch does the work: salt loves acid and bubbles, fat wants cutting acid or matching richness, food acid demands equal wine acid, chile is inflamed by alcohol and tannin, sweet dishes need sweeter wine, bitter stacks with tannin, umami makes tannin harsh. Name the game and the switch in one sentence: that is what you say at the table.

    The cellar comes next, whites before reds. The Whites You Will Pour leads with three grapes, Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc and Riesling, three sentences each, with the aromatic whites beside Sauvignon Blanc. Two facts matter most: Riesling is the answer to chile heat, and Chardonnay's oak level must match the butter level. The Reds You Will Pour runs from the Bordeaux blend and Pinot Noir through the Rhône school and the food-wine trinity to the New World crowd-pleasers; its test is steering a table off Cabernet when the plate is chile or delicate fish. Bubbles, Sherry and the Fortified End sells Champagne as the rescue for almost any plate, not a toast, tells Prosecco from Cava from Pét-Nat, sets Fino beside Manzanilla, and matches Port or Madeira to cheese or chocolate.

    Everything That Is Not Wine covers the rest of the list. Beer pairs by its two families, sake climbs a polishing ladder and has a right serving temperature, tea and coffee are a service and not an afterthought, and a guest who is not drinking deserves a genuine non-alcoholic pairing. Take it straight after the cellar, while the pairing games are still fresh.

    The cheese board closes the level, and its spine is a formula. The Board: Formula and Families asks for 3 to 5 cheeses spanning milk, texture and volume, 30 to 50 g a head, always at room temperature an hour out, and asks you to place any cheese in one of five families: fresh, bloomy, washed, semi-hard to hard, or blue. It also says why halloumi holds its shape under heat and gruyère flows. The Levant, South Asia and the Americas then covers the cheeses outside the classical board: paneer, akkawi and nabulsi, labneh, the Mexican trio and the American four, including why American cheese is engineered the way it is.
