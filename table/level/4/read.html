  1. Dishes

    6 at this level

    Project dishes, two from the fire and the rest from the capstone, that cook for a room rather than a plate and run from one hour to a twelve-hour day.

    None of the dishes at Chef is a plate you put up between tickets. Two come from Fire & Smoke: Tacos al Pastor (Home Trompo) and Central Texas Smoked Brisket, the semester where live fire is the ingredient and the cook is counted in hours. Four come from The Restaurateur's Capstone, the final semester that cooks for a room, not a plate: Sauce Espagnole & Demi-Glace, Dry-Brined Roast Turkey with Real Gravy, Salt-Baked Whole Fish and Baked Alaska. What they share is not one technique but a plan: all but the fish ask you to schedule the day and hold a standard over a long wait, and two are finished in front of people.

    Take Salt-Baked Whole Fish first. It is the shortest here at an hour and the only one at difficulty 2, and it teaches you to trust a method you cannot see into: the fish steams inside its crust and you judge it by time for its weight, or by a probe pushed through the crust, not by looking. Next take Sauce Espagnole & Demi-Glace, because it is the base a brown-sauce station stands on: a roux, seared bones, then hours of skimming and reducing until the sauce coats a spoon. It is the mother sauce Chef de Partie named and this level makes at scale.

    Then the holiday bird and the theater dessert. Dry-Brined Roast Turkey with Real Gravy is the bird: the salt goes on a day or more ahead, it roasts in a hot oven, and the gravy is built from the drippings and passed through a sieve so it arrives clean. Baked Alaska is a dessert made to be seen: a meringue whipped stiff enough to insulate the ice cream and cake beneath it, then browned fast. Both are cooked for a table that is watching.

    Leave the two fire dishes for days you own from morning to night. Tacos al Pastor (Home Trompo) is four hours: the marinade of acid, salt and time does its work before the stack goes near the heat, then the stack roasts in a hot oven and the slices are seared hard on the plancha as you carve. Central Texas Smoked Brisket is twelve. Low and slow smoking carries the dish, with a wrapped braise through the middle hours, and the last two steps, resting the meat and slicing against the grain, are where most briskets are lost. Cook the pastor before the brisket: it roasts in the oven and finishes on the plancha, a plate you can put up in one day before you give twelve hours to the smoker.

    The doors pick the next dish for you. Read the semester opens the Path of Study at the semester teaching the first dish you have not cooked, so start there and read the whole semester before you shop. Cook the next dish takes you straight to that recipe page. The library at this level shows the grid filtered to this difficulty when you want your own order. A dish is met when it has been cooked and logged: cooked once, honestly, and written down. Nothing else is asked of it.

    Named here Salt-Baked Whole Fish·Sauce Espagnole & Demi-Glace·Dry-Brined Roast Turkey with Real Gravy·Baked Alaska·Tacos al Pastor (Home Trompo)·Central Texas Smoked Brisket

    Next There is no level above Chef. What keeps them sharp is the calendar: put the brisket and the bird on it once a season, make demi-glace whenever the stock pot is on anyway, and log each cook so the next one starts from what you learned.

  2. Techniques

    8 at this level

    Techniques on one to five recipes each, taught by no semester, each turning on one moment you have to be present for.

    At Sous Chef the techniques governed a whole service. Here they sit on the fewest recipes in the guide, one to five each, and no semester teaches them. None carries a written standard, so each is met by any cook of one of its recipes.

    Start with the three that make rice the dish: Biryani & dum, Paella & socarrat, Sushi rice & rolling. In biryani the rice is parboiled, layered with the marinated or part-cooked meat, and the pot is sealed and finished on the lowest heat so steam does the last of the cooking; dum is that sealed finish, and the discipline is not lifting the lid. Paella is the opposite instinct: a wide shallow pan, rice never stirred once it is in the stock, and the heat pushed in the last minutes so the bottom sets into socarrat, the toasted crust you hear crackle before you see it. Sushi rice is seasoned with vinegar while still warm and folded with a cutting stroke, then rolled on the mat. Within the rice, biryani first, since the sealed pot forgives a minute and socarrat does not, and sushi rice at the end of the group.

    The second group is dough: Hand-pulled noodles, Tandoor breads, The pretzel bath. All three treat gluten as a material, and the Lexicon anchor under the noodles (gluten and hydration) is the key: the elastic network built when wheat's proteins meet water and agitation, developed by kneading and relaxed by rest. A noodle dough is worked hard and rested long so it stretches instead of snapping. Tandoor breads want a soft dough slapped against a wall far hotter than a home oven; on a line, a stone or a skillet at full heat stands in for the wall. The pretzel bath is the shortest step and the least intuitive: shaped dough dipped in an alkaline solution before it bakes, which is where the deep mahogany color and the tight, chewy skin come from. Take the dough next, beginning with the pretzel bath, one step on an ordinary dough, and ending with the noodles, which take longest to learn.

    Mole and The soufflé stand apart: both are built before the final heat. A mole is stages, dried chiles toasted and soaked, nuts, seeds and spices toasted on their own, everything ground, fried as a paste and thinned into sauce over a long simmer. The failure is a scorched chile, and it takes seconds. The soufflé rests on the second anchor (creaming, foaming and cake structure): the rise is air you built before the oven, whites whipped to a foam and folded into a base, and once air is in you fold and never stir, because every rough stroke costs height. Mole follows the dough. Take the soufflé last of all, since with one recipe there is nowhere to hide.

    Read the techniques opens the index at this level. The next technique takes you straight to the first not yet met. This level's Dishes are a separate list; nothing here waits on them.

    Named here Biryani & dum·Paella & socarrat·Sushi rice & rolling·Hand-pulled noodles·Tandoor breads·The pretzel bath·Mole·The soufflé

  3. The Lexicon

    39 at this level

    At Chef the Lexicon is mostly the building, not the plate: restaurant finance and opening, and a handful of the rarest things a kitchen ever handles.

    Chef holds the rest of Restaurant Finance & Opening and the last of the Fruit, Fungi and Vegetable atlases. Nothing here is drilled at a station. It is owned, without notes. Take Why Restaurants Fail: The Honest List first, because most of the finance entries answer one line of it. Then read the money spine in order: The Concept & Business Plan, Feasibility & Market Analysis, Startup Costs, The Pro Forma & Realistic Projections, Funding, The Restaurant P&L, Line by Line, Break-Even Analysis and Cash Flow & the Working Capital Trap. The discipline they share: build the number bottom-up, then secure more than the spreadsheet says. Carry one figure out with you, break-even in covers a day, the number a host stand can chase.

    The Owner's Capstone names three documents that can follow you home, so read The Lease, Personal Guarantees & the Good-Guy Clause and Payroll, Taxes & Compliance as a set, with Legal Entity & Partnership Agreements and Deal Structures & the Investor's Waterfall beside them: those decide who owns the name, and who is paid first, when partners stop speaking. Follow the pointers: Startup Costs sends you to The Build-Out, Licenses & Permits hands off to Liquor License Economics, and the liability that license brings is a line in Insurance.

    The rest go in pairs: Site Selection & the Location Math with TI Allowance & Lease Negotiation, The Chart of Accounts & 4-Week Periods with The Org Chart & Management Structure, The Tech Stack with Third-Party Delivery Economics, Catering, Events & Revenue Diversification with Marketing & the Regulars Math, The Opening with Culture & the Owner's Job, and Growth last.

    The atlas entries come through rarely, each with one handling rule. Four fruits: Feijoa drops when ripe and browns fast once cut, so eat it with a spoon; Mangosteen rind carries a bitter latex that stains, so cut shallow, and the lobes on the base count the segments; Medlar is picked hard and bletted for weeks until it yields like a soft persimmon; Tamarillo will not peel raw, so cut a cross, blanch and shock it like a tomato, red types tart, gold sweeter.

    Three fungi are ruled by water. Lion's Mane will not brown until its water is gone: a dry pan, a weight, and only then fat. The scent of Matsutake leaves with the steam, so wet or long heat strips it: a brief fierce grill, or torn into a clear dashi. White Truffle goes the same way in a pan and is meaningfully weaker three days out of the ground: shave it at the table, last, over warm fat. Two vegetables are bitterness managed by dark and cold: Cardoon is blanched in the field, and most of it is boiled in acidulated water first; Puntarelle is split into strips and soaked in ice water until it curls, then dressed with anchovy, garlic, vinegar and oil.

    Read the terms opens the Lexicon at this level; take one finance entry between services, and the atlas ones at the walk-in, thing in hand. Flashcards and Quiz run over this level's terms, where a term is met when recalled met or close on the flashcard ladder, or in the quiz, and a miss alone is never met.

  4. The Floor Deck

    88 at this level

    At Chef the deck is the rare word on a serious menu, and you own each one well enough to explain it at the table without looking.

    Most cards carry a not-this line naming what the word gets mistaken for: tenderloin for Lomo, tuna for Escolar, bulgur for Freekeh. Where a card has one, read it as carefully as the why, because that difference is what you will be asked to explain.

    Take first the cards that lean on a word you already hold from Sous Chef, because the difference is the whole lesson. Grenobloise is meunière's brown butter with capers, diced peeled lemon in place of juice, and croutons. Washed Rind is the orange, tacky, pungent opposite of a bloomy rind, and most Robiola is paler and milder than Taleggio. Secreto Ibérico is a fresh cut for a fast grill, not the cured jamón.

    Then the proteins and the cures. Chicken Oysters are the two coin-sized pieces of dark meat on the back above the thigh. Moulard is the sterile hybrid behind most foie gras and a thick magret. Koji-Aged meat is softened in days by a mold culture, where dry-aging takes weeks. Ikejime says only how a fish was killed and bled, never its species or cut. Cured Lomo, Culatello and Bottarga are served uncooked: know which is the whole loin, which the boned rear muscle of the leg cured in a bladder, which the dried roe sac grated like cheese. Much of Escolar's oil is wax esters the body cannot digest, which is why kitchens keep portions small.

    The rest sell on texture, scent or label. Abalone Mushroom holds a springy chew, Lion's Mane sears into strands like crab, Chicken of the Woods tears like cooked chicken and must be cooked through, and Matsutake loses its pine scent to hard cooking. Farro Piccolo is einkorn, Acquerello is a brand of husk-aged carnaroli, Freekeh is green wheat charred and rubbed. Banyuls, Saba and Verjus are three grape products with three different balances of sweet and sour, and Brebis is ewe's milk cheese, rich and sweet. Last, three classics: Nage is a simple poaching broth served as the sauce, not a clarified consommé; a Ballotine is a boned bird or leg rebuilt around forcemeat; a Vacherin is a crisp meringue shell, most often filled with ice cream.

    Open Flip cards for the Floor Deck at this level to read them first, then move to Say it back, recall before recognition, the one deck mode that counts as a round: say the gist, and the not-this where the card has one, aloud before you turn the card. A card is met when it has been graded met or close in the deck's modes; a miss alone is never met. The deck's landing shows what is owed today. The written test for this level is untimed and ends on what you missed, with the right answers and no number.

  5. The Palate

    2 at this level

    Spicy and muddy, the faults with the least obvious levers, and every taste on the ladder taken to rung 5.

    The repair table opens at Commis with flat and salty, the faults with a direct counter, and spicy and muddy are the last the level ladder places. What they share is that the fault is already in the pot. Only fat pulls heat back out, and nothing pulls brown out, so the obvious move is rarely the whole answer, and that is why they wait for Chef.

    Take spicy first. The symptom is that the heat has stopped being a flavor and become the only one. The table lists five levers in order of gentleness. Dairy or fat dissolves capsaicin and is the one that physically removes heat. Sugar soothes. Acid refocuses the palate elsewhere. Starch dilutes. More bland base is the honest fix, and usually the only one that works at service. Read the five in two groups: sugar and acid change how the heat is felt, fat, starch and base change how much heat is in each spoon. At the pass, with a sauce that has gone over, more base is the answer, and the table says so. Reach for fat only where the dish takes it, taste, and expect to end on base. Sugar and acid are for a dish that is close, not one that is on fire.

    Then muddy. The symptom is deep, brown and indistinct: every layer present and none of them legible. You will meet it in the braise that has reduced all afternoon and the sauce built on a dark stock. The table gives a single lever, brightness at the top: fresh herb, zest, raw allium, the finishing register applied as medicine. The fault is not an excess of any one taste but a loss of contrast, and contrast cannot be cooked back in. The fix sits on the plate, on top, added raw at the last moment.

    The order matters because these faults teach opposite lessons. Spicy shows the difference between masking a fault and removing it. Muddy shows that a fault can have a single lever on the table and that it can live outside the pot. Owning the menu means going upstream of both: heat concentrates as liquid leaves, so a chile added early to something that will reduce is a decision, and a finishing register belongs on the recipe card, not left to whoever notices at the pass.

    Two doors open from this page. The repair table lists the faults with their levers, in order; a fault is met when it has been named on a plate there, so name spicy and muddy on plates you have tasted. Calibrate is the tasting ladder, and at Chef it asks each of the six tastes, salt, sweet, acid, bitter, umami and seasoned, to rung 5; a taste is met when its ladder is cleared to that rung. Take Calibrate alongside the table, because a palate at rung 5 is the one that can tell a repaired plate from a merely less wrong one.

    Named here Spicy·Muddy

    Next Nothing sits above Chef. What keeps the palate sharp is use: taste every sauce before it leaves the pass, and when you name a fault, name its lever in the same breath, because the brigade learns to taste from the chef.

  6. Food Safety

    6 at this level

    At Chef the Food Safety page turns over: one fact about your jurisdiction, and five places where the guide names a rule it never writes down.

    Commis to Chef de Partie read what the guide states, and Sous Chef met the first place it names a rule without stating it, sanitizer buckets titrated to a concentration never given. Chef makes that reading the subject. Only one item here is a fact you can learn: Know your jurisdiction's system, with letter grades posted in the window in one city, a count of points in another, and a risk-based frequency that decides how often the door opens. The other five are gaps, places where the guide names a subject and never states the rule. A chef owns both, because a chef is the person the missing rule lands on.

    Take the jurisdiction first, because it is the one item here that is a fact and not a gap. Sous Chef framed one kitchen, one inspector, one set of findings in writing. At Chef the framing changes with the jurisdiction: what is posted, how it is counted and how often anyone comes are set by the authority that holds your permit, not by this guide. Find out which system you are under.

    Then read the gaps in pairs, because they share a shape. Two are half a law. The two-hour rule governs everything left in the danger zone, and cooling is given a law of its own, but reheating and keeping food hot on the line are never given one. Raw below ready-to-eat in storage gives the shelf order, and how frozen product is brought out for the cook is not covered anywhere in the guide. In both, the guide states the first half of a process and goes silent on the second. The page marks the silence and stops. It does not fill it with a figure. You must, and not from this page: that number belongs to your jurisdiction's code and the written plan on your own wall.

    Two more are asserted without being specified. Identify hazards, control critical points, document is HACCP compressed to three verbs. What makes those verbs a working system, the limits, the frequency, the verification and the records, is not there. The protocol for a guest's declared restriction, which the guide calls service-critical and legal-critical, is asserted twice as critical and never specified once. Read both as a list of what your own documents must hold, not as a lesson in how to hold it.

    The last gap is the plainest. The power outage's food-disposition rules are a pointer to rules the guide does not give. When the walk-in goes dark you will have to act, and the guide will not be what you act from. Know where that answer lives before you need it.

    The doors are two: Read, which opens the Food Safety page at this level's first slice, and The whole page. Take the slice between services and the whole page once, end to end, because a gap only reads correctly beside the statement it leans on. Met here is one line: Food Safety is read, never graded, and nothing about it is recorded. What you carry out is a map of where the guide ends and your jurisdiction and your own plan begin.

  7. Service

    31 at this level

    Service at Chef leaves the cellar and the boards for the bar, then for why the shift makes money.

    At Sous Chef, Service was the grape atlas and the boards: what is in the bottle and on the slate. Chef holds the last modules of the track, and they turn the same server toward the bar and the ledger. The Cocktail Templates, Bar Craft and Why the Shift Makes Money share one bar: a guest orders by name, you say what is in it or build it to spec, and the house counts what it sold. Two are for anyone on the floor; one is for the server who also builds the drink.

    Take The Cocktail Templates first. Seven templates, and every classic collapses into one of them: the sour, the old fashioned, the stirred aristocrats (Martini and Manhattan), highballs and the Collins family, the Negroni and its equal-parts architecture, tiki and the rum canon, and the hot drinks, toddy and Irish coffee among them. Learn the seven and a name you have never poured still answers to a shape you know: you can say what is in it for anything ordered by name.

    Bar Craft (Server-Bartenders Only) is the longest module, for the hands that make the drink. Start with the first law, shake what is cloudy and stir what is clear, then ice and dilution as an ingredient, then the jigger and the spec, because the rest of the build sits on them. After them come the tools and the programs: the Hawthorne and julep strainers and the double strain, egg whites and the dry shake, the syrups (simple, rich and the oleo), the citrus program, the expressed twist, glassware and the chill law, batching, and bar mise within a three-step reach. It closes on the starting bar, zero-proof cocktails and the spritz. If you never stand behind the bar, read it once anyway, so you know what the bartender is doing while your table waits.

    Why the Shift Makes Money comes last because it prices the other two. Its terms cross wine, bar and finance: wine list engineering and the markup question; by-the-glass economics, Coravin and corkage; pour cost; the well, the call and the back bar; covers, turns and dayparts; reservations, no-shows and the book; tipping models, pools, tip credits and service charges; and menu pricing and psychology. The outcome is plain: you understand what your recommendation does to the room's numbers, where the value bottles sit, what a glass really costs to pour, and how the tip pool you are in actually works.

    The level page opens three doors. The first module opens The Cocktail Templates. The track opens every module, the right door if the Sous Chef boards have gone faint. The drill is the service drill over this level's modules with the whole track as the field. Met has one rule: a module is met through its terms, every term of the module recalled met or close in the drill.

    Named here The Cocktail Templates·Bar Craft (Server-Bartenders Only)·Why the Shift Makes Money

    Next Nothing sits above Chef in Service. What keeps it sharp is the drill, and the bar itself: every order is a template to call, and every bottle you sell is a line in the numbers you now read.
