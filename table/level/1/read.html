  1. Dishes

    10 at this level

    Your first plates teach heat, the knife and the pan a few techniques at a time, and every one of them should go up without notes.

    Most of the dishes placed at Commis come from the opening semesters of the Path of Study, and the library rates nearly all of them Easy. Each rests on techniques from your first weeks, and a couple of runs should put it in your hands.

    Start where the Path starts, with Knife & Fire Foundations, and take The French Omelette first. It is eight minutes of heat control, seasoning and pan work, and the library rates it a step harder than the rest here, so cook it until the heat stops surprising you. Ratatouille is the knife-cut dish: dice, julienne and bias, then the sauté. Chicken Piccata strings four moves onto one plate: the dredge, the sear, the deglaze and a reduced pan sauce. Salade Niçoise asks for blanching and shocking, so what you blanch keeps its color and stops cooking the moment it hits the ice.

    Then the simmer. Miso Soup teaches dashi and soaking dried goods back to life, and the semester asks for dashi's restraint and for watching the bubble, not the clock. Pasta e Fagioli teaches sweating aromatics, soft and never browned, which is the onion's patience in a single pot.

    The sauté station follows, the semester the Path calls the line cook's exam. Shrimp Scampi repeats the piccata's sear and deglaze on something that cooks in moments, so pull it once it turns opaque. Chicken Teriyaki is about resting the meat and slicing against the grain before it reaches the plate. Garides Saganaki sweats its aromatics, takes a flambé and keeps its cooking liquid.

    Panna Cotta sits in Pastry Fundamentals, later on the Path, but the library rates it Easy and its techniques are small ones: the liquid held at a bare simmer below the boil, passed through a sieve, and the set judged by the wobble. Cook it whenever you want a dessert on the log.

    A dish is met when it has been cooked and logged, and nothing more is asked of it. Use the Read the semester door to open the Path at the semester that teaches the first dish you have not yet cooked, Cook the next dish to go straight to its recipe page, and The library at this level for the full grid of Easy plates. The Level test at the foot of the page runs across every subsection, untimed, and hands back what you missed with the right answers.

  2. Techniques

    42 at this level

    The techniques a station runs on all day: the cuts, salted water, the sweat, the sear, the simmer, and the pan you never wash before it has made its sauce.

    Take the board first. Knife cuts sit on more recipes than almost anything else here, and the standard is uniformity, not speed: any three pieces should match. Then the pot. Salted water & the float test, blanching & shocking and the bare simmer are one lesson at two heats, the rolling boil and the shiver under it: water seasoned before anything goes in, an ice bath ready before the timer starts, a surface watched rather than set once. Skimming and keeping the cooking liquid come after: lift the foam, hold it below the boil, save the liquid.

    The pan is one lesson with three heats. Sweating aromatics is low heat and no color. Sautéing & the shallow fry is a single layer kept moving, light color fast. Searing is the hard crust: a pan that never falls quiet and a piece that releases itself. A recipe that says cook the onions means one of the three; learn which before the rest of the pan. Deglazing & pan sauces, reducing a sauce and making a roux follow: the fond brown and not black, salt at the end because it concentrates, the roux taken to its stage and no further.

    Roasting in a hot oven, the broiler and charring over open flame are dry heat from three directions: all around, from above, and straight against the surface. Each has its marks: the oven fully up to heat and air around every piece; the broiler lit first and somebody standing at it, because pale turns bitter in tens of seconds; nothing between flame and surface, no acrid edge. Griddle & plancha work and tortillas on the comal want full contact with dry metal. Resting meat & slicing against the grain closes the roast: off before the target, a dry board when you cut. The fryer is a chain in order: the coat before the fry, deep frying at temperature, draining fried food on a rack, salted within seconds.

    Salt, cold, time and hands come next. Salting to draw the water out is salt on every face, somewhere for the water to go, and that water poured off before heat; brining & curing is full commitment, timed against weight and thickness, dried before anything crisps. Chilling to firm and the overnight rest are waiting done properly: cooled fast first, cold through, not cold at the rim. Shaping by hand, whipping a meringue and the wobble & the skewer belong to the bake: matched pieces with sealed seams, stiff peaks with a slight bow, a skewer tested at the true center. Caramelizing onions, brown butter, poaching eggs, trussing, flambé and sourdough starter carry no written standard.

    Eight more carry their own marks. Soaking dried goods back to life is tested at the center of the largest piece; steaming is the lid left on over a rolling boil; straining & passing through a sieve and mashing & puréeing are judged on the tongue; dashi is pale gold and clear, kombu out before the first bubbles. Frying the paste until the oil splits, dry-pan toasting and egg wash & the baker's shine each have one signal: clear fat pooling at the edges, fragrance before color, a thin even coat with nothing pooled.

    Met has one rule. A technique with a written standard is met when a cook on one of its recipes was graded met against that standard; one without a standard is met by any cook of one of its recipes.   3. The Lexicon

    268 at this level

    At Commis the Lexicon is the words the pass will use on you: the prep and pan verbs, the staples, and how to taste.

    Start with the words the first semesters lean on, because the rest of the level hangs on them. Mise en Place is the habit before the skill: read the recipe fully, prep completely, then light the burner. Knife Cuts: The Classical Ladder gives you brunoise, julienne, batonnet and the dice, and uniformity is the exam: speed comes later. Then the pan verbs. Sweat vs. Sauté vs. Sear separates three different flavors that a recipe may lump together as cooking the onions, and Deglaze & Fond turns the browned layer left in the pan into the base of a sauce.

    Take the heat words next, as one family, since each is a decision about temperature. Poach, Simmer, Boil teaches you to watch the bubbles, not the dial. Blanch & Shock is brief boiling in heavily salted water, then an ice bath, the move that lets a short service plate a long mise. Three entries build the sauce corner, taken in this order: Stocks; Roux, Slurry & Liaison; then Reduction, Nappe & Mounting. Skim the stock and never salt it, pick the thickener for the heat the sauce will see next, and reduce before you season. Searing & Pan Technique and Resting, Carryover & the Thermometer close the set: dry the protein, never crowd the pan, cook to temp and not to time.

    The produce and staples are the words on your board from day one. Allium Family, Yellow Onion and Garlic explain why the size of the cut sets the pungency, and why garlic goes in after the onions, off the fiercest heat. Mirepoix, Trinity, Sofrito & Friends names the aromatic bases that open cuisine after cuisine. Starchy vs. Waxy Potatoes tells you to match the potato to the job before the recipe, and Hearty vs. Tender Herbs tells you when each goes in: rosemary and thyme early, basil and parsley at the end. The rest, such as the fruit and vegetable atlases, cuts like Chuck and pans like Cast Iron and Stainless Clad (Tri-Ply), read best as they land on your prep list, not front to back.

    Keep one group open for the whole level: Tasting & Seasoning Like a Chef, Salt & Perception and The Repair Table. Taste at every stage and season in layers. The Palate on this page puts the repair table's first two faults on a plate, lever by lever; here you learn the words your chef will use at the pass when a plate comes back.

    Use the doors in order. Read the terms opens the Lexicon at this level, and Flashcards and Quiz then run over the same words. A term is met once your recall of it is marked met or close on the flashcard ladder, or in the quiz; a miss alone is never met.

  4. The Floor Deck

    110 at this level

    The Floor Deck at Commis is the menu words a guest will ask about tonight, taken by method first, then the cuts, the fish case and the larder.

    These are the deck's first cards, one to a menu word. Each card gives a gist, a why and a menu line, and most give the thing it is not. Read the line first, the way the word reaches you on the floor, then the not-this where the card has one: that is what a guest will confuse it with.

    Take the twenty methods first; every other card leans on them. Boiled, Simmered and Poached are one ladder of heat in liquid, from a rolling bubble to none at all, and Steamed sits above the water, not in it. Seared, Sautéed, Fried and Grilled are the dry, hot, fast side, with Blackened and Chicken-Fried as two Southern turns in a skillet. Braised is the slow one, half in liquid under a lid; Roasted is the oven's dry heat, fast or slow. Brined, Cured and Pickled happen to food before or instead of cooking; Smoked either flavors it cold or cooks it low and slow. Learn each by the neighbor it is set against: Simmered is not Boiled, Seared is not Caramelized, Cured is not Smoked.

    Bacon, Prosciutto, Pastrami, Corned Beef and Chorizo tie straight back to Brined, Cured and Smoked, so take them while those cards are fresh. Then the block: the steakhouse cuts run from Tenderloin, the softest and mildest, through New York Strip to Ribeye, the most marbled, and Porterhouse and T-Bone hold two of them on one bone. Brisket, Short Rib, Shank and Shoulder want hours of low heat, and braised Short Rib has no temperature to ask for. Pork Chop, Rack of Lamb and Duck Breast each say how the kitchen sends them. Salmon, Tuna, Swordfish and Halibut each carry a doneness note, Flounder, Snapper and Trout are the mild, lean fish, and Shrimp, Scallops, Oysters and Clams each carry a sizing or serving note.

    The rest is the larder and the dessert menu, fastest by family: Parmesan, Ricotta, Feta and Blue Cheese; Grits, Risotto, Ravioli and Tortellini; Stock, Jus, Hollandaise, Vinaigrette and Pesto; Brioche, Sourdough, Puff Pastry and Tart. Sort the sweets by what holds them: Custard and Crème Brûlée by egg, Mousse by the whipped cream or whites folded in, Ice Cream, Sherbet and Sorbet by how much dairy is churned in. Then Jam against Compote by how far the fruit is cooked, Marmalade by its citrus peel, and Kimchi against a vinegar pickle, the sour grown rather than poured. Among the mushrooms, the pair is Truffle against Truffle Oil.

    Save the nine menu words for last: Prix Fixe against Tasting Menu, À la Carte, Market Price, and the claims House-Made, Grass-Fed, Wild-Caught, Organic and Heirloom, each saying one narrow thing, not the wider thing a guest hears. Market Price has a job attached: know tonight's figure before service.

    Take the doors in this order. Flip cards is where you read, one section at a time. Say it back is the one deck mode that counts as a round: recall before recognition. The written test is untimed and ends on what you missed with the right answers and no number: use it to find the section you skipped. The deck landing shows what is owed today. A card is met once it has been graded met or close in the deck's modes; a miss on its own is never met.

  5. The Plates

    3 at this level

    Folios for the first weeks: the chicken on your board, the spices in their whole form and the mushrooms in the walk-in, six subjects to each.

    Each plate is a folio: one illustration keyed to six numbered subjects, a guide that names each one and says how to tell it from its neighbor, and a short list of sources for reading further. Commis holds Chicken: cuts and giblets, Spices: from fruit to flower and Mushrooms: six cultivated market forms. They are read, never graded, and nothing about them is recorded; the six questions at the foot of each folio are a quiet check for you alone.

    Take the chicken first, because it is the bird you portion this week. Four subjects are the portions. The breast is the white meat of the chest; the thigh sits above the leg joint and the drumstick below it, both dark meat; and the wing carries the small drumette, which belongs to the wing and not the leg. The last two are the giblets a kitchen keeps. The liver is a soft organ, not a muscle cut, and the gizzard is the thick, muscular stomach that grinds the bird's food, which is how you tell the two apart.

    Then the spices, each shown whole before it becomes a powder. Black pepper is the fruit of a tropical vine, and black, white and green peppercorns are one species handled three ways. Cumin is slender and ridged where coriander is round, and coriander is the seed of the plant whose leaves you call cilantro. Cinnamon is rolled bark, turmeric is an underground rhizome, and cloves are dried flower buds with no tie to a clove of garlic. The lesson of this folio is origin: know which part of the plant you are holding.

    Leave the mushrooms for last. These are market mushrooms, not a guide for foraging. The white button, the cremini and the portobello are one species in three forms, so the color of the cap is not an age clock. Shiitake has an umbrella cap and a stem that can be tough enough to save for stock. Oyster mushrooms grow in fan-shaped clusters and take their name from their form, and maitake, also called hen of the woods, is a frilled cluster rather than a single cap.

    Read the first plate opens the chicken folio, and The wall shows all twenty folios by kind, these three among them. Beneath each guide sits the original poster archive: the earlier poster, its transcription and the correction notes recorded against it, kept for reference. Learn from the six-subject guide, which is the current lesson, and open the archive only when you want the history.

    Named here Chicken: cuts and giblets·Spices: from fruit to flower·Mushrooms: six cultivated market forms

  6. The Palate

    2 at this level

    Two faults open the repair table at Commis, flat and salty, and each has three levers you pull in order, one at a time.

    The palate at Commis asks two things of you. Name flat and salty, the two faults the repair table opens with, each on a plate, and take each of the six tastes on the ladder to rung 1. These two faults come first because a commis meets them every shift: the soup that tastes of nothing and the stock reduced too far that sends the guest to the water glass. Between them they teach the one habit this subsection rests on. Taste, name the fault, pull one lever, taste again.

    Flat is the fault where every flavor is present and none of it arrives, the dish tasting like a photograph of itself. Its levers run in a fixed order and the order is the lesson. Salt is always first, because most flat dishes are unsalted dishes. If the plate is already salted, acid is the next lever, not more salt. If it is salted and bright and still flat, umami is the third and the check ends there.

    Salty is the fault that reaches for the water glass before the second bite. Its levers are dilute, bulk it out and counter it. Dilute means unsalted liquid or more of the base. Bulk it out means starch or cream, volume the salt has to spread through. Counter it means acid and a touch of sugar, which bend the perception without removing any salt. The potato trick mostly just dilutes, so count it as the dilute lever.

    Take flat first. The table opens with it, and its first lever is the same one that causes salty when pulled too hard. Learn to salt in pinches with a taste between each, and you will reach for salty's levers far less often. Then take salty, and read its levers as an order as well: dilute and bulk before you counter.

    The two doors do the work. The repair table lists the faults with their levers, in order, so open it with a plate in front of you and walk flat and salty lever by lever until the plate tells you which one it wanted. Calibrate is the tasting ladder, each taste to this level's rung, which at Commis is rung 1 for salt, sweet, acid, bitter, umami and seasoned. Salt, acid and umami are also the three levers of flat, so the ladder and the table teach each other.

    Met here has one rule. A fault is met when it has been named on a plate in the repair table, and a taste when its ladder is cleared to this level's rung.

  7. Food Safety

    6 at this level

    Four things before you touch a station: the danger zone, the two-hour rule, cross-contamination and FIFO, read once and kept for good.

    Food Safety at Commis is a short subsection and the one the whole kitchen assumes you already have. The items here fold into four ideas: the danger zone, the two-hour rule, cross-contamination and FIFO. The only thing asked of you is that you read it and carry it to the line.

    Take the danger zone first, because the rest hangs off it. The guide states it as a band, 4 to 60 C (40 to 140 F), where pathogens multiply, and then states the one rule the band produces: the two-hour rule governs everything left in it. The same clause goes on to the cooling law, with its figures, its shallow pans and its ice baths, but the cooling law is placed at Chef de Partie, so read it now as the shape of what comes next. Notice what the guide states and what it leaves out: the band and the two hours are stated; what a cook does with a pan that has crossed the two hours is not, and this primer does not supply it.

    Cross-contamination is the second idea, and the guide gives it three lines and nothing more: raw below ready-to-eat in storage, color-coded boards, and hands and towels as vectors. Read that as two controls and the vectors they answer, not as a full procedure. The guide names color-coded boards without stating which color belongs to which product, and this primer leaves that gap where the guide left it. What you can act on today is the order of the walk-in shelf and the towel on your shoulder: the first puts raw below ready-to-eat, the second the guide names as a vector.

    FIFO comes last of the four because it is a habit rather than a fact: first in, first out, and the guide's practical gloss is to label and date everything, with its own aside that masking tape and a Sharpie run more restaurants than any software. The same clause names HACCP as the systematized version, identify hazards, control critical points, document, and names a service-critical and legal-critical protocol beside it. Both are placed at Chef de Partie, so at Commis you take the tape and the pen and leave the system for later. The room's half of that protocol is not deferred, though: Service on this page already asks it of you, in What We Screened and What We Did Not, as the one question you never answer from the app. The clause closes on the line worth keeping: none of this is glamour; all of it is the license to do the glamorous parts.

    The doors are plain. Read: the Food Safety page at this level's first slice, and then the whole page, so you have seen where the cooling law and HACCP sit before anyone at the pass mentions them. Met here means you have read it; there is no exercise and nothing is written down about you.

  8. Service

    9 at this level

    Service at Commis is the words the room runs on, the screen you never answer from, the cut-off you can run and the bottle you can open and pour without ceremony.

    Service at Commis runs in two pairs. The Room's Vocabulary and What We Screened and What We Did Not are about words: the ones the room runs on and the one question you never answer from the app. Responsible Service and the Law and The Bottle at the Table are about the drink in your hand: when to stop pouring, and the bottle you carry to the table.

    Take The Room's Vocabulary first, because it is the first module and the first door opens on it. Its four terms are the pass and expediting, the brigade de cuisine, line slang, and service styles with front-of-house fluency. Its outcome is concrete: you act on "86 the halibut" without asking, you say "behind" without thinking, and you can name who is working the pass. The guide keeps menu format and service school as two separate axes, so state both for your room. Know what it does not teach: "dupe" is glossed in four words, and there is no fire or all-day sequencing anywhere, so reading a ticket is your room's lesson and not the track's.

    What We Screened and What We Did Not is one term and one habit: you never answer a guest's question about what is in a dish from the app. The recipe screen is a text screen over the ingredient lines. It cannot see a shared fryer, a dusted board or a supplier's label, and a dish showing nothing means the screen found nothing, never that the dish is clear. The screen names what it checked and what it did not on every recipe, so the outcome is saying that in your own words to the guest who asks.

    Responsible Service and the Law is one term from Cocktail & Bar, and its outcome is a cut-off you can run in order: slow the pace, water, food, then a clear kind no with a manager behind you. You check anyone who could plausibly be under 30, you know dram-shop liability makes the room civilly liable for a guest it overserved, and you can offer a good zero-proof or a spritz instead of a fourth negroni.

    The Bottle at the Table has three terms: the sommelier's service ritual and floor fluency, wine faults, and decanting, temperature and glassware. The taste pour exists to check for faults and not preference. Corked smells of wet cardboard, oxidized of bruised apple, reduced of struck match, which may blow off if you wait. A corked bottle you replace graciously without arguing. Whites go out chilled, reds cool, just under room temperature. Present, open and pour without ceremony, and that is this module's outcome.

    The doors on the level page are The first module, which opens The Room's Vocabulary; The track, all 27 modules; and the Drill, which runs the service drill over this level's modules with the whole track as the field. A module is met through its terms: every term of the module recalled met or close in the drill.
